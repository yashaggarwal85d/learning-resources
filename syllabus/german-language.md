# German Language — Syllabus

The German-language domain of the learning experience: German for working professionals who want to live and work in a German-speaking country. The learner may start as a complete beginner in German, but is a fast, analytical adult. So the domain teaches the *system* (why the verb goes where it goes, how case endings carry meaning) instead of phrasebook drills, and treats every rule with the same precision as the technical domains.

**Goal:** C1, working fully in German (meetings, documents, negotiation), with a C2 track. Levels follow the Common European Framework of Reference (CEFR) and map to the recognised exams (Goethe-Zertifikat, telc, ÖSD, TestDaF, DSH).

## Structure: Milestones → Chapters → Topics

- **Milestones** are the CEFR levels, A1 to C2, taken in order.
- **Chapters** are areas within a level: sounds, sentence structure, grammar, communication, life and work.
- **Topics** are the learnable, testable units: each bullet is one topic, and each topic becomes one interactive reel. Topics marked *(refresher)* are short reference pages.

**Every milestone closes with a "Can-do check"**: what you should be able to do in German at that level, in our own words, and the matching exam.

## How this domain differs from the technical ones

- **Sound is essential.** Every example in every reel has native-speaker audio in standard German, with Austrian and Swiss variants flagged where they differ. Narration explains in the learner's language; the German itself is always spoken by native speakers.
- **Grammar is animated as a mechanism.** Case endings flow through a noun phrase to the first word that can carry them; the verb bracket closes around the middle field; a subordinating conjunction pushes the verb to the end. These are exactly the moving parts animation explains best.
- **Production, not just recognition.** Labs become *Practice* tasks: speak (recorded and compared against a model), write, and role-play. Every topic ends with something the learner produces.
- **Vocabulary in chunks.** Words are learned as collocations with gender and plural attached (die Entscheidung, -en; eine Entscheidung treffen), and every chunk goes into the spaced-repetition review queue.

## Canonical references

- *Common European Framework of Reference for Languages: Companion Volume* (Council of Europe, 2020): level descriptors
- *Duden – Die Grammatik* and *Duden – Die deutsche Rechtschreibung*: grammar and spelling reference

# Milestone 1 — A1: The Sound and Skeleton of German
The sound system and the one structural rule everything else hangs on: the finite verb's position.

## Chapter 1 — Sounds and Spelling
- **The German sound system** — vowel length (Miete vs Mitte), the umlauts ä/ö/ü, the two *ch* sounds (ich-Laut vs ach-Laut), the uvular and vocalised r (-er as a vowel), final devoicing (Tag sounds like "Tak"), the glottal stop before initial vowels.
- **Spelling-to-sound rules** — ei vs ie, eu/äu, sch, sp and st at the start of a word, z, v and w; ß after long vowels and diphthongs vs ss after short vowels; every noun capitalised.
- **Word stress and sentence melody** — stress on the stem syllable; separable prefixes stressed (ánrufen) vs inseparable ones unstressed (verstéhen); falling intonation for statements and W-questions, rising for yes/no questions.
- *Practice:* minimal-pair listening (Miete/Mitte, Hölle/Höhle, Küche/Kuchen), then record yourself and compare against the model.

## Chapter 2 — The Sentence Skeleton
- **Verb-second word order** — the finite verb sits in position 2 of a main clause; anything can take position 1 (subject, time, object), and the subject then follows the verb. The single most important rule in German.
- **Present tense and conjugation** — personal endings; stem-vowel changes in the 2nd and 3rd person singular (geben → gibt, lesen → liest, fahren → fährt); the irregular core: sein, haben, werden.
- **Questions** — W-questions (W-word in position 1, verb second), yes/no questions (verb first), and *doch* as "yes" to a negative question.
- **Negation: nicht vs kein** — *kein* negates nouns with an indefinite article or no article; *nicht* negates everything else; where *nicht* goes (before the element it negates, or late in the clause to negate the whole sentence).
- **Modal verbs and the verb bracket** — können, müssen, dürfen, sollen, wollen, möchten: the modal in position 2 and the infinitive at the end, the first appearance of the *Satzklammer* (verb bracket).
- **Separable and inseparable verbs** — the separable prefix goes to the end (Ich rufe dich an), the bracket again; prefixes that never separate (be-, emp-, ent-, er-, ge-, miss-, ver-, zer-).

## Chapter 3 — Nouns, Gender and Case (first pass)
- **Grammatical gender** — der, die, das; why it is largely arbitrary and which cues are reliable (-ung, -heit, -keit, -schaft, -ion → feminine; -chen, -lein → neuter); always learning a noun with its article and plural.
- **Plural formation** — the main patterns (-e, -er, -(e)n, -s, no ending; with or without umlaut) and how strongly each correlates with gender.
- **Nominative and accusative** — subject vs direct object; only the masculine article changes (der → den, ein → einen); why case lets German word order move more freely than English.
- **Personal and possessive pronouns** — ich/mich, du/dich, er/ihn …; mein, dein, sein, ihr, unser, euer, Ihr with the same endings as *kein*.
- **Formal and informal address** — du/ihr vs Sie, capitalised Sie and Ihr; workplace norms (Sie by default in most companies, du common in many tech teams) and how to offer or accept the switch.

## Chapter 4 — First Conversations
- **Introducing yourself** — name, origin, job, languages; spelling with the German alphabet (including ä, ö, ü, ß); numbers, phone numbers and e-mail addresses.
- **Time, dates and appointments** — clock times (halb drei is 2:30), days, months, ordinal dates (am 3. Mai); making, moving and cancelling an appointment.
- **Shopping, ordering and paying** — ich möchte / ich hätte gern, quantities and prices, polite requests.
- *Practice:* introduce yourself for 60 seconds; book and then reschedule an appointment in a phone role-play.

## Can-do check — A1
You can introduce yourself, ask and answer simple personal questions, handle numbers, times and prices, and get through basic transactions, as long as the other person speaks slowly. Exam: Goethe-Zertifikat A1 / telc Deutsch A1.

# Milestone 2 — A2: Everyday German
Talking about the past, the dative, and connecting ideas.

## Chapter 5 — Talking About the Past
- **The perfect tense** — haben or sein plus the past participle at the end of the clause; when to use sein (change of place or state, plus sein, bleiben, werden); why the perfect is spoken German's default past.
- **Forming past participles** — ge-…-t for weak verbs, ge-…-en with a stem change for strong verbs, no ge- for inseparable prefixes and -ieren verbs, ge- inside separable verbs (angerufen).
- **Simple past of sein, haben and the modals** — war, hatte, konnte, musste: used even in speech, where other verbs take the perfect.

## Chapter 6 — The Dative and Prepositions
- **The dative case** — the indirect object (Ich gebe dem Kollegen das Buch); dative articles (dem, der, dem, den + -n in the plural); dative pronouns (mir, dir, ihm, ihr …).
- **Dative verbs** — helfen, danken, gefallen, gehören, passen, antworten; the experiencer logic behind *Das gefällt mir*.
- **Prepositions by case** — accusative-only (durch, für, gegen, ohne, um), dative-only (aus, bei, mit, nach, seit, von, zu), and the contractions (zum, zur, im, am, beim).
- **Two-way prepositions** — an, auf, hinter, in, neben, über, unter, vor, zwischen: *wohin?* (movement to) takes the accusative, *wo?* (location) the dative; the verb pairs legen/liegen, stellen/stehen, setzen/sitzen, hängen.
- **Order of objects** — a dative noun before an accusative noun; an accusative pronoun before a dative one (Ich gebe es ihm).

## Chapter 7 — Adjectives and Comparison
- **Adjective endings** — one principle instead of three tables: the ending marks case and gender only where the article doesn't (strong, weak and mixed declension); animated as the signal travelling to the first word that can carry it.
- **Comparison** — comparative and superlative (schneller, am schnellsten), the irregulars (gut/besser/am besten, viel/mehr, gern/lieber), and *als* vs *wie*.

## Chapter 8 — Connecting Sentences
- **Coordinating conjunctions** — und, aber, oder, denn, sondern: they sit outside the clause (position 0) and change nothing.
- **Subordinate clauses and verb-final order** — weil, dass, wenn, ob, als send the finite verb to the end; a subordinate clause in position 1 makes the main clause start with its verb (Weil es regnet, bleibe ich zu Hause).
- **Reflexive verbs** — sich freuen, sich interessieren für, sich vorstellen; accusative vs dative reflexive (Ich wasche mich / Ich wasche mir die Hände).
- **The imperative** — du, ihr and Sie forms; softening with mal, doch and bitte.

## Chapter 9 — Everyday Life
- **Housing** — WG vs Wohnung, Kaltmiete, Warmmiete and Nebenkosten; reading a flat advert; registering your address (Anmeldung).
- **Health** — the family doctor (Hausarzt), appointments, describing symptoms (Ich habe Kopfschmerzen; Mir ist schlecht); reporting sick to an employer.
- **Work basics** — job titles and workplace vocabulary; e-mail openings and closings from formal (Sehr geehrte Frau …, Mit freundlichen Grüßen) to friendly (Hallo …, Viele Grüße).
- *Practice:* write an e-mail to a landlord about a repair; talk for two minutes, in the perfect tense, about what you did last weekend.

## Can-do check — A2
You can describe your background, your surroundings and recent events, handle routine tasks that need a simple, direct exchange, and write short practical messages. Exam: Goethe-Zertifikat A2 / telc Deutsch A2.

# Milestone 3 — B1: The Independent User
Narrating, explaining and giving reasons; the genitive, relative clauses and the subjunctive for politeness.

## Chapter 10 — Tense and Time
- **The simple past in writing** — the strong-verb vowel series (fahren–fuhr–gefahren, gehen–ging–gegangen); why written narrative uses the simple past while speech uses the perfect, and the north–south difference.
- **Pluperfect and *nachdem*** — sequencing events in the past; *nachdem* with the pluperfect.
- **Talking about the future** — the present tense plus a time expression as the normal future; *werden* + infinitive for intentions and for assumptions (Er wird krank sein: he's probably ill).
- **Temporal clauses** — *als* (one event in the past) vs *wenn* (repeated, present or future), während, bevor, seit(dem), bis.

## Chapter 11 — The Genitive and Complex Noun Phrases
- **The genitive case** — des/der and the -(e)s ending; genitive prepositions (wegen, trotz, während, statt); *von* + dative as the spoken alternative.
- **Weak masculine nouns (n-declension)** — der Kollege → den/dem/des Kollegen; der Student, der Mensch, der Name (des Namens).
- **Relative clauses** — relative pronouns look like the definite article (except dessen, deren, denen); their case comes from their role in the relative clause; verb-final order; *wo* and *was* as relatives.

## Chapter 12 — The Subjunctive for Politeness and Hypotheticals
- **Konjunktiv II in the present** — *würde* + infinitive and the one-word forms hätte, wäre, könnte; unreal conditions (Wenn ich Zeit hätte, würde ich …), wishes, polite requests (Könnten Sie …?), advice (Sie sollten …).
- **Konjunktiv II in the past** — hätte/wäre + past participle (Wenn ich das gewusst hätte …); with modals, the double infinitive (Ich hätte es machen müssen).

## Chapter 13 — Infinitives and Verb Complements
- ***zu* + infinitive** — after verbs and adjectives (Ich versuche, pünktlich zu sein; Es ist wichtig, …); *zu* inside separable verbs (anzurufen); where *zu* is not used (modals, lassen, sehen, hören).
- ***um … zu*, *ohne … zu*, *(an)statt … zu*** — purpose and manner; *damit* when the subjects differ.
- **Verbs with fixed prepositions** — warten auf + accusative, sich erinnern an + accusative, teilnehmen an + dative; the *da-* and *wo-* compounds (darauf, worauf; Worauf wartest du?).

## Chapter 14 — Communication at B1
- **Giving opinions and reasons** — meiner Meinung nach, ich finde, ich glaube; agreeing and disagreeing; connecting adverbs (deshalb, trotzdem, außerdem) that take position 1 and push the subject after the verb.
- **Phone calls and meetings** — leaving a message, asking someone to repeat or slow down, summarising and agreeing next steps.
- **Formal letters and complaints** — structure, subject line, reference to earlier contact, polite but firm wording.
- *Practice:* a three-minute opinion monologue using at least five connectors; a formal complaint e-mail in exam format.

## Can-do check — B1
You can deal with most situations at work and in daily life, describe experiences and plans, give reasons and opinions, and write connected text on familiar topics. Exam: Goethe-Zertifikat B1 / telc Deutsch B1. B1 is also the language level generally required for German naturalisation.

# Milestone 4 — B2: Professional German
The passive, the structure of complex sentences, reported speech, and working in German.

## Chapter 15 — Passive and Impersonal Structures
- **The process passive** — *werden* + past participle in every tense (ist … worden), the agent with *von* or *durch*; why German documentation and process descriptions prefer the passive.
- **The state passive** — *sein* + past participle (Das Fenster ist geöffnet) vs the process passive (Das Fenster wird geöffnet).
- **Passive with modals, and its alternatives** — muss geprüft werden; *man* sentences, *sich lassen* + infinitive, *sein* + *zu* + infinitive, and -bar adjectives (lösbar).

## Chapter 16 — The Architecture of Complex Sentences
- **The middle field** — ordering what sits between the two parts of the verb bracket: pronouns first, known before new information, the time–cause–manner–place tendency, and where *nicht* lands.
- **Two-part connectors** — sowohl … als auch, weder … noch, entweder … oder, nicht nur … sondern auch, zwar … aber, je … desto.
- **Concession, cause and consequence** — obwohl vs trotzdem, da/weil/denn, sodass vs so … dass, falls and sofern.
- **Participles as adjectives** — present participle (die steigende Zahl) and past participle (die geprüften Daten); extended participial attributes (die im letzten Quartal stark gestiegenen Kosten) and how to unpack them right to left.

## Chapter 17 — Reported Speech and Nuance
- **Konjunktiv I and indirect speech** — er sagt, er sei / habe / könne …; when Konjunktiv II replaces it (where Konjunktiv I looks like the indicative); its use in news and minutes.
- **Modal particles** — doch, ja, mal, eben, halt, schon, eigentlich, wohl: how they soften, insist or signal shared knowledge, and where they sit in the middle field.
- **Subjective modal verbs** — Er soll reich sein (reportedly), Sie muss krank sein (inference), Er will es gesehen haben (his claim).

## Chapter 18 — German at Work
- **Meetings and presentations** — opening, structuring (zunächst, anschließend, abschließend), taking questions, summarising.
- **Technical and data vocabulary** — die Daten (plural only), die Datenbank, die Abfrage, die Schnittstelle, die Anforderung, die Bereitstellung, der Datenschutz; English loanwords and the genders German gives them (das Meeting, der Bug).
- **Workplace culture and register** — when to switch from Sie to du, titles (Frau Dr. …), direct feedback, punctuality, the works council (Betriebsrat) (→ *Data Engineering* §10.5).
- **Applications and interviews** — the tabular CV (Lebenslauf), the cover letter (Anschreiben), the coded language of reference letters (Arbeitszeugnis: "stets zu unserer vollsten Zufriedenheit" means excellent), common interview questions, and salary vocabulary (Bruttojahresgehalt).
- *Practice:* present a technical design in German for five minutes; write a bug report and a weekly status e-mail.

## Can-do check — B2
You can work in German on technical and familiar professional topics, follow meetings and argue a point with reasons, and write clear, detailed texts, with only occasional strain for native speakers. Exam: Goethe-Zertifikat B2 / telc Deutsch B2.

# Milestone 5 — C1: Advanced
Formal and academic German, precision of style, real-world listening and full professional performance.

## Chapter 19 — Nominal Style and Formal German
- **Nominalisation** — verb to noun (entscheiden → die Entscheidung, das Entscheiden); light-verb constructions (eine Entscheidung treffen, zur Verfügung stellen, in Frage kommen).
- **Nominal vs verbal style** — turning clauses into noun phrases and back (weil die Kosten stiegen → aufgrund steigender Kosten); when nominal style serves precision and when it only obscures.
- **Prepositions of formal German** — aufgrund, infolge, hinsichtlich, bezüglich, mittels, anhand, gemäß, zufolge: their cases and positions.
- **Cohesion across a text** — pronominal adverbs, demonstratives (dieser, jener, derselbe), connectors between sentences, and how each sentence picks up what the last one introduced.

## Chapter 20 — Precision and Style
- **Word formation** — what prefixes do to meaning (ver-, zer-, er-, be-), derivational suffixes, compounds and their linking elements (Arbeit + s + Platz), reading long compounds.
- **Collocations and register** — colloquial vs standard vs official German; choosing between near-synonyms (bekommen, erhalten, kriegen).
- **Fine points of grammar and punctuation** — genitive vs *von*, *als ob* + Konjunktiv II, and the comma rules for subordinate clauses and infinitive groups.

## Chapter 21 — Listening in the Real World
- **Fast speech** — reductions in everyday spoken German (haben wir → hamma, ist es → isses) and how to hear through them.
- **Regional varieties** — Austrian Standard German (Jänner, Paradeiser), Swiss Standard German (no ß, Velo) and the Swiss German dialects; the main German dialect areas and regional accents.
- **Media German** — the register of news, interviews and podcasts; techniques for extensive listening.

## Chapter 22 — Writing and Speaking at C1
- **The argumentative essay (Erörterung)** — thesis, argument, example; linking and hedging (es lässt sich feststellen, dass …).
- **Summaries, reports and minutes** — writing a Protokoll; reported speech in minutes.
- **Negotiation and discussion** — interrupting politely, conceding, steering a discussion, disagreeing diplomatically.
- *Practice:* a 350-word Erörterung; a written summary of a ten-minute talk.

## Can-do check — C1
You can use German flexibly and effectively at work and in study, understand demanding and longer texts including implied meaning, and produce clear, well-structured, detailed text on complex subjects. Exam: Goethe-Zertifikat C1 / telc Deutsch C1 / TestDaF / DSH.

# Milestone 6 — C2: Mastery
Near-native command: idiom, implication, and specialist registers.

## Chapter 23 — Near-Native Command
- **Idioms and fixed expressions** — Redewendungen (auf dem Holzweg sein, ins Fettnäpfchen treten): what they mean, and when using them sounds natural vs forced.
- **Irony, humour and implication** — understatement, the subjunctive as a distancing device, reading between the lines of reference letters and e-mails.
- **Reading literature** — the sentence architecture of literary German, from Kafka to contemporary fiction; the narrative simple past.
- **Legal and contract German** — reading an employment contract or terms and conditions (AGB): Kündigungsfrist, Probezeit, "sofern nicht anders vereinbart".
- *Practice:* rewrite one text for three registers: colloquial, standard and official.

## Can-do check — C2
You can understand virtually everything you hear or read, summarise and reconstruct arguments from different sources, and express yourself spontaneously and precisely, distinguishing fine shades of meaning. Exam: Goethe-Zertifikat C2: Großes Deutsches Sprachdiplom.

# Appendix — Exams and Certificates

- **General certificates, A1–C2:** Goethe-Zertifikat, telc Deutsch, ÖSD (Austria).
- **University admission:** TestDaF and DSH, both at C1-equivalent level.
- **Work and residence:** requirements depend on the visa, the employer and the country, and they change; always check the current official sources (for Germany, the Federal Foreign Office and the immigration authorities) before relying on a level.
