## A. What "standard pronunciation" means

### 1. There is a de facto standard, and it has national variants
- **Statement:** German pronunciation has no official body behind it the way spelling does. The standard comes from pronunciation dictionaries and broadcasters, and newsreaders in Germany, Austria and Switzerland each speak their own standard form, so the reel's model audio is one standard and its flagged variants are others.
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_German_phonology
  > the pronunciation has no official standard and relies on a de facto standard documented in reference works such as Deutsches Aussprachewörterbuch
- **Source:** https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aussprache_der_deutschen_Sprache
  > Nachrichtensprecher aus Deutschland, Österreich und der Schweiz unterscheiden sich in ihrer Aussprache des Standarddeutschen.
- **Placement:** here

## B. Vowel length (Miete vs Mitte)

### 2. Length changes meaning in stressed syllables
- **Statement:** In a stressed syllable, a long vowel and a short vowel make different words (Bahn/Bann, Miete/Mitte). Length is something learners have to hear and produce, not an optional accent feature.
- **Source:** https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vokall%C3%A4nge
  > Eine Besonderheit des Deutschen liegt darin, dass der Wechsel der Vokalquantität die Wortbedeutung verändert oder auslöscht.
- **Placement:** here

### 3. Long vowels are also tense, short vowels also lax
- **Statement:** Long *i, ü, u, e, ö, o* are tense (closer) and short ones are lax. The *ie* in Miete is the tense [iː] (close to English *seed*), and the *i* in Mitte is the lax [ɪ] (close to English *sit*). Learners should hear a change in quality as well as in duration.
- **Source:** https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aussprache_der_deutschen_Sprache
  > Ritt /rɪt/ und riet /riːt/ unterscheiden sich beispielsweise auch in der Qualität voneinander
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German
  > ist, Tschernobyl sit
- **Placement:** here

### 4. The *a* pair differs only in length
- **Statement:** Long and short *a* (Bahn/Bann) are practically the same quality in the standard, so length is the only cue. This is the cleanest pair for training duration on its own.
- **Source:** https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vokall%C3%A4nge
  > Minimalpaare wie Bahn/Bann sich in puncto Vokalqualität nicht nennenswert unterscheiden.
- **Placement:** here

### 5. Short (lax) vowels are "checked", as in English
- **Statement:** Like the English vowels in *sit, bet, put*, German lax vowels have to be followed by a consonant. A stressed vowel at the end of a word (ja, so, du, Knie) is therefore long. English speakers already have this pattern.
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_German_phonology
  > Like the English checked vowels, the German lax vowels require a following consonant
- **Placement:** here

### 6. Long ä [ɛː] often merges with long e [eː]
- **Statement:** Many northern speakers say Mädchen with [eː], and some scholars doubt that /ɛː/ is a phoneme at all. Learners can use either pronunciation, and long ä vs long e should not be drilled as a key contrast.
- **Source:** https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vokall%C3%A4nge
  > In großen Teilen des norddeutschen Sprachraums entfällt der lange [ɛː]-Laut.
- **Placement:** here

### 7. Long vowels shorten when unstressed
- **Statement:** Long vowels lose most of their length in unstressed syllables (vital, Genom, Opa), so length is mostly a property of stressed syllables.
- **Source:** https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aussprache_der_deutschen_Sprache
  > Geschlossene (lange) Vokale werden in unbetonter Position meistens kurz ausgesprochen
- **Placement:** later: "Word stress and sentence melody"

### 8. Spelling shows vowel length (doubled consonants mark short vowels)
- **Statement:** The official rules mark a short stressed vowel by doubling the consonant after it (Mitte, Hütte, Hölle). Spellings like *ie* and *h* mark long vowels (Miete, Höhle).
- **Source:** https://www.rechtschreibrat.com/DOX/rfdr_Regeln_2016_redigiert_2018.pdf
  > Folgt im Wortstamm auf einen betonten kurzen Vokal nur ein einzelner Konsonant, so kennzeichnet man die Kürze des Vokals durch Verdopplung des Konsonantenbuchstabens.
- **Placement:** later: "Spelling-to-sound rules" (this topic can point ahead, because the Miete/Mitte spelling difference is visible)

## C. The umlauts ä, ö, ü

### 9. ü is a front vowel with rounded lips
- **Statement:** ü [yː]/[ʏ] keeps the tongue position of *i* and rounds the lips. The reliable teaching cue for English speakers is to say "ee" and then round the lips. ü is not a variant of *u*, and Kuchen and Küche are different words.
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German
  > like meet, but with the lips rounded
- **Placement:** here

### 10. ö has a long tense member and a short lax member (Höhle vs Hölle)
- **Statement:** ö is a front rounded vowel: long [øː] (Höhle, hören) and short [œ] (Hölle, Köln). English has no exact equivalent, and the nearest one (a non-rhotic *heard/hurt*) lacks the lip rounding.
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German
  > somewhat like hurt; RP ugh
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German
  > somewhat like heard
- **Placement:** here

### 11. Short ä sounds the same as short e
- **Statement:** Short ä and short e are the same sound [ɛ]. The spelling only reflects word families (Wand – Wände, but Wende), so learners need no separate "ä sound" for short vowels.
- **Source:** https://www.rechtschreibrat.com/DOX/rfdr_Regeln_2016_redigiert_2018.pdf
  > den Umlaut (zum Beispiel Wand – Wände, aber Wende)
- **Placement:** here

### 12. Umlaut is also a grammatical alternation
- **Statement:** Umlaut is also a vowel change inside word families (Gast – Gäste, Lamm – Lämmer). It marks plurals and appears in verb forms like *fährt*, and these patterns are taught with the grammar.
- **Source:** https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umlaut
  > Während er etwa in Gast – Gäste und Lamm – Lämmer lautgesetzlich ist
- **Placement:** later: "Plural formation" (also "Present tense and conjugation" for fahren → fährt)

### 13. Writing ae/oe/ue in place of ä/ö/ü, never a bare a/o/u
- **Statement:** When umlauts can't be typed (e-mail addresses, forms, passports), German writes ae/oe/ue. Dropping the dots changes the word.
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_orthography
  > simply using the base vowel (e.g., ⟨u⟩ instead of ⟨ü⟩) would be wrong and misleading
- **Placement:** later: "Introducing yourself" (spelling names and e-mail addresses)

## D. The two ch sounds

### 14. ich-Laut and ach-Laut are one sound that changes with the vowel before it
- **Statement:** *ch* is [x] after back vowels and *a* (Buch, Bach, doch) and [ç] after front vowels and after consonants (ich, mich, durch, manchmal). Which one to use depends only on the sound before it.
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_German_phonology
  > The allophone [x] occurs after back vowels and /a aː/ (for instance in Buch [buːx] 'book'), the allophone [ç] after front vowels (for instance in mich [mɪç] 'me/myself') and consonants
- **Placement:** here

### 15. The full ich-Laut context: front vowels, umlauts, ei/eu/äu, l/n/r
- **Statement:** The ich-Laut also follows the umlauts, the diphthongs *ei/ai/eu/äu* and the consonants *l, n, r* (Milch, manchmal, durch). *au* is a back diphthong and takes the ach-Laut (rauchen, auch). Umlaut therefore switches the ch sound: Buch [x] becomes Bücher [ç].
- **Source:** https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ich-Laut
  > Ein ch, das den Vokalen E und I, den Konsonanten L, N, R und Y, den Umlauten Ä, Ö und Ü und den Diphthongen EI, ÄU, AI und EU folgt
- **Placement:** here

### 16. -chen is always the ich-Laut
- **Statement:** The diminutive -chen is always [çən], even after a back vowel (Frauchen [ç] vs rauchen [x]). This is the one regular exception to the vowel-before rule, and A1 learners meet it at once (Mädchen, bisschen).
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_German_phonology
  > The diminutive suffix -chen is always pronounced with an ich-Laut [-çən].
- **Placement:** here

### 17. The ach-Laut is velar or uvular
- **Statement:** After *a* (and often *o, u*), the "back ch" is pronounced right at the uvula [χ] (Bach). After long *u, o* it is velar [x] (Buch). Learners can treat both as one rasping back fricative, and should not use [k] or [h].
- **Source:** https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aussprache_der_deutschen_Sprache
  > Dieser Laut (auch Ach-Laut) bildet zusammen mit [ç] und [x] ein Phonem. Er tritt nach /a/, /a:/, /ɔ/ und /ʊ/ auf.
- **Placement:** here

### 18. The ich-Laut is not *sch*
- **Statement:** Standard German keeps [ç] (ich) apart from [ʃ] (Tisch). Merging them is a regional western feature, and it is also the most common English-speaker substitution, so ich/isch and Kirche/Kirsche are worth a contrast drill.
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_German_phonology
  > In Western varieties, there is a strong tendency to realize /ç/ as unrounded [ʃ] or [ɕ]
- **Placement:** here

### 19. Word-final -ig is said with the ich-Laut in the northern standard
- **Statement:** The ending -ig is [ɪç] (wichtig, zwanzig, König). Southern and Austrian standard German keep [ɪk]. A1 learners meet it in numbers (zwanzig, dreißig). Using [ç] or [x] for other final g's (Krieg, lag) counts as sub-standard.
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_German_phonology
  > the fricative pronunciation of final ⟨g⟩ is prescribed by the Siebs standard, for instance wichtig [ˈvɪçtɪç]
- **Source:** https://www.rechtschreibrat.com/DOX/rfdr_Regeln_2016_redigiert_2018.pdf
  > ewig, Ewigkeit (wegen ewige), gläubig (wegen gläubige)
- **Placement:** here

### 20. Initial ch in loanwords varies by region (China, Chemie)
- **Statement:** At the start of a word, *ch* is [ç] in the north, [k] in the south and [ʃ] in the west (China). All three are heard from native speakers.
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_German_phonology
  > in the Northern varieties of standard German, it is [ç], while in Southern varieties, it is [k], and in Western varieties, it is [ʃ]
- **Placement:** later: "Spelling-to-sound rules"

## E. The r: uvular and vocalised

### 21. The consonant r is usually uvular
- **Statement:** Most German speakers pronounce consonantal r (rot, Brot, Ehre) as a uvular fricative or approximant [ʁ], made at the back where the ach-Laut is made but voiced and softer. A tongue-tip trill is also standard (Bavaria, Austria, Switzerland, singing). The English r is not.
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guttural_R
  > most varieties of German are now spoken with a uvular rhotic, usually a fricative or approximant [ʁ], rather than a trill [ʀ]. The alveolar pronunciation [r ~ ɾ] continues to be considered acceptable in all Standard German varieties
- **Placement:** here

### 22. After a vowel, r becomes a vowel glide (as in non-rhotic English)
- **Statement:** At the end of a syllable, r is vocalised to a short [ɐ̯] glide (wir, Uhr, mehr, Tür, Bär). This works much like the "dropped r" of British English, so learners should not pronounce a consonant there.
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German
  > In many regions except for most parts of Switzerland, the /r/ in the syllable coda is vocalized to [ɐ̯] after long vowels or after all vowels, and /ər/ is pronounced as [ɐ].
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guttural_R
  > This is most common in the syllable coda, as in non-rhotic English
- **Placement:** here

### 23. -er is an a-like vowel [ɐ], kept distinct from -e [ə]
- **Statement:** Unstressed -er (Lehrer, Mutter, aber) is an open [ɐ], close to *a*. In the northern standard, Oper and Opa sound alike. It has to stay distinct from the schwa of -e (bitte vs bitter, Lehre vs Lehrer).
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German
  > Standard German has mid [ə] and open [ɐ] schwas. Care must be taken to clearly distinguish between the two.
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_German_phonology
  > Word pairs such as Oper 'opera' (traditionally transcribed [ˈoːpɐ]) and Opa 'grandpa' (traditionally transcribed [ˈoːpa]) are thus homophones
- **Placement:** here

### 24. Schwa occurs only unstressed and often disappears before n/l
- **Statement:** Unstressed *e* is schwa [ə] (bitte, viele). In -en and -el it often drops out so that the n or l becomes syllabic (haben, Kissen [ˈkɪsn̩]). Learners hear this in nearly every infinitive.
- **Source:** https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwa
  > Im Deutschen erscheint er nur in unbetonten Silben (z. B. bereit, viele) und wird mit dem Buchstaben <e> schriftlich dargestellt.
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_German_phonology
  > If a sonorant follows in the syllable coda, the schwa often disappears so that the sonorant becomes syllabic, for instance Kissen [ˈkɪsn̩]
- **Placement:** here

## F. Final devoicing (Tag sounds like "Tak")

### 25. Obstruents are voiceless at the end of a syllable and voiced again before a vowel
- **Statement:** At the end of a syllable, b/d/g/v/z (and borrowed ʒ/dʒ) are pronounced voiceless, so Tag sounds like [taːk] and Rad sounds like Rat. When an ending moves the consonant to the start of the next syllable, its voice comes back (Tage, Räder).
- **Source:** https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auslautverh%C3%A4rtung
  > Plosive, Affrikaten und Frikative sind in der Silbenkoda stimmlos.
- **Source:** https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auslautverh%C3%A4rtung
  > Im Silbenanlaut bleibt hingegen die Stimmhaftigkeit der Konsonanten erhalten, etwa in Räder.
- **Source:** https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auslautverh%C3%A4rtung
  > Sie betrifft folgende Konsonantenphoneme: die Plosive /b d g/, die Frikative /v z ʒ/ sowie die Affrikate
- **Placement:** here

### 26. It applies to syllable ends, not just word ends
- **Statement:** Devoicing also happens inside words, before a consonant or at a syllable or compound boundary (Handlung [t], Eigner [k], abfahren [p]). "Only at the end of the word" is a common half-truth.
- **Source:** https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auslautverh%C3%A4rtung
  > Die Auslautverhärtung im Deutschen betrifft Konsonanten in der Silbenkoda und nicht nur am Wortende, wie oft behauptet.
- **Placement:** here

### 27. The spelling ignores devoicing (stem spelling)
- **Statement:** The official rules keep a stem's spelling constant whatever the pronunciation, so Tag, Rad and Hund are written with g/d. The written letter shows the voiced form you hear in Tage/Räder/Hunde.
- **Source:** https://www.rechtschreibrat.com/DOX/rfdr_Regeln_2016_redigiert_2018.pdf
  > Rad und Rat werden gleich ausgesprochen, aber unterschiedlich geschrieben wegen des Rades und des Rates
- **Placement:** here (the spelling strategy of extending to a related form to choose the letter belongs in "Spelling-to-sound rules")

### 28. English has no final devoicing, so English speakers have to learn it
- **Statement:** English keeps final voicing (*bad* vs *bat*), so English speakers carry voiced finals into German (a voiced "Tag", "Hund"). German speakers make the mirror-image error in English.
- **Source:** https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auslautverh%C3%A4rtung
  > beispielsweise im nördlichen Deutschen, im Niederländischen und im Türkischen, nicht aber im südlichen Deutschen oder im Englischen
- **Placement:** here

### 29. Devoicing is a northern feature, and the south sounds different
- **Statement:** Standard final devoicing developed in northern Germany. Southern German, Austrian and Swiss standard speech don't have it in the same form, because their b/d/g are weakly voiced or voiceless lenis sounds everywhere. Variant audio will reflect this.
- **Source:** https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auslautverh%C3%A4rtung
  > im südlichen Deutschland sowie in Teilen Mitteldeutschlands tritt sie ebenso wenig auf wie im österreichischen Deutsch oder im Schweizer Hochdeutsch
- **Placement:** here (as a variant flag)

### 30. Fortis/lenis is the technical analysis behind "voiced/voiceless"
- **Statement:** Phoneticists describe p/b, t/d, k/g as fortis/lenis pairs rather than voiceless/voiced pairs, because aspiration and articulatory force carry much of the contrast.
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_German_phonology
  > These pairs are often called fortis–lenis pairs, since describing them as voiced–voiceless pairs is inadequate.
- **Placement:** out of scope: theoretical detail. For A1 learners "voiced/voiceless" plus English-like aspiration of p/t/k is enough.

## G. The glottal stop before initial vowels

### 31. Each vowel-initial word starts with a glottal catch
- **Statement:** In standard German (especially northern), a word that starts with a vowel begins with a glottal stop [ʔ] (Acht [ˈʔaxt], der Alte), the catch in English "uh-oh". German has no letter for it.
- **Source:** https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knacklaut
  > vor vokalischem Anlaut, beispielsweise Acht [ˈʔaxt], der Alte
- **Placement:** here

### 32. It also appears inside words: before vowel-initial stems, and in hiatus before a stressed vowel
- **Statement:** The glottal stop marks a vowel-initial stem after a prefix or inside a compound (beachten [bəˈʔaxtən], Spiegelei, vereinen). It also appears between two vowels when the second is stressed (Theater, Oase [ʔo.ʔaː.zə]).
- **Source:** https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knacklaut
  > vor vokalisch anlautenden Wortstämmen in zusammengesetzten Wörtern, beispielsweise beachten
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_German_phonology
  > as in Oase [ʔo.ʔaː.zə] (twice)
- **Placement:** here

### 33. No linking across words, so "mein Auto" is not "mei-Nauto"
- **Statement:** English and French link a final consonant onto a following vowel (*an apple* → "a-napple"). German blocks this with the glottal stop. Learners find it hard to copy, so the reel should model and drill it.
- **Source:** https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knacklaut
  > Wenn er nicht ausgesprochen wird, dann werden die Wörter wie im Französischen oder Englischen direkt miteinander verbunden.
- **Source:** https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knacklaut
  > Umgekehrt fällt es Menschen, die Deutsch als Fremdsprache lernen, regelmäßig schwer, die Verwendung des Knacklautes nachzuahmen.
- **Placement:** here

### 34. It is not a phoneme, and it is weaker or absent in the south, in Austria, in Switzerland and in casual speech
- **Statement:** Leaving out [ʔ] never changes a word's meaning. Austrian, southern and Swiss standard speech and most colloquial speech use it much less. Learners should be taught to produce it, but it should be presented as a feature of clear standard speech, not as a contrast between words.
- **Source:** https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Appendix:German_pronunciation
  > The presence or absence of [ʔ] is not phonemic. In most standard varieties of German, all initial vowels are preceded by [ʔ]. However, this does not generally hold true for Austrian, southern, and Swiss standard German and most colloquial varieties.
- **Placement:** here

### 35. The glottal stop as a "gender pause"
- **Statement:** Since the 1980s the glottal stop has also been used to read gender-inclusive forms aloud (Lehrer*innen as Lehrer-[ʔ]innen).
- **Source:** https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knacklaut
  > wird der Glottisschlag im deutschsprachigen Raum als „Gender-Pause“ beim gendergerechten Sprechen (Gendern) eingesetzt
- **Placement:** out of scope: a sociolinguistic and stylistic usage, not part of the A1 sound system. It could appear as a listening note much later.
