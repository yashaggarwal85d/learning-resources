# The German sound system: what an expert must know

Syllabus line: vowel length (Miete vs Mitte), umlauts ä/ö/ü, ich-Laut vs ach-Laut, uvular and vocalised r (-er as a vowel), final devoicing (Tag = "Tak"), glottal stop before initial vowels.
Audience: adult professionals who speak English, may start from zero, and learn fast and analytically.

Key sources:
- [K] Kohler, K. J. (1999), "German", *Handbook of the International Phonetic Association*, CUP, pp. 86-89 (first published in JIPA 20(1), 1990)
- [W] Wikipedia, "Standard German phonology"
- [D] Duden Band 6, *Das Aussprachewörterbuch*
- [KR] Krech, Stock, Hirschfeld & Anders (2009), *Deutsches Aussprachewörterbuch*, de Gruyter
- [WI] Wiese, R. (1996), *The Phonology of German*, OUP
- [M] Moulton, W. G. (1962), *The Sounds of English and German*, Univ. of Chicago Press

| 5 | Unstressed -e, be- and ge- are schwa [ə] (bitte, Miete). In casual standard speech -en becomes a syllabic nasal: haben [ˈhaːbm̩]. | KR; W | here |
| 6 | Spelling cues for length: a double consonant or ck marks a short vowel (Mitte), while h, ie or a doubled vowel marks a long one (Sohn, Miete, Boot). | D; W ("German orthography") | Spelling-to-sound rules |
| 7 | ü and ö are front rounded vowels: say [i]/[e] and round your lips as for [u]/[o]. This gives [yː ʏ øː œ]. | K; M | here |
| 8 | The umlaut contrast is lexical and grammatical: schon/schön, Mutter/Mütter, Bruder/Brüder, zahlen/zählen. If you blur it, you lose meaning. | W; WI | here (the sound). Plural and verb umlaut belong to grammar, not to this course's phonetics topics |
| 9 | The fallback spellings ae/oe/ue (Muenchen) are used where umlauts can't be typed. They are not diphthongs. | W ("German orthography") | Spelling-to-sound rules |
| 10 | ch is the ach-Laut [x] after a, o, u and au (Bach, Buch, auch), and the ich-Laut [ç] everywhere else: after front vowels, after l/n/r (Milch, manch, durch) and in -chen. | K; W | here |
| 11 | The ich-Laut is not [ʃ]. Approach it from a whispered [j] or from the initial sound of English "hue" [çuː]. | W ("Voiceless palatal fricative"); M | here |
| 12 | Diminutive -chen is always [ç], even after a back vowel, because the morpheme boundary blocks [x]: Kuhchen [ç] vs Kuchen [x]. | WI; W | here |
| 13 | Standard final -ig is [ɪç] (König, wichtig), while -ige is [ɪɡə]. Southern and Austrian [ɪk] is also widely heard. | D; KR | here |
| 14 | chs is pronounced [ks] (sechs, Fuchs, wachsen), and word-initial ch varies: Chemie [ç]/[k], Chef [ʃ], Christ [k]. | D; W ("German orthography") | Spelling-to-sound rules |
| 15 | Consonantal r is usually uvular: a fricative or approximant [ʁ], or a trill [ʀ]. The alveolar trill [r] is also standard, common in the south, Austria and Switzerland. English [ɹ] is never correct. | K; W ("Guttural R") | here |
| 16 | After a vowel in the coda, r vocalises to [ɐ̯]: Tier [tiːɐ̯], Uhr [uːɐ̯], mehr, Ohr. The r becomes part of a diphthong. | K; WI | here |
| 17 | Unstressed -er is the vowel [ɐ], so the pairs bitte/bitter, Lehre/Lehrer and diese/dieser are distinguished only by [ə] vs [ɐ]. | KR; W | here |
| 18 | The prefixes er-, ver-, zer- and her- carry vocalic r: verstehen [fɛɐ̯ˈʃteːən]. Before a vowel, r stays consonantal: Lehrerin [ˈleːʁəʁɪn]. | KR; WI | here |
| 19 | Final devoicing: in the syllable coda, voiced obstruents become voiceless (b>p, d>t, g>k, v>f, z>s). This gives Tag [taːk] and Hund [hʊnt], and Rad and Rat sound the same [ʁaːt]. | W ("Final-obstruent devoicing"); WI | here |
| 20 | Devoicing is triggered by the syllable, not only by the word end. Its effects alternate: Tag [k]/Tage [ɡ], Kind [t]/Kinder [d], lieblich [p], Handlung [t]. Spelling keeps the underlying letter. | WI; W | here |
| 21 | For p/t/k vs b/d/g, the main cue is fortis/lenis and aspiration (as in English), not voicing. Southern speakers often use voiceless lenis b/d/g. | K | here |
| 22 | A glottal stop [ʔ] precedes vowel-initial words and stems: ein Apfel [ʔaɪ̯n ˈʔapfl̩], beachten [bəˈʔaxtn̩]. German does not link across words like English "an_apple". | K; W | here |
| 23 | The glottal stop separates words that would otherwise sound alike: vereisen [fɛɐ̯ˈʔaɪ̯zn̩] vs verreisen [fɛɐ̯ˈʁaɪ̯zn̩]. It is also used inside words before a stressed vowel (Theater [teˈʔaːtɐ]). | W; KR | here |
| 24 | The glottal stop is not a phoneme and is often realised as creaky voice. It is weaker or absent in fast, unstressed or southern speech, so learners should produce it but not over-stress it. | K; KR | here |
| 25 | Full vowel length and the glottal-stop onset are strongest in stressed syllables, so learners need to know where stress falls (stem stress; ˈumfahren vs umˈfahren). | WI; D | Word stress and sentence melody |
| 26 | The diphthongs ei [aɪ̯], au [aʊ̯] and eu/äu [ɔʏ̯], and ie as a long [iː], are a reading problem rather than a new-sound problem. | W ("German orthography") | Spelling-to-sound rules |
| 27 | Regional variants include coronalised ich-Laut ([ɕ]/[ʃ] in the Rhineland and Saxony), Swiss Standard German without final devoicing or vocalised r, and northern [ɛː]=[eː]. | W; KR | out of scope: learners only need to recognise these, not produce them, since the production target is Standard German |
