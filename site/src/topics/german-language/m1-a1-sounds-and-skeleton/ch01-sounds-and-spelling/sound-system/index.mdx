## After this topic you can

- Tell long, tense vowels from short, lax ones (Miete [iː] vs Mitte [ɪ]), and predict length from the spelling cues ie, h, a doubled vowel and a doubled consonant, while knowing where those cues fail.
- Build ü and ö from parts you already have: the tongue of i or e plus the rounded lips of u or o. Say why ä is pronounced like e.
- Choose the ich-Laut [ç] or the ach-Laut [x] from the sound before the ch, and apply the one override: the suffix -chen is always [ç] (Frauchen vs rauchen).
- Say r as a consonant at the back of the mouth before a vowel, and as a vowel after one ([ɐ̯], and [ɐ] for -er). Keep -er [ɐ] apart from -e [ə].
- Apply final devoicing at the end of every syllable (Rad = Rat [ʁaːt], lieb|lich), and the northern -ig → [ɪç].
- Put a glottal catch before vowel-initial words and stems (mein | Auto), and know where it does not go (before suffixes, and in Austria and Switzerland).
- Read both lines of the opening flat-viewing exchange aloud, sound by sound.

**How to read the notation.** Square brackets show sounds, not spelling; ː means long; ˈ marks the stressed syllable. The reel draws the vocalised r as [ɐ] and the syllabic n as [ən] because the combining marks under ɐ̯ and n̩ are missing from the site's fonts; this page keeps the exact marks. The reel writes the uvular r as [ʁ] and the ach-Laut as [x] (the convention of Wikipedia's Help:IPA and the German Wiktionary); DWDS, the digital dictionary of German, writes [ʀ] and [χ] (Rad [ʀaːt], auch [aʊ̯χ]). Neither is wrong.

## The system in one table

**Phoneme and allophone, in one paragraph.** A phoneme is a sound that can change a word: the long [iː] and short [ɪ] of Miete and Mitte are different phonemes. An allophone is a variant of one sound that never changes a word, and is chosen by its surroundings: the ich-Laut [ç] and ach-Laut [x] are two allophones of one ch, chosen by the vowel before it. Most of this topic is allophones, which is why a rule about context explains them better than a list of sounds.

**The vowels.** Standard German has about 15 monophthong phonemes, written with only 8 vowel letters (a, e, i, o, u, ä, ö, ü); the pronunciation article on German Wikipedia (the ADS table below) lists them, and the descriptions here are translated from it. Length and quality go together: the short vowel is usually also lower and more central, except a, which differs (almost) only in length.

| Phoneme | Example | Description (translated) |
|---|---|---|
| /a/ | Kamm | short, open, front-to-back, unrounded |
| /aː/ | kam | long, open, front-to-back, unrounded |
| /ɛ/ | Stelle | short, open-mid, front, unrounded |
| /ɛː/ | Käse | long, open-mid, front, unrounded |
| /eː/ | stehlen | (long,) close-mid, front, unrounded |
| /ɪ/ | Mitte | short, near-close, near-front, unrounded |
| /iː/ | Mieter | (long,) close, front, unrounded |
| /ɔ/ | offen | short, open-mid, back, rounded |
| /oː/ | Ofen | (long,) close-mid, back, rounded |
| /œ/ | Hölle | short, open-mid, front, rounded |
| /øː/ | Höhle | (long,) close-mid, front, rounded |
| /ʊ/ | Mutter | short, rounded, centralised, near-close back vowel |
| /uː/ | Mut | (long,) close, back, rounded |
| /ʏ/ | Müller | short, near-close, near-front, rounded |
| /yː/ | Mühle | (long,) close, front, rounded |
| /ə/ | bitte | schwa, unstressed only |

**The consonants this topic touches.** The two ch allophones [ç] and [x]; the r in its consonant and vowel forms; the stops and fricatives that lose their voice at the end of a syllable (b, d, g, v, z and their class); and the glottal stop [ʔ].

## Into the system

### Vowels: length and tension

Vowel length is phonemic in German (Miete vs Mitte) but is not consistently shown in the spelling. There are three standard analyses. One treats quantity as the distinguishing feature and lets quality follow from it. Another treats quality (tense vs lax) as the feature. A third, the syllable-cut analysis, ties length to how the vowel meets the next consonant. The reel uses the practical description that works for all three: long vowels are tense and fairly close, short vowels are lax and a little more open or central (Ritt [rɪt] and riet [riːt] differ in quality as well as length). The short vowels /ɪ ʏ ʊ ɛ œ ɔ/ are *checked*, as in English: they need a consonant after them in the same syllable, so a stressed syllable that ends in a vowel has a long vowel.

Unstressed long vowels are often shortened (vital, Genom); the topic on word stress returns to this.

**Spelling cues, in full.** Long: ie (Liebe, hier); a silent h after the vowel (Zahn; ah, eh, oh, uh); a doubled a, e or o (Saat, See, Moor); a vowel in an open syllable, i.e. one followed by a single consonant and another vowel (geben, sagen). Short: a doubled consonant letter (Sonne, irren), and ck and tz (Ratte, Masse and their relatives). A doubled consonant is pronounced as a single one, except across a compound boundary. **Where the cues fail:**

- Mond is long, although two consonants follow;
- nothing marks length before ch: Drache has a short a, Sprache a long one;
- the short function words an, das, es, in, mit and von have short vowels without any marking;
- the h in enthalten (the opening word of the viewing question) is a normal [h] at the start of the stem -halten, not a length h.

**Is /ɛː/ a phoneme?** The long ä of Käse is [ɛː] in careful speech, but in the north of Germany and the east of Austria it is usually said as [eː], and many authors treat /ɛː/ as peripheral. The reel teaches ä as "the sound of e", which is exactly right for short ä and for the northern long ä.

### The umlaut vowels

ä, ö and ü mark *umlaut*: the fronting of a back vowel (a → ä, o → ö, u → ü). ü [yː] is close and front like i, with the lips compressed as for u; the rounded front vowels are often a little centralised, and German /yː/ is described as close near-front. That is why "i's tongue plus u's lips" is a recipe, not a metaphor. Wikipedia's Help:IPA says the sound is "somewhat like few", which is a rough English cue, not a match.

ö has the same pair structure: long [øː] (Höhle) and short [œ] (Hölle). Short ä is [ɛ], the same sound as short e (Männer, Bett).

**When you can't type them.** Without umlaut letters, write ae, oe and ue. Writing the bare a, o or u would be wrong and misleading. The practice is covered again in the topic on introducing yourself.

**Umlaut as grammar.** The fronting is also a grammatical alternation: Gast → Gäste, Buch → Bücher. Plural formation returns to it; the sound system's contribution is that the vowel's move also moves a following ch (Buch [x] → Bücher [ç]).

### Schwa and the -er vowel

The schwa [ə] occurs only in unstressed syllables (bitte, besetzen). Before a sonorant in the coda it often disappears, leaving a syllabic consonant: Kissen [ˈkɪsn̩], enthalten [ɛntˈhaltn̩]. The reel draws these as [ˈkɪsən] and [ɛntˈhaltən] for font reasons.

The -er ending is a different vowel: [ɐ], near-open central and unrounded (Lehrer, besser, Wasser). It is a vowel allophone of /ər/ in all areas but Switzerland, where it is "widespread, but less common". In some regions -er is a syllabic r, [ʀ̩] or [r̩].

**Oper = Opa?** The Duden pronunciation dictionary (7th edition) and DWDS write the two endings differently, [ˈoːpɐ] and [ˈoːpa], separating [ɐ] from [a] by height. A 2020 study of six northern speakers reading minimal pairs such as Oper and Opa (Rathcke and Mooshammer, *Journal of the International Phonetic Association*) found no reliable difference in production, and listeners could not use one. Their conclusion is limited: six speakers, read speech, "unlikely to generalise to other dialects". Wiese also notes a tendency to neutralise [a(ː)], [aɐ̯] and [ɐ]. That is why the reel's question asks which sound the ending is *closest* to: the answer, the a of Opa, holds under both views.

### ich-Laut and ach-Laut: one phoneme

In German, "Laut" means "sound". The ich-Laut is the voiceless palatal fricative [ç]; the ach-Laut is the voiceless velar fricative [x]. A palatal consonant is made with the middle or back of the tongue raised to the hard palate; a velar one with the back of the tongue at the soft palate; voiceless means no vibration of the vocal folds.

**Complementary distribution.** [x] follows back vowels and /a aː/ (Buch, ach); [ç] follows front vowels (mich), the umlauts, the diphthongs ei, ai, eu and äu (leicht, euch), and consonants (Furcht, manchmal, Molch). The vowel *sound* decides, not the letter: euch ends in a u letter but [ɔɪ̯ç]. The spelling-to-sound table in the orthography article agrees: ch after a, o, u → [x]; after other vowels or consonants → [ç]; the suffix -chen → [ç].

**-chen and Frauchen.** The diminutive suffix -chen is always [çən]. Frauchen (a diminutive of Frau) has [ç] although au normally takes [x] (rauchen [ˈʁaʊ̯xn̩]). After a consonant, as in Hündchen, [ç] would happen anyway, so Frauchen and Mädchen-type words, not Hündchen, show the override.

**Why [ç] is the underlying form.** The better analysis treats the ach-Laut as backing of /ç/ after back vowels: [ç] appears in word onsets (Chemie [çeˈmiː]) and after consonants (Molch [mɔlç]), where no vowel has pulled it back.

**Kohler's finer distinction.** Kohler separates [x] after /uː oː/ (Buch, hoch) from the uvular [χ] after /a aː/ (Bach, doch). DWDS follows this: its auch is [aʊ̯χ]. The reel uses [x] throughout (Help:IPA's broad convention).

**Variation.** Western varieties tend to merge /ç/ with /ʃ/, with a hypercorrection Fisch → [fɪç]. In Austro-Bavarian, especially in Austria, [ç] may always be replaced by [x]; southeastern Furcht can be [fʊɐ̯xt]. Word-initial ch in loanwords (China, Chemie) varies by region; the topic on spelling-to-sound rules returns to it.

### The r and its variants

The most common r is the **uvular fricative or approximant [ʁ]**, which predominates among professional announcers; the dorsal r is used in almost all areas apart from Bavaria and parts of Switzerland. The **uvular trill [ʀ]** occurs in conservative varieties, and rot may be [roːt], [ʀoːt] or [ʁoːt]: free variants. The **apical (tongue-tip) trill [r]** is common in the south, in Bavaria and many parts of Switzerland and Austria. Kohler adds that the place of the r varies from uvular (rot) to velar (treten).

**R-vocalisation.** After a vowel, r vocalises: sehr [zeːɐ̯], der [deːɐ̯], wir [viːɐ̯], für [fyːɐ̯]; -er becomes [ɐ] (besser, Oper, Bär and others in the table in the phonology article). The rule is regular after long vowels and variable after short ones. A rule "after a vowel" would be wrong for the first r in Lehrer, which follows e but comes before a vowel: the reel's rule is "at the end of a syllable, after a vowel".

**Austria.** Northern Lehrer is [ˈleːʁɐ]; Austrian is [ˈlɛɐ̯ʁɐ], and Austrian rar is [ʁɑː]. The uvular fricative is the most common r in the Standard Austrian accent, with variable voicing ([ʁ] or [χ]).

### Final devoicing, fortis and lenis

**The rule.** In the syllable coda, plosives, affricates and fricatives are voiceless. This applies inside words as well as at their ends: lieblich [ˈliːplɪç] but lieben [ˈliːbn̩], endlich [ˈɛntlɪç] but Ende [ˈɛndə], Radweg [ˈʁaːtˌveːk]. It affects /b d g/, /v z ʒ/ and the affricate; loanwords follow it too (Klub, Trend, Standard).

**Disputed cases.** The German Wikipedia article on devoicing gives handeln – Handlung and eigen – Eigner as examples with a devoiced coda (Han[t]lung, Ei[k]ner). The German Wiktionary transcribes Handlung [ˈhandlʊŋ], Eigner [ˈaɪ̯ɡnɐ] and Adler [ˈaːdlɐ] with the voiced stop. Where the syllable boundary falls before l, n and r is analysed differently, and so is whether devoicing applies. The reel uses lieblich and endlich, which are not disputed.

**Fortis and lenis.** "Voiced" and "voiceless" are a convenient description; a better one is fortis and lenis (strong and weak), because the contrast is mainly one of force and aspiration. Aspiration is strongest in the onset of a stressed syllable (Taler) and weakest in the coda.

**-ig.** The fricative ending [ɪç] is prescribed by Siebs (wichtig [ˈvɪçtɪç]) for northern Germany only; elsewhere it is [ɪk], and in the Austro-Bavarian and Alemannic standard varieties [ɪɡ̊], a soft g. Other word-final g becoming [ç] or [x] (Krieg, lag) is frequent in central and northern Germany but colloquial. Before -lich the g stays a stop: königlich [ˈkøːnɪklɪç]. The -ig change is a twist, not an exception to devoicing: [ç] is voiceless too.

**Spelling and history.** German spells the stem (*Stammprinzip*), so Tag, not Tak. Middle High German spelled the alternation: tac but tages.

### The glottal stop as a boundary marker

The glottal stop [ʔ] is made by closing the glottis (the vocal folds) and releasing them, as in English "uh-oh". German writes no letter for it. The Duden grammar notates it [|].

**Where it goes.** In careful speech, in standard German from Germany: before a vowel-initial word (Acht [ˈʔaxt], der Alte); before a vowel-initial stem in a compound or after a prefix (beachten [bəˈʔaxtʰən], Spiegelei); and in hiatus before a more stressed vowel (Theater, Aorta, Oase [ʔoˈʔaːzə], twice in one word). Frequent words may lose it (erinnern, Verein), but vereinen always has it.

**Where it does not.** Before suffixes that start with a vowel (Ach-tung, Fah-rer, kin-disch), and not when the second vowel is less stressed (Thea vs Theater). It is absent in Austrian and Swiss standard German and in some dialects, and much rarer in the south of Germany; casual speech often drops it, especially when unstressed.

**Not a phoneme.** In most analyses the catch is a boundary marker before a foot-initial vowel, not a phoneme: its presence or absence alone does not change a word. This is why the "minimal pairs" often quoted (das Spiegel-Ei vs die Spiegelei, die Ode vs Diode, Sandaale vs Sandale) are not clean: Spiegel-Ei and Spiegelei differ in stress too; Diode [diˈʔoːdə] has its own catch by the hiatus rule, so it differs from die Ode [diː ˈʔoːdə] in the length of the i; Sandale [zanˈdaːlə] differs from Sandaale in stress and in [t] vs [d]. Beamter is [bəˈʔamtɐ].

**Without it.** Without the catch, mein Auto sounds like "mei Nauto", just as English "an apple" without a catch sounds like "a napple". German speakers carry the catch into foreign languages, and learners of German find it hard to imitate.

## Practice: minimal pairs, out loud

What this site offers, and nothing more.

- **Listen.** With sound turned on (it is off by default), the narration says the pairs in steps 2, 3, 5, 6, 9, 10 and 12: Miete/Mitte, Höhle/Hölle, Ofen/offen, Küche/Kuchen, die Oper/der Opa, das Rad/der Rat and mein Auto. Replay a step with the R key or the replay control, say the pair in the hold, and check yourself against the transcription.
- **The syllabus pairs, with IPA.**

| Pair | First | Second |
|---|---|---|
| Miete / Mitte | [ˈmiːtə] rent | [ˈmɪtə] middle |
| Hölle / Höhle | [ˈhœlə] hell | [ˈhøːlə] cave |
| Küche / Kuchen | [ˈkʏçə] kitchen | [ˈkuːxn̩] cake |
| Müller / Mühle | short ʏ | long yː |
| Kamm / kam | [kam] comb | [kaːm] came |

Küche and Kuchen are a *near*-minimal pair: they differ in vowel length and quality, in ch and in the ending, and Küche is not the umlaut of Kuchen. The German Wiktionary also notes that northern standard speech often has a long [ˈkyːçə].

- **A self-check list.** Is your Mitte shorter *and* lower than your Miete? Do your lips stay round through ü? Does your tongue stay back after a, o and u, and move forward after i and ü? Does the d in Rad lose its voice? Do you stop your voice before the A of Auto?
- **Be careful about the voice.** The German you hear on this site is a **synthetic** voice, not a native speaker. Where the voice and the transcription seem to disagree, trust the transcription. Recording yourself and comparing against a model is not available on this site; your phone's voice recorder works for now.

## Prove it

The two questions in the reel, plus four more.

- **(a) Rauchen vs Frauchen: why do they differ?** In rauchen the ch belongs to the stem rauch- and follows au, so it is [x]. In Frauchen the ch starts the diminutive suffix -chen, which is always [çən]. The cut in the word decides, not the letters.
- **(b) Is lieblich said with [b] or [p], and why does "only at the end of a word" fail?** [p]: lieb ends the first syllable, lieb|lich, so its coda devoices: [ˈliːplɪç]. The rule is about the end of a syllable, not of a word; lieben keeps [b] because the b starts the second syllable.
- **(c) Thea vs Theater: where does the catch go?** Theater has one inside the word, before the stressed a after the e: [teˈʔaːtɐ]. Thea has only the word-initial one: the second vowel is unstressed, so it takes none, [ˈteːa].
- **(d) Debug this transcription: [ˈʁaːd] for Rad in northern standard.** Final devoicing: the d ends the syllable, so Rad is [ʁaːt], the same as Rat.

## In real life

### Germany, Austria, Switzerland

German is *pluricentric*: there is no official body for pronunciation, and the standard is a de facto one documented in dictionaries (the Duden pronunciation dictionary, now in its 7th edition of 2015 by Kleiner, Knöbl and the Dudenredaktion, and Krech et al.'s dictionary). Newsreaders in Germany, Austria and Switzerland each speak a standard of their own. Some of what differs:

| Feature | Germany (north) | Austria | Switzerland |
|---|---|---|---|
| Final devoicing (Rad = Rat) | yes | no | no |
| Glottal stop (mein \| Auto) | yes | no | no |
| -ig | [ɪç] | [ɪɡ̊], a soft g | [ɪɡ̊], a soft g |
| -er as [ɐ] | yes | yes | less common |
| ch | [ç] after front vowels | [x] possible even after front vowels | |
| r | uvular | uvular, with tongue-tip r common in the south | tongue-tip r common |
| Long ä, /ɛː/ | usually said as [eː] | usually said as [eː] in the north and east | |

In Austrian standard German /b d g z/ are voiceless lenis, so there is nothing to harden at the end of a syllable. Devoicing is also absent in southern Germany, and the catch is rarer there. This reel models the dictionary standard usually taught to learners: it is a choice, not a ranking. Hearing the other standards is part of the skill.

### Mistakes in both directions

**What English speakers get wrong:** a short vowel in Miete; u for ü; k or h for ch; sch for ich; an English r (and a voiced final d in Rad); linking mein Auto as "mei Nauto". Note that English itself has [iː] and [ɪ] (beat, bit), so the problem is not that English speakers cannot hear a long and a short i. It is that German treats the difference as part of the word, and that the spelling ie does not signal a vowel to be shortened.

**What Germans get wrong in English:** they carry final devoicing and the catch over into the foreign language, which is part of a typical German accent in English. Learners will hear it from colleagues; it is the same two rules.

## Open questions

- **Is /ɛː/ a phoneme?** Pairs such as Ähre/Ehre and Bären/Beeren exist for speakers who keep [ɛː] and [eː] apart, but in the north of Germany and the east of Austria the contrast has largely gone.
- **Oper = Opa?** The dictionaries separate [ɐ] and [a] by height; the 2020 study found them merged in six northern speakers' read speech. Whether the merger holds in other dialects and in spontaneous speech is open.
- **Which ch is underlying?** The backing analysis treats [ç] as basic; the traditional analysis treats [x] as the phoneme and [ç] as its allophone. Kohler's [x]/[χ] split adds a third place.
- **Why is the glottal stop not a phoneme?** The pairs said to show it separating words all differ in something else too (stress, vowel length, or [t]/[d]); the die Ode/Diode example has a catch in both.
- **Küche.** [ˈkʏçə] in the dictionaries, but often a long [ˈkyːçə] in northern standard speech, according to the German Wiktionary citing the Duden pronunciation dictionary (2015).
- **Transcription conventions.** DWDS writes [ʀ] and [χ]; the reel writes [ʁ] and [x]; neither is wrong.
- **A newer use of the catch.** Since the mid-1980s a glottal stop is also used as a "gender pause" (Bäcker*innen). It is a matter of usage, not of the sound system; mentioned here only for awareness.
