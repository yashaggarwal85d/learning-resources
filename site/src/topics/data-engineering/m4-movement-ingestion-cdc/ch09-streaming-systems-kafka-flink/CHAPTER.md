## The chapter

### The problem it solves

Chapter 8 ended with a database's committed rows reaching the analytical side, in order, once. This chapter asks what happens when there is no database to extract from in the first place — when the thing you are handed is a stream someone is already appending to, with no "as of" snapshot, no `WHERE updated_at > :cursor`, and no natural end. The partitioned log (Kafka) is the one abstraction almost every other streaming idea in this chapter is built on top of or defined in opposition to: a durable, ordered, replayable sequence of records that many producers can write to and many consumers can read from independently, at their own pace, without the broker tracking who has read what. Once that log exists, two questions follow immediately and dominate the rest of the chapter. First, **correctness**: a crash can duplicate a write or a read, a network partition can reorder what looks ordered, and "exactly once" is a phrase thrown around far more casually than it is true — the chapter earns the right to use it precisely. Second, **computation over something that never ends**: a window has to close before all its data has technically "arrived" (there is no such moment), so every stream processor is really a machine for turning a policy decision (how late is late, how wrong can the first answer be) into a concrete program.

The chapter teaches, in order, the log itself (9.1): how a partition is actually bytes on disk, how a producer decides when to send and how much it risks losing by acknowledging early, how replication keeps a partition alive when a broker dies, how Kafka 4.x replaced ZooKeeper with its own replicated log of metadata, how a consumer group divides partitions among its members and what that division costs while it happens, how a log discards old data by time, size, or by key, what "ordered" actually means and does not mean, and how a log can be cheap to retain for a long time without keeping every byte on local disk. Then correctness (9.2): the vocabulary of at-least/at-most/exactly-once as statements about *effects*, not deliveries; the idempotent producer and Kafka's own transactions as the two building blocks a real exactly-once pipeline is assembled from; what happens when the far end is not Kafka at all; the operational reality of deduplication, dead letters, and replay; the contract a schema registry enforces between a world of independently-deployed producers and consumers; and the conceptual apparatus — event time, watermarks, windows, triggers — that the Dataflow Model gave the industry for being honest about completeness instead of pretending a late event never happened. Finally, the engines that run all of this continuously at scale (9.3): Flink's runtime and its asynchronous-barrier checkpointing, keyed state and what rescaling actually redistributes, the architectural shift to state that lives primarily on object storage, Flink's SQL layer over changing data, Kafka Streams as the library-not-cluster alternative, and where all of this lands — a lakehouse table or a real-time OLAP store — so that a stream's output is actually queryable by someone.

The learner leaves able to look at a streaming design and say exactly what can be lost, duplicated, or delayed, under which failure, and name the specific mechanism (an ISR, a transaction, a watermark, a checkpoint barrier) that closes each gap or the cost of not closing it.

### Order of ideas, and why

1. **9.1 Kafka internals (topics 1-8).** First the physical substrate everything else assumes: a partition as segment files with an offset index (1), because every later claim about "reading from an offset" or "retention" means something concrete only once this exists. Then the producer side of the same log (2): how writes actually leave the client, since replication (3) is about what happens to those writes once they're accepted. Then replication and the high watermark (3), the mechanism that makes a single partition durable across broker failure — placed before KRaft (4) because KRaft is "replication, but for the cluster's own metadata", easier to teach once the learner has the ISR/high-watermark model for ordinary data. Then consumer groups and rebalancing (5), the read side, which depends on partitions (1) existing and is otherwise independent of 2-4. Then what the log does to old data: retention and compaction (6), since compaction is what makes a topic usable as a changelog, a concept 9.2 and 9.3 both lean on. Then ordering guarantees (7), placed right after compaction because both are about what a consumer can rely on finding (or not) at a given offset. Last, tiered storage (8), which only makes sense once the learner knows retention (6) is a cost, not a law of nature.
2. **9.2 Streaming correctness (topics 9-19).** Delivery semantics (9) first, the vocabulary every later topic in this section assumes. Then the two building blocks, in the order a producer actually gets them: the idempotent producer (10), a single-producer-session guarantee, then transactions (11), which generalize it across partitions and into a consume-transform-produce loop — placed after (10) because transactions are built on the same producer-epoch mechanism. Then end-to-end exactly-once once Kafka is not the only system in the picture (12), which needs (11)'s vocabulary (two-phase commit, checkpoints) to state precisely. Then the two practical techniques that replace transactions when transactions aren't available or aren't enough: deduplication and idempotent consumers (13), then dead letters and retry topics (14), the operational answer to "a record simply can't be processed". Then replay and backfill (15), which depends on (6)'s retention/compaction and on (8)'s tiered storage, and is the direct remedy for a mistake replay is meant to fix. Then schema registry and compatibility (16) — placed here, slightly apart from the correctness chain, because schema breakage is the other way a "correct" pipeline silently produces garbage, and the chapter needs it taught before any topic draws a change-event payload casually. Then the Dataflow-Model concepts, which depend on nothing above except the general vocabulary of a stream: event time and watermarks (17), the time semantics; windowing (18), which needs (17)'s watermark to say when a window can close; and triggers, lateness and retractions (19), which needs (18)'s windows to have something to retract.
3. **9.3 Stream processing engines (topics 20-27).** Flink's runtime architecture (20) first, the mechanical model (JobManager, TaskManagers, slots, the network exchange) everything else in the section runs inside. Then checkpointing (21), Flink's own answer to exactly-once state, which depends on (20)'s network model (credit-based flow control, backpressure) to explain aligned vs unaligned barriers. Then keyed state and rescaling (22), which depends on (21) — a checkpoint is what a rescale restores from. Then disaggregated state (23), the Flink-2.x architectural shift, placed right after (22) because it only means something once the learner knows what state backends were before it (heap, RocksDB) and what rescaling used to cost. Then Flink SQL and dynamic tables (24), a layer on top of (20)-(23) that needs the runtime and state model as its substrate; Kafka Streams and the KStream/KTable duality (25), a deliberate contrast — a library, not a cluster, re-deriving a changelog-backed state model the learner already has from 9.1's compaction (6) and this section's (22). Finally, where the output goes: streaming into the lakehouse (26), which depends on Chapter 6's table formats (named, not retaught) and this chapter's exactly-once vocabulary (11-12); and real-time OLAP ingestion (27), the sibling destination, closing the chapter on "the stream has to land somewhere a person can query it".

### Shared notation and terms

- **The log's three axes** (topic 1 introduces, reused through 9.1): **offset** (a record's position within a partition, assigned once, never reused), **segment** (a bounded file of consecutive records plus its sparse offset/time index), and **partition** (the unit of ordering and parallelism — Kafka orders only within one). Every figure in 9.1 that draws a partition draws it as a left-to-right strip of segments with offsets ticked along it.
- **Replication vocabulary** (topic 3 introduces, reused through 9.1 and by Kafka Streams in 25): **leader**, **follower**, **ISR** (in-sync replica set), **high watermark** (the offset every ISR member has, the point a consumer may read up to), **leader epoch** (a monotonically increasing fencing number that invalidates a stale leader's writes after a failover).
- **Correctness vocabulary** (topic 9 introduces, reused through 9.2): **at-most-once**, **at-least-once**, **exactly-once** always said as a statement about the *effect* of processing, never about delivery, which is always at-least-once at best in a system with retries. **Idempotence** (topic 10): a producer id, an epoch, and a per-partition sequence number. **Transactional id** and **zombie fencing** (topic 11).
- **Dataflow-Model vocabulary** (topics 17-19 introduce, reused through 9.3): **event time** vs **processing time**, a **watermark** as an assertion ("no more data older than T will arrive", which can be wrong), a **window** (tumbling, sliding, session, global), a **trigger** (when a window emits), **allowed lateness**, and the three **accumulation modes** (discarding, accumulating, accumulating-and-retracting). A retraction is drawn as a signed delta card: `+` for an addition, `-` for a correction of an earlier emission.
- **Checkpoint vocabulary** (topics 21-23 introduce, reused through 9.3): a **checkpoint barrier**, **aligned** vs **unaligned**, a **savepoint** (a checkpoint taken deliberately, for a planned restart or rescale), a **key group** (the unit redistributed on rescale).
- **Spoken numbers:** narration has no digits, symbols or code names (the automatic check of earlier chapters): "high watermark", "key group", "idempotent producer", "aligned checkpoint" are said as plain words; any number is spoken aloud; setting names (`min.insync.replicas`, `linger.ms`) stay on screen and in the description, never in the narration.

### 1. Partitioned log storage: segments and indexes (`de.partitioned-log-segments-indexes`): full, 9.1

- **Folder:** `partitioned-log-segments-indexes`. About 15 min.
- **Scope:** "a partition as a directory of segment files with sparse offset and time indexes; active segment rolling; reads served from the page cache with zero-copy `sendfile`; offsets as positions owned by consumers, not the broker" — clause by clause:
  - *segment files with sparse indexes:* a partition directory holding `.log` (the records), `.index` (a sparse offset-to-byte-position map, not one entry per record), and `.timeindex` (timestamp-to-offset) files; built as a toy Python implementation with real byte offsets and a real sparse index, queried and timed.
  - *active segment rolling:* the currently-written segment rolls to a new file at a size or time limit (`log.segment.bytes`, `log.roll.ms`); shown by writing past the toy limit and watching a new segment open.
  - *reads served from the page cache with zero-copy `sendfile`:* Kafka's own design documentation describes avoiding a user-space copy by having the broker hand the kernel a file descriptor and let `sendfile` move bytes from the page cache straight to the socket; measured conceptually (what copies are avoided) since the sandbox cannot measure real kernel zero-copy, disclosed as sourced.
  - *offsets owned by consumers, not the broker:* the broker never tracks "has this consumer read offset N" as part of partition state — only `__consumer_offsets` (taught properly in topic 5) does, and deleting a consumer group's offsets does not touch the log at all; shown by the toy log being fully readable from offset 0 regardless of any consumer's position.
- **Leaves out:** how a producer decides when to send is topic 2; replication across brokers is topic 3; retention deleting whole segments and compaction rewriting them is topic 6; consumer offset storage mechanics are topic 5; the Rust build-it-yourself depth is *Rust Systems Engineering* Ch.27 (named, not retaught).
- **Outcomes:** Draw a partition's on-disk layout from segment and index files and explain why the index is sparse; demonstrate active-segment rolling with real byte counts; state what zero-copy reads avoid and why it matters at scale; explain why offsets are a consumer-owned concept, not a broker-tracked one.
- **Introduces:** `partition-segment-index`, `zero-copy-log-read`. **Uses:** none from earlier chapters (first topic of the chapter). **Declared:** basic familiarity with append-only files and binary search.
- **Bridge:** "Chapter 8 moved data from where it is produced to where it can be read, once and in order. This chapter takes the other kind of source: not a database to be read, but a stream someone is already appending to. This topic is the thing every later topic in this chapter assumes exists: the partitioned log itself, as bytes on disk." (Answers Chapter 8 topic 24's handoff.)
- **Handoff:** "A partition is a log a producer writes to. What a producer actually sends, and when, and what it risks by not waiting for an acknowledgement, is the next topic."
- **Prerequisites panel:** none required beyond general programming (Before this). **Pre-notes terms:** partition, offset, segment, sparse index, page cache, zero-copy.
- **Hook:** a team believes "Kafka is slow to seek", greps a 50 GB partition for one offset, and it returns in milliseconds because the sparse index only ever needed to scan one segment's worth of records, not the whole log; the figure shows the index jumping straight to the right segment while a naive linear scan crawls the whole strip.

### 2. Producer batching, compression and acks (`de.producer-batching-compression-acks`): full, 9.1

- **Folder:** `producer-batching-compression-acks`. About 15 min.
- **Scope:** "record accumulator per partition, `batch.size` and `linger.ms` (default 5 ms since Kafka 4.0), compression per batch (zstd/lz4), sticky partitioning; `acks=0/1/all` and why idempotence + `acks=all` is the default since 3.0" — clause by clause:
  - *record accumulator, `batch.size`/`linger.ms`:* records for a partition collect in an in-memory batch until it hits `batch.size` or `linger.ms` elapses, whichever first; toy-simulated with a clock and a queue, showing throughput vs latency trade-off numbers for a few `linger.ms` settings.

### 3. Replication, ISR and the high watermark (`de.replication-isr-high-watermark`): full, 9.1

- **Folder:** `replication-isr-high-watermark`. About 15 min. Hosts the leader-kill leg of the 9.1 chapter lab.
- **Scope:** "leader/follower fetch replication, in-sync replica set, high watermark as the committed point, `min.insync.replicas`; leader-epoch-based truncation; unclean leader election vs Eligible Leader Replicas (KIP-966, default on new clusters from 4.1) and the \"last replica standing\" data-loss case" — clause by clause:
  - *fetch replication, ISR, high watermark:* followers pull from the leader (not pushed to); the ISR is the set that has fetched up to a recent enough point; the high watermark is the minimum offset acknowledged by every ISR member, and only records at or below it are visible to consumers; `min.insync.replicas` sets how many must ack for an `acks=all` write to succeed.
  - *leader-epoch-based truncation:* a follower that resumes after a leader change truncates any divergent tail using leader-epoch markers rather than comparing raw offsets, avoiding silent data divergence.
  - *unclean leader election vs ELR:* classic unclean election (promoting a replica outside the ISR, risking data loss) against Eligible Leader Replicas (KIP-966), a narrower, safer pool the controller tracks explicitly, default on new clusters from Kafka 4.1 per the KIP.
  - *the "last replica standing" case:* when `min.insync.replicas` shrinks the effective ISR to one replica under failures, that replica alone can acknowledge writes that are then lost if it dies before any other replica catches up — the precise scenario the lab's loss-counting experiment reproduces.
- **Leaves out:** the metadata-quorum version of this same replication idea (KRaft's own Raft log) is topic 4; what a producer actually sends before it gets here is topic 2; consumer-side offset tracking is topic 5.

### 7. Kafka ordering guarantees (`de.kafka-ordering-guarantees`): full, 9.1

- **Folder:** `kafka-ordering-guarantees`. About 15 min.
- **Scope:** "total order only within a partition; idempotent producers preserve order with up to 5 in-flight requests; key-to-partition stability breaks when partition counts change; designing keys so the order you need is the order you get" — clause by clause:
  - *total order only within a partition:* Kafka makes zero ordering promise across partitions; two records with different keys (hence, usually, different partitions) have no defined relative order at all, a fact drawn directly from the partition-strip model (topic 1).
  - *idempotent producers and in-flight requests:* with `enable.idempotence=true`, Kafka's idempotent producer preserves send order even with multiple requests in flight (up to five, the documented maximum for ordering guarantees to hold), by having the broker reject an out-of-order sequence number rather than accept it; without idempotence, a retried batch can be accepted out of order after a transient failure.

### 9. Delivery semantics and where duplicates arise (`de.delivery-semantics-duplicates`): full, 9.2

- **Folder:** `delivery-semantics-duplicates`. About 15 min.

  - *the three semantics, as statements about effects:* at-most-once (never redeliver, so a lost ack means lost data), at-least-once (redeliver on any doubt, so duplicates are possible but nothing is silently lost), exactly-once (the *effect* of processing happens exactly once, regardless of how many times delivery actually occurred) — stated precisely enough that "Kafka guarantees exactly-once" is shown to be meaningless without naming which boundary it applies to.

  - *exactly-once as end-to-end:* the broker alone cannot make a whole pipeline exactly-once — a sink that isn't idempotent or transactional turns at-least-once delivery straight back into duplicate effects, which is why the next several topics (10-14) each supply one missing piece.

### 10. Idempotent producer (`de.idempotent-producer`): full, 9.2

- **Folder:** `idempotent-producer`. About 15 min.
- **Scope:** "producer id + epoch + per-partition sequence numbers let the broker reject duplicates and out-of-order batches; scope (one producer session, one partition) and what it does not cover" — clause by clause:
  - *the mechanism:* on enabling idempotence, the broker assigns the producer a **producer id (PID)**; every batch sent to a given partition carries a monotonically increasing **sequence number**; the broker tracks the last accepted sequence number per (PID, partition) and rejects (as a harmless duplicate) any batch whose sequence number it has already seen, or any batch that arrives out of order relative to what it expects — exactly the mechanism that closes topic 9's first crash window, built as a toy Python simulation that really rejects a replayed sequence number.

  - *transactional id and fencing:* a stable, application-assigned **transactional id** (distinct from the per-session PID) lets a producer recover its transactional state across restarts; a new instance with the same transactional id gets a bumped producer epoch that fences out any zombie instance still trying to write under the old epoch — a toy transaction-coordinator state machine demonstrates a zombie's write being rejected.
  - *the coordinator and `__transaction_state`:* a dedicated transaction coordinator broker tracks each transaction's state (ongoing, committing, committed, aborting, aborted) in the internal `__transaction_state` topic, and writes **commit** or **abort control markers** into every partition the transaction touched.
  - *last stable offset (LSO) and `read_committed`:* the LSO is the point past which no open transaction's writes exist; a `read_committed` consumer never sees a record from a transaction that later aborts, by only reading up to the LSO and filtering around open/aborted ranges — demonstrated on the toy coordinator by showing an aborted transaction's records invisible to a `read_committed` reader but visible to a `read_uncommitted` one.
  - *consume-transform-produce with `sendOffsetsToTransaction`:* committing a consumer's input offsets inside the same transaction as its output writes, so "I processed up to here" and "I wrote this output" either both happen or neither does — the exact mechanism that closes topic 9's second crash window end to end within Kafka.
  - *transactions v2 (KIP-890):* server-side defence against a client that starts a transaction and then hangs forever without committing or aborting (which used to pin the LSO and block every `read_committed` consumer indefinitely); an epoch bump per transaction (not just per producer session) tightens fencing further; completed in Kafka 4.0 per the KIP and release notes.
  - *KIP-939:* lets an external transactional system (notably a database) participate in a two-phase commit alongside a Kafka transaction, so a Kafka write and a database write can be made atomic together — named and sourced, not built (no real two-system 2PC is runnable in the sandbox).
- **Leaves out:** what happens when the "external sink" isn't Kafka at all (a database, a file sink) needs its own two-phase-commit or idempotent-upsert pattern, taught in topic 12; application-level dedup when transactions aren't available is topic 13.
- **Outcomes:** Explain transactional id, zombie fencing, and the coordinator/`__transaction_state` model; reproduce a `read_committed` consumer correctly ignoring an aborted transaction's writes; implement consume-transform-produce with offsets committed inside the transaction and show a kill-mid-commit scenario producing zero duplicates and zero gaps; state what KIP-890 and KIP-939 each add.
- **Introduces:** `kafka-transaction-coordinator`, `read-committed-lso`, `consume-transform-produce-eos`. **Uses:** `idempotent-producer-pid-epoch`, `leader-epoch-fencing`, `delivery-semantics-vocabulary`. **Declared:** none beyond topic 10.

### 12. End-to-end exactly-once with external sinks (`de.end-to-end-exactly-once-sinks`): full, 9.2

- **Folder:** `end-to-end-exactly-once-sinks`. About 15 min.
- **Scope:** "two-phase-commit sinks (pre-commit on checkpoint, commit on checkpoint-complete), idempotent upsert sinks keyed by deterministic ids, storing offsets atomically with the output; where it breaks (non-deterministic processing, external side effects, transaction timeouts shorter than checkpoint intervals)" — clause by clause:
  - *two-phase-commit sinks:* a sink pre-commits its pending write when a checkpoint is taken (the data is durably staged but not yet visible) and only finalizes ("commits") it once the checkpoint itself is confirmed complete — the same two-phase shape KIP-939 (topic 11) formalizes for Kafka-plus-database, generalized to any transactional sink (Flink's TwoPhaseCommitSinkFunction family is the canonical example, named and sourced).
  - *idempotent upsert sinks:* instead of two-phase commit, a sink that writes by **deterministic id** (an upsert keyed on a value derived from the input, not a counter) makes a retried write harmless because it overwrites the same row rather than appending a new one — the direct external-sink analogue of topic 10's producer idempotence.
  - *storing offsets atomically with the output:* the offset marking "how far we've consumed" must be committed in the same atomic unit as the output write (the same transaction, or the same upsert row, e.g. a side table updated in the same statement) — if they're stored separately, a crash between them reintroduces topic 9's exact crash window at the sink boundary.
  - *where it breaks:* **non-deterministic processing** (a random value, a wall-clock timestamp computed differently on retry) means a retried write is not actually the same write, breaking idempotent-upsert's premise; **external side effects** (an email sent, a payment API called) cannot be rolled back by any database transaction, so "exactly-once effect" is simply false once a side effect like that exists; **transaction timeouts shorter than checkpoint intervals** abort an in-flight two-phase-commit transaction before the checkpoint that was supposed to complete it ever does, silently losing the staged write — a configuration mismatch that is a documented, real production failure mode.
- **Leaves out:** this topic's two-phase pattern as Flink specifically implements it for checkpointed jobs is topic 21 (named, not retaught here); CDC's own version of exactly-once-to-a-sink was already taught in Chapter 8 (`cdc-exactly-once-caveats`, used by name).
- **Outcomes:** Implement a toy two-phase-commit sink and a toy idempotent-upsert sink and show both survive a mid-write crash with no duplicate and no loss; explain why offsets must be committed atomically with output, not separately; name the three concrete ways end-to-end exactly-once breaks and give a real example of each.
- **Introduces:** `two-phase-commit-sink`, `idempotent-upsert-deterministic-id`, `exactly-once-failure-modes`. **Uses:** `kafka-transaction-coordinator`, `consume-transform-produce-eos`, `checkpoint-output-atomicity` (Chapter 8 topic 6), `exactly-once-effect-recipe` (Chapter 8 topic 6). **Declared:** none beyond topic 11.

- **Handoff:** "A deterministic id is one specific way to make a retried write harmless. The general version of that idea — tracking which event ids have already been applied, at all — is its own topic, and it's needed even when no transaction or two-phase sink exists at all."

### 13. Deduplication keys and idempotent consumers (`de.deduplication-idempotent-consumers`): full, 9.2

- **Folder:** `deduplication-idempotent-consumers`. About 15 min.
- **Scope:** "event ids assigned at the source, dedup state bounded by TTL or watermark, approximate dedup with Bloom filters; deterministic ids for derived events" — clause by clause:
  - *event ids at the source:* a unique id assigned once, by the producer, at creation — never regenerated on retry — is the one piece of information that lets any downstream consumer recognize a redelivery as the *same* event rather than a new one, independent of any Kafka-level mechanism.
  - *dedup state bounded by TTL or watermark:* remembering every event id forever is unbounded memory; a consumer instead keeps a bounded dedup window — either a time-to-live on each remembered id, or (once topic 17 exists) a watermark-relative bound ("forget ids older than the current watermark minus some slack") — accepting that a redelivery older than the window is no longer deduplicated, a conscious, stated trade-off.
  - *approximate dedup with Bloom filters:* for very large dedup sets, a Bloom filter trades a small, bounded false-positive rate (occasionally treating a genuinely new event as a duplicate) for memory that doesn't grow linearly with the number of ids seen — the probabilistic-structures concept Chapter 5's §5.6 already introduced, used here, not re-derived.
  - *deterministic ids for derived events:* when a consumer itself produces a new event derived from an input (not just passing the input through), giving that derived event a deterministic id (a function of its inputs) lets the *next* consumer downstream deduplicate it the same way — propagating idempotence through a chain of processing stages instead of stopping at the first hop.
- **Leaves out:** the Kafka-native producer/transaction mechanisms this topic is the application-level fallback for are topics 10-11; the probabilistic-structure mechanics of a Bloom filter itself are Chapter 5 (used by name, not retaught).
- **Outcomes:** Design an event-id scheme assigned once at the source; bound a dedup window by TTL or watermark and state what it deliberately gives up; use a Bloom filter for large-scale approximate dedup and quantify its false-positive cost; derive a deterministic id for a derived event and show deduplication propagating through a second processing stage.
- **Introduces:** `event-id-deduplication`, `bounded-dedup-window`. **Uses:** `delivery-semantics-vocabulary`, `two-phase-commit-sink`, `idempotent-upsert-deterministic-id`, `count-min-sketch` (Chapter 5 §5.6, Bloom-filter-adjacent probabilistic structure, used as the pattern). **Declared:** none beyond topic 12.

### 14. Dead-letter and retry topics (`de.dead-letter-retry-topics`): full, 9.2

- **Folder:** `dead-letter-retry-topics`. About 15 min.
- **Scope:** "poison messages that block a partition; non-blocking retry topics with tiered delays and the ordering they sacrifice; DLQ payload and metadata design, alerting and replay tooling (Kafka Connect `errors.tolerance`, Kafka Streams DLQ handlers from 4.2)" — clause by clause:
  - *poison messages:* a record a consumer can never successfully process (malformed, referencing missing state) that, if simply retried in place, blocks every record behind it in the same partition forever — a direct consequence of topic 7's per-partition ordering: you cannot skip ahead without breaking order for everything else in that partition.
  - *non-blocking retry topics with tiered delays:* instead of retrying in place, a failed record is republished to a separate `retry-5s`, `retry-1m`, `retry-1h`-style topic (tiered by increasing delay) so the original partition keeps moving; the record is reprocessed later from the retry topic, at the cost of no longer preserving its original position relative to records that succeeded on the first try.
  - *the ordering sacrificed:* explicitly named — a retried record, by construction, is now out of its original order relative to everything that was behind it and already succeeded; this is accepted as the price of not blocking the partition, not hidden.
  - *DLQ payload and metadata design:* a dead-letter record carries the original payload plus metadata (the original topic/partition/offset, the exception, a timestamp, a retry count) so that an operator or a replay tool has enough to diagnose and, if fixed, replay it.
  - *alerting and replay tooling:* Kafka Connect's `errors.tolerance` setting controls whether a connector dead-letters a bad record or fails the whole task; Kafka Streams gained its own DLQ handler support from version 4.2 per the syllabus and release notes — both named as the concrete, current implementations of this pattern.
- **Leaves out:** replaying a dead-lettered or retry-topic record back into the main flow once fixed overlaps with the general replay mechanics of topic 15, used there; the general dedup machinery a replay needs is topic 13 (used).
- **Outcomes:** Explain why a poison message blocks a partition and why in-place retry makes it worse; design a tiered retry-topic scheme and state exactly what ordering guarantee it gives up; design a DLQ record's metadata for diagnosability; name the current Kafka Connect and Kafka Streams mechanisms for this pattern.
- **Introduces:** `poison-message-blocking`, `tiered-retry-topics`, `dlq-metadata-design`. **Uses:** `kafka-ordering-scope`, `event-id-deduplication`. **Declared:** none beyond topic 13.
- **Bridge:** "Deduplication assumes a record can eventually be processed. - **Prerequisites panel:** topics 7, 13 (Before this). **Pre-notes terms:** poison message, dead-letter queue, retry topic, tiered delay, `errors.tolerance`.

### 17. Event time, watermarks and completeness (`de.event-time-watermarks-completeness`): full, 9.2

- **Folder:** `event-time-watermarks-completeness`. About 15 min. Generates the 9.2 chapter lab's synthetic late/duplicate event stream.

  - *event time vs processing time:* **event time** (when something actually happened, stamped by the source) against **processing time** (when the stream processor happens to see it) — the two needles of this chapter's shared time-axis figure, which can drift apart arbitrarily under network delay, retries, or backlogs.
  - *watermarks as an assertion:* a watermark is a claim — "no more data with event time before T will arrive" — that is a **heuristic** in almost every real system (it can be wrong, and late data after the watermark has passed is exactly what topic 19 handles) rather than a provable guarantee, except in narrow cases with perfectly ordered, complete sources.
  - *per-partition generation and idle sources:* a watermark is generated per source partition from the records actually seen on it, which creates a problem when one partition goes **idle** (no new records at all): a naive global watermark that waits for the slowest partition never advances if an idle partition simply never reports anything, so idle-source detection (advancing a partition's local watermark on a timeout even with no data) is a necessary, named refinement.
  - *watermark alignment:* without coordination, a fast source's watermark can race far ahead of a slow source's, forcing a downstream operator to either buffer enormously or emit against an unrealistically advanced watermark; alignment mechanisms hold a fast source back to bound how far its watermark can outrun the slowest one.
  - *the latency vs completeness trade-off:* a tighter watermark (less slack for late data) means lower latency to a result but a higher chance that result is later revised (topic 19); a looser watermark means higher latency but fewer, more complete results on the first try — made concrete with a measured curve on the toy synthetic stream (built here, reused by topic 18's lab).

### 19. Triggers, allowed lateness and retractions (`de.triggers-lateness-retractions`): full, 9.2

  - *early/on-time/late firings:* a window can fire **early** (before its watermark-defined "on time" point, giving a speculative, incomplete answer sooner), exactly **on time** (when the watermark passes the window's end), or **late** (a correction after a late record arrives within the allowed-lateness period) — each demonstrated on the toy aggregator as a sequence of real emitted values for the same window, visibly changing.
  - *allowed lateness vs dropping:* a window configured with an allowed-lateness period keeps its state open past the watermark for exactly that long, accepting late corrections; once that period passes, a late record is simply **dropped** (the pane's state is gone) — a deliberate, bounded trade-off between correctness and unbounded state retention, not a bug.

### 20. Flink runtime architecture (`de.flink-runtime-architecture`): full, 9.3

- **Folder:** `flink-runtime-architecture`. About 15 min.
- **Scope:** "JobManager/TaskManagers, slots, operator chaining, pipelined network exchange with credit-based flow control and backpressure; application mode (per-job mode removed in 2.0); unified batch/stream execution with adaptive batch scheduling" — clause by clause:
  - *JobManager/TaskManagers and slots:* the **JobManager** schedules a job's tasks and coordinates checkpoints; **TaskManagers** run the actual work, each offering a fixed number of **slots** (the unit of resource allocation a task is scheduled into); a job's parallelism is bounded by the total slots available across all TaskManagers.
  - *operator chaining:* consecutive operators with compatible parallelism and no shuffle between them are fused into one task at runtime (no network hop, no serialization between them), reducing overhead — shown as a toy cost comparison between chained and deliberately un-chained equivalent operators.
  - *pipelined network exchange, credit-based flow control, backpressure:* data moves between tasks as a continuous pipelined stream (not batch handoffs); **credit-based flow control** means a receiving task grants the sender "credits" (buffer slots it's allowed to fill) rather than the sender pushing unboundedly, which is exactly how **backpressure** propagates upstream without an explicit signal — a slow consumer simply grants fewer credits, and the slowdown ripples backward through the whole chain, demonstrated conceptually with a toy credit-counter simulation.
  - *application mode, per-job mode removed in 2.0:* **application mode** runs the job's `main()` on the cluster itself (one JobManager per application, not shared), the model Flink standardized on; the older **per-job mode** (a dedicated cluster per job, spun up by the client) was removed in Flink 2.0 per its own release notes, a currency fact stated precisely, not glossed as "the modern way" without saying the old way is actually gone.
  - *unified batch/stream execution, adaptive batch scheduling:* the same runtime and APIs execute both bounded (batch) and unbounded (stream) jobs; adaptive batch scheduling adjusts a batch job's parallelism between stages based on the actual data volume produced by the previous stage, rather than fixing parallelism up front — named and sourced from Flink's own documentation.
- **Leaves out:** how a job's state survives a failure (checkpointing, the mechanism credit-based flow control's backpressure interacts with directly) is topic 21; what the state itself is and where it's rescaled is topic 22; this topic is the mechanical skeleton only.
- **Outcomes:** Draw the JobManager/TaskManager/slot relationship and compute a job's maximum parallelism from available slots; explain operator chaining and when it can't apply; explain how credit-based flow control makes backpressure an emergent property rather than an explicit message; state what application mode replaced and when adaptive batch scheduling applies.

### 21. Asynchronous barrier snapshots (`de.async-barrier-snapshots`): full, 9.3

- **Folder:** `async-barrier-snapshots`. About 15 min. Hosts the aligned-vs-unaligned leg of the 9.3 chapter lab.
- **Scope:** "Chandy–Lamport-derived checkpoint barriers, aligned vs unaligned checkpoints under backpressure, incremental checkpoints, exactly-once vs at-least-once mode; checkpoints vs savepoints and restoring a changed job" — clause by clause:
  - *Chandy–Lamport-derived barriers:* a **checkpoint barrier** is injected at every source and flows through the job graph just like a regular record, carried along by the same pipelined exchange topic 20 described; an operator that receives a barrier on all its input channels has seen everything "before" that checkpoint on every input, the same global-snapshot idea Chandy and Lamport's distributed-snapshot algorithm formalized, applied here to a dataflow graph instead of a general message-passing system.
  - *aligned vs unaligned, under backpressure:* **aligned** checkpoints make an operator wait until a barrier has arrived on *every* input channel before taking its snapshot and forwarding the barrier — under backpressure, a slow channel's barrier can take a long time to arrive, stalling the whole checkpoint (the exact alignment stall the State Management in Apache Flink paper, already named in the syllabus's Milestone 4 material, identifies as the motivating problem); **unaligned** checkpoints let an operator snapshot immediately on the *first* barrier it sees, including the in-flight data already buffered ahead of the barrier on the other channels, trading a larger snapshot for no alignment stall — demonstrated on the toy barrier-sweep simulation reproducing the stall qualitatively and showing it vanish under the unaligned variant.
  - *incremental checkpoints:* instead of re-snapshotting all state every time, an incremental checkpoint only writes the state that changed since the last checkpoint (RocksDB's own incremental-checkpoint support, named and sourced, is the concrete mechanism), bounding checkpoint cost by the size of the *delta*, not the whole state.
  - *exactly-once vs at-least-once mode:* Flink's checkpointing can run in a mode that guarantees exactly-once *state* consistency (the default) or a cheaper at-least-once mode that skips some of the barrier-alignment overhead at the cost of occasionally double-processing a record on recovery — a real, named configuration trade-off, not a hidden default.
  - *checkpoints vs savepoints, restoring a changed job:* a **checkpoint** is Flink's own automatic, periodic mechanism, managed and expired by Flink itself; a **savepoint** is a checkpoint taken deliberately, in a self-contained, portable format, specifically so a job can be stopped and restarted — possibly with a changed job graph, a changed parallelism, or a changed Flink version — and still resume from it, which an ordinary checkpoint is not guaranteed to support across such changes.
- **Leaves out:** what the snapshotted state actually consists of, and how rescaling redistributes it, is topic 22; the disaggregated-state architectural shift that changes what a checkpoint even costs is topic 23.
- **Outcomes:** Explain how a checkpoint barrier generalizes the Chandy-Lamport snapshot idea to a dataflow graph; reproduce the aligned-checkpoint stall under induced backpressure and show it disappear under unaligned checkpoints, with measured durations; explain what an incremental checkpoint bounds its cost by; distinguish exactly-once from at-least-once checkpointing mode; distinguish a checkpoint from a savepoint by what each is guaranteed to survive.

### 23. Disaggregated state in Flink 2.x (`de.disaggregated-state-flink`): full, 9.3

- **Folder:** `disaggregated-state-flink`. About 15 min. Hosts the RocksDB-vs-ForSt comparison leg of the 9.3 chapter lab.
- **Scope:** "ForSt stores state primarily on remote object storage with local disk as cache; the asynchronous execution model and async state API hide remote latency while preserving per-key order; checkpoints become near-metadata operations, enabling fast recovery and rescaling; what latency-sensitive jobs give up" — clause by clause:
  - *ForSt's architecture:* unlike RocksDB (state primarily on the TaskManager's own local disk, topic 22), **ForSt** keeps the **primary** copy of state on remote, distributed object storage, using local disk only as a cache — inverting which copy is authoritative, the exact shift the "Disaggregated state in Flink 2.0" paper (Mei et al., 2025 VLDB, already named in the syllabus's Milestone 4 material) frames as the motivating architectural change.
  - *the asynchronous execution model and async state API:* because a state access can now mean a remote round trip rather than a local disk read, Flink 2.x's async state API lets an operator issue a state request and continue processing other records while that request is in flight, rather than blocking the whole task on every access — and crucially **preserves per-key order** (two requests for the same key still complete in the order issued) even though requests for different keys may complete out of order, which is exactly what a naive async rewrite would otherwise break.
  - *checkpoints as near-metadata operations:* since the state's primary copy already lives on the same remote storage a checkpoint would copy it to, taking a checkpoint largely means recording *which* remote objects constitute the current state, not physically copying gigabytes of data — the direct explanation for the paper's own reported large recovery and checkpoint-time improvements, named and sourced, never asserted without the paper's own numbers.

- **Scope:** "streams as changelogs of dynamic tables (append, upsert, retract), regular joins with unbounded state vs interval, temporal and lookup joins, delta joins and multi-way joins that cut state; materialised tables; process table functions; `FROM_CHANGELOG`/`TO_CHANGELOG` (2.3); model inference with `ML_PREDICT` (2.1+)" — clause by clause:
  - *streams as changelogs of dynamic tables:* Flink SQL's foundational idea — a stream is a **dynamic table**'s changelog, in one of three modes: **append** (every record is a pure insert, the table only grows), **upsert** (a record replaces the prior row with the same key, the exact relationship topic 6's compacted-topic-as-changelog idea generalizes into SQL), or **retract** (every change is an explicit retract-then-insert pair, the SQL-level realization of topic 19's retraction streams) — demonstrated on the toy `orders.events` stream read as each of the three table modes.
  - *regular, interval, temporal and lookup joins, delta and multi-way joins:* a **regular join** between two unbounded streams must keep *all* history of both sides as state forever (any row could still match a future row); an **interval join** bounds that state by only matching rows within a stated time window of each other; a **temporal join** matches against the state of a changing table *as of* the other side's event time (a "price at the time of the order", not the current price); a **lookup join** enriches against an external, mostly-static table rather than another stream; **delta joins** and **multi-way joins** are newer optimizations (named and sourced from Flink's own SQL documentation) that avoid materializing full intermediate join state by pushing filtering earlier or sharing state across more than two inputs — each compared by what state it actually needs to keep, the single axis that makes the whole list coherent.
  - *materialised tables:* a Flink SQL object that defines its own refresh (continuous or periodic) and is queried like an ordinary table, bridging the SQL layer toward the lakehouse-landing concerns of topic 26.
  - *process table functions:* a user-defined-function mechanism that can read and transform a whole dynamic table's changelog with custom logic, the SQL layer's escape hatch for logic that doesn't fit a declarative query.
  - *`FROM_CHANGELOG`/`TO_CHANGELOG` (2.3) and `ML_PREDICT` (2.1+):* `FROM_CHANGELOG`/`TO_CHANGELOG` let a query explicitly convert between a raw changelog stream and a dynamic table view of it, named as Flink-2.3-era functions (a currency fact to re-verify at build time); `ML_PREDICT` lets a SQL query call a model for inference inline, from Flink 2.1 onward, named as the concrete bridge between streaming SQL and model serving.
- **Leaves out:** the runtime and state mechanics these joins and tables are built on were taught in topics 20-23 (used, not retaught); real-time OLAP ingestion as an alternative destination for query is topic 27.
- **Outcomes:** Read a stream as append, upsert, or retract changelog and predict the resulting table's behaviour; choose among regular, interval, temporal, lookup, delta and multi-way joins for a stated state-cost constraint; explain what a materialised table and a process table function each add; place `FROM_CHANGELOG`/`TO_CHANGELOG` and `ML_PREDICT` correctly by version.
- **Introduces:** `dynamic-table-changelog-modes`, `streaming-join-state-cost`. **Uses:** `retention-vs-compaction`, `accumulation-modes-retraction`, `state-backend-comparison`. **Declared:** basic SQL (Chapter 2).
- **Bridge:** "Everything about Flink so far has been operators and state, underneath any particular API. This topic is the layer most people actually write: SQL over streams, formalized as changelogs of dynamic tables, with a join's cost measured entirely by how much state it has to keep." (Answers topic 23's handoff.)
- **Handoff:** "Kafka Streams takes almost the same changelog-and-table idea and implements it as a library inside your own application, with no separate cluster at all. Seeing the same idea built the other way sharpens both. That's next."
- **Prerequisites panel:** topics 6, 19, 22-23, Chapter 2 (Before this). **Pre-notes terms:** dynamic table, changelog, append/upsert/retract, regular/interval/temporal/lookup join, materialised table.
- **Hook:** a regular join between two growing streams on the toy SQL-changelog interpreter keeps every row of both sides forever and its state grows without bound, while switching the identical query to an interval join (bounding the match window to the real delay between an order and its payment) keeps state flat — measured directly as a state-size-over-time curve for both; the figure is those two curves, one climbing, one flat.

### 25. Kafka Streams and the KStream/KTable duality (`de.kafka-streams-ktable-duality`): full, 9.3

- **Folder:** `kafka-streams-ktable-duality`. About 15 min.
- **Scope:** "a library, not a cluster: tasks per input partition, local state stores backed by compacted changelog topics, standby replicas, exactly-once v2; stream-table duality; the Streams rebalance protocol; ksqlDB as the SQL layer on top (Confluent Community License, no longer accepting community contributions)" — clause by clause:
  - *a library, not a cluster:* Kafka Streams has no separate broker tier or scheduler of its own — it is a client library linked into an ordinary application process; **tasks** map one-to-one to input partitions, and parallelism is just "how many application instances are running", a direct, deliberate contrast to topic 20's Flink JobManager/TaskManager model.
  - *local state stores backed by compacted changelog topics:* a `KTable`'s state lives in a local, embedded key-value store (RocksDB by default, named and compared to topic 22's Flink state-backend discussion) on whichever instance owns that task, and is made durable and recoverable by continuously writing it to a **compacted changelog topic** — exactly topic 6's compaction mechanism, now shown as the thing a real system is built directly on top of, not just a theoretical possibility.
  - *standby replicas:* an optional, warm-standby copy of a task's local state, kept on a different instance by consuming the same changelog topic, so a failover doesn't have to rebuild state entirely from the changelog from scratch — a direct analogue to topic 3's ISR idea, applied to application-level state instead of broker-level log replication.
  - *exactly-once v2:* Kafka Streams' own current exactly-once processing guarantee, built on top of topic 11's Kafka transactions (named explicitly as "the same mechanism, used by the library on your behalf"), with v2 referring to a documented efficiency improvement over the original exactly-once implementation (fewer producers needed per task), a currency fact to reconfirm at build time.
  - *stream-table duality:* a `KStream` (a stream of independent events) and a `KTable` (a changelog-updated, current-value-per-key table) are two views of the same underlying idea — a stream can always be aggregated into a table, and a table's changes can always be read back out as a stream — the precise, named relationship topic 24's dynamic-table changelog modes already generalized at the SQL level.
  - *the Streams rebalance protocol:* named in topic 5 as GA with a limited feature set in Kafka 4.2; here it's revisited specifically for what it does for stateful task placement (trying to reassign a task back to an instance that already holds a standby or warm state for it, reducing rebuild time after a rebalance).
  - *ksqlDB and its licence:* ksqlDB is Confluent's SQL layer built on top of Kafka Streams, under the Confluent Community License, and the syllabus's own note that it is "no longer accepting community contributions" is stated plainly as a real, current governance fact (to be re-verified against Confluent's own ksqlDB repository/documentation at build time), not elaborated on speculatively.
- **Leaves out:** the detailed mechanics of compaction and the ISR idea this topic reuses were taught in full in topics 6 and 3; Flink SQL's own, cluster-based version of the stream-table idea was topic 24 (contrasted, not repeated).
- **Outcomes:** Explain why Kafka Streams needs no separate cluster and what "a task per partition" means for its parallelism model; explain how a `KTable`'s local state store relates to its changelog topic and what a standby replica buys on failover; state the stream-table duality precisely, with the shop's own `orders.events` as the example; place ksqlDB's licence status and the Streams rebalance protocol's current scope accurately.
- **Introduces:** `kafka-streams-task-model`, `stream-table-duality`, `changelog-backed-state-store`. **Uses:** `retention-vs-compaction`, `isr-high-watermark`, `kafka-transaction-coordinator`, `rebalance-protocol-comparison` (topic 5), `dynamic-table-changelog-modes`. **Declared:** none beyond topic 24.
- **Bridge:** "Flink SQL formalized streams as changelogs of dynamic tables, run by a separate cluster. Kafka Streams builds almost the identical idea — a stream and a table as two views of the same thing — as a library with no cluster of its own at all, and this topic is that contrast, concretely." (Answers topic 24's handoff.)
- **Handoff:** "Every engine in this chapter eventually has to put its output somewhere a person, or another system, can actually query. The next two topics are those two destinations: the lakehouse, and a real-time OLAP store."
- **Prerequisites panel:** topics 3, 5, 6, 11, 24 (Before this). **Pre-notes terms:** task, local state store, changelog topic, standby replica, exactly-once v2, stream-table duality, ksqlDB.
