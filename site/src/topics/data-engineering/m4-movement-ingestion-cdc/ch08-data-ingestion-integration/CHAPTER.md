## The chapter

### The problem it solves

Milestone 3 ended with engines that compute brilliantly over data already sitting in storage. This chapter, which opens Milestone 4, asks the question those engines took for granted: how did it get there, from systems that were never built to be read by an analytics platform, without losing a row, duplicating one, or taking the source down? The classic incident is two sentences long. A nightly extract reads `WHERE updated_at > :last_run`; a transaction that began before midnight commits at 00:01 with an `updated_at` of 23:58, and that row is never extracted, ever, with no error anywhere. And the other classic: the team switches to log-based change data capture, the consumer stalls over a weekend, and on Monday the production database's disk is full of write-ahead log that a replication slot refuses to release. This chapter is the discipline between those two failures.

It teaches, in order, the **extract layer** first, because every pipeline starts with a decision about who owns the schedule and the buffer, and because the failure modes of cursor-based extraction are exactly what motivates log-based capture (8.1). It teaches how to read a production database **without hurting it** (parallel range reads, consistent snapshots, replicas, and what a long extract does to vacuum and replica lag), how to extract **incrementally** and why a timestamp cursor lies, how to ingest from **APIs** and from **files** whose object stores have no atomic rename, how to make any of this **idempotent and resumable**, what an immutable **raw landing layer** must record so the whole warehouse can be rebuilt by replay, and when to **buy** a connector instead of building one. It then opens the **database's own change log** (8.2): query-based against log-based capture, then the internals that make log-based capture work and fail on PostgreSQL and MySQL (logical decoding, slots, `REPLICA IDENTITY`, GTIDs), the WAL-retention incident, how an initial snapshot hands off to the stream with no gap and no overlap, how DBLog-style incremental snapshots re-snapshot one table while the log keeps flowing, what order and transaction boundaries survive a trip through Kafka, how schema changes and deletes travel, what exactly-once can honestly mean for a CDC pipeline, Debezium's architecture, the transactional outbox, the Kafka-less Flink CDC path, and finally landing all of it in a lakehouse or warehouse. It closes with the part of integration that is about meaning rather than transport (8.3): the **master data management styles** and **semantic integration** of schemas, codes and units, so that two systems' records of one customer or one plant reconcile.

The learner leaves able to look at an ingestion design and name which rows its cursor misses, which crash windows duplicate, what its slot can pin, and who owns the truth when two systems disagree.

### Order of ideas, and why

1. **8.1 Ingestion patterns and the extract layer (topics 1-8).** First the vocabulary of a pipeline's front door, a refresher (1), because every later topic says "pull", "push", "micro-batch" and means one of them. Then the hardest constraint on any extract, the source must keep serving customers (2). Then the most common extraction technique and its most common silent bug, the high-watermark cursor (3), placed straight after (2) because the cursor is an optimisation of (2)'s full read. Then the two other source shapes that have no database log to read: a rate-limited API (4) and a directory of files on an object store (5). Then the property all four need and none gives for free, idempotent and resumable extraction (6), which is the general recipe (3)-(5) each applied piecemeal. Then where the extracted bytes first land, the raw/bronze layer (7), because it is the thing idempotency and replay are *for*. Then the buy-or-build decision (8), last, because it can only be judged by someone who knows what a good connector must do (1)-(7).
2. **8.2 CDC architecture (topics 9-22).** Query-based against log-based capture first (9), the pivot of the chapter and the direct answer to the cursor's missed deletes and missed intermediate states in (3). Then the production incident that model implies (12). Then the two snapshot problems: the initial snapshot handed to the stream (13), then the incremental snapshot that repairs it online (14). Then what survives in the stream: ordering and transaction boundaries (15), schema evolution (16) and deletes (17). Then the position bookkeeping that makes replay safe (18). ### Shared notation and terms

- **A pipeline's four positions** (topic 1 introduces, reused through 8.2): the **source** (where truth lives), the **buffer** (where bytes wait, owned by one side), the **sink** (the landing table or topic) and the **cursor** (the durable record of how far extraction has got). Every figure names which side owns the buffer and where the cursor is stored.
- **Cursor vocabulary** (topics 3 and 6 introduce): a **high watermark** (the largest cursor value already extracted), a **lookback window** (re-reading a margin behind it), an **idempotent merge** (a write whose repeat leaves the same state), a **deterministic batch id** (a run's identity derived from its input range, so a re-run names the same output).
- **Log vocabulary** (topics 9-10 introduce, reused through 8.2): an **LSN** (a position in PostgreSQL's WAL; Chapter 5 teaches WAL and LSNs), a **replication slot** (the server-side bookmark that pins WAL), `restart_lsn` against `confirmed_flush_lsn`, an **output plugin**, a **publication**, a **before-image** and an **after-image**, a **GTID** (MySQL's position-independent transaction id), a **source offset** (the connector's own bookmark, distinct from a Kafka offset).
- **Snapshot vocabulary** (topics 13-14 introduce): an **initial snapshot** (a consistent copy at a known log position), a **chunk** (a primary-key range read), **watermark events** (the open and close markers written around a chunk), a **READ event** (`op: r`) as Debezium names a snapshot row.
- **Change-event envelope** (topic 19 introduces; topics 15-18 use the field names in prose from the start): `before`, `after`, `source`, `op` (`c`, `u`, `d`, `r`, `t`), `ts_ms`. Written exactly so in figures.
- **Spoken numbers:** narration has no digits, symbols or code names (the automatic check of Chapters 1-5): "high watermark", "log sequence number", "replication slot", "before image" are said as plain words, any number is spoken aloud. Setting names (`max_slot_wal_keep_size`, `binlog_row_image`) stay on screen and in the description, never in the narration.

### 3. Incremental extraction with high-watermark cursors (`de.incremental-extraction-high-watermark`): full, 8.1

- **Folder:** `incremental-extraction-high-watermark`. About 15 min. Hosts the Postgres half of the 8.1 chapter lab.
- **Scope:** "full vs incremental loads; cursor columns (`updated_at`, sequence ids) and why they miss rows (commit order ≠ timestamp order, clock skew, non-monotonic updates, invisible hard deletes); lookback windows plus idempotent merge as the fix; when the answer is log-based CDC instead (8.2)" - the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *full vs incremental:* the cost model: bytes read and written per run against the rows that actually changed, with the break-even change rate measured on `orders`.
  - *cursor columns:* `updated_at` timestamps and sequence ids as the two cursors.
  - *why they miss rows:* four distinct causes, each reproduced. **Commit order is not timestamp order:** PostgreSQL's `now()` and `transaction_timestamp()` return the transaction's *start* time (PostgreSQL 18 date/time functions page), so a transaction that starts at 23:58 and commits at 00:01 stamps its rows 23:58, behind a watermark that moved past it. **Sequence ids are not commit order either:** `nextval` values are handed out at call time and never rolled back, so ids commit out of order and have gaps (PostgreSQL 18 sequence page). **Clock skew** between application servers that write the timestamp themselves. **Non-monotonic updates** (a row's `updated_at` set backwards, or not updated by a bulk `UPDATE` that bypassed the trigger) and **invisible hard deletes** (a deleted row leaves nothing behind for a cursor to find).
  - *lookback windows plus idempotent merge:* re-reading a margin behind the watermark makes the late transaction visible on the next run, and an idempotent merge (keyed on the primary key, `INSERT ... ON CONFLICT` or `MERGE`, Chapter 2 topic 9) makes the re-read rows harmless; the lookback is sized from the longest transaction, with a measured miss rate against lookback length.
  - *when the answer is log-based CDC:* the exact limit of the fix (unbounded transaction length, hard deletes, intermediate states), stated as a decision rule and handed to 8.2.
- **Leaves out:** the full-extract mechanics are topic 2; how to make the merge and the checkpoint idempotent and resumable in general is topic 6; log-based capture is 8.2 (topic 9 builds on this topic's missed-row list); SCD2 and late-arriving dimensions are Chapter 3 topics 13 and 15; Airflow data intervals are Chapter 11.
- **Outcomes:** Reproduce the four ways an `updated_at` cursor misses a row and show the commit-order timeline for each; size a lookback window from the longest transaction and measure what it still misses; combine lookback with an idempotent merge and prove a re-run changes nothing; state precisely when a cursor is the wrong tool.
- **Introduces:** `high-watermark-cursor`, `cursor-miss-modes`, `lookback-idempotent-merge`. **Uses:** `merge-statement`, `upsert-atomicity` (Chapter 2 topic 9), `idempotent-merge-load` (Chapter 3 topic 13), `parallel-range-extraction`, `isolation-anomalies` (Chapter 5 topic 11). **Declared:** none beyond topic 2.
- **Bridge:** "A full extract is safe but redoes all the work every night. The obvious saving is to read only what changed since last time, and this topic is that saving, the four ways its cursor lies, and the lookback-plus-merge repair." (Answers topic 2's handoff.)
- **Handoff:** "A cursor works only when the source is a database you can query. Many sources are not: a vendor's REST API, with its own pagination, limits and failures, is the next shape of the same problem."
- **Prerequisites panel:** Chapter 2 topic 9, Chapter 3 topic 13, Chapter 5 topic 11 (Before this). **Pre-notes terms:** high watermark, `updated_at`, sequence, transaction timestamp, commit order, hard delete, upsert, lookback.

### 10. PostgreSQL logical decoding internals (`de.postgresql-logical-decoding-internals`): full, 8.2

- **Folder:** `postgresql-logical-decoding-internals`. About 15 min. Hosts the failover-slot demonstration of the 8.2 lab as far as one machine allows.
- **Scope:** "WAL, logical replication slots, output plugins (`pgoutput`, decoderbufs), publications, `REPLICA IDENTITY` (DEFAULT vs FULL for before-images), `restart_lsn` vs `confirmed_flush_lsn`; decoding on standbys (PG16) and failover-synchronised slots (PG17) so CDC survives a primary failover" - the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *WAL and logical decoding:* logical decoding turns the WAL's storage-level records into row changes by decoding committed transactions in commit order (PostgreSQL 18 logical-decoding concepts: "extracting all persistent changes to a database's tables into a coherent, easy to understand format"). Chapter 5 topics 3-4 taught the WAL; here the learner reads real decoded changes with `pg_logical_slot_get_changes` and `test_decoding` on the sandbox.
  - *logical replication slots:* a slot is a named stream of changes for one database, persisted independently of any connection and crash-safe; it emits each change once in normal operation, but its position is persisted only at checkpoint, so after a crash the slot can return to an earlier LSN and **the client must tolerate repeats** (the documentation's own words); one receiver at a time. This is the first appearance of the chapter's central at-least-once fact.
  - *output plugins:* `pgoutput` (the standard plugin since PostgreSQL 10, used by PostgreSQL's own logical replication, always installed) and decoderbufs (Protobuf, maintained by the Debezium community, must be installed), and what a plugin decides (the format).
  - *publications:* what a publication selects (tables, and which operations are published); Debezium can create one and, with `publication.autocreate.mode = filtered`, restricts it to the captured tables; `skipped.operations` is propagated into the publication's `publish` option.
  - *`REPLICA IDENTITY`:* DEFAULT carries the old primary-key columns only; FULL carries all old columns; USING INDEX carries the indexed columns; NOTHING carries none; and **a table without a primary key under DEFAULT yields only create events in Debezium** (Debezium's own wording). The before-image is exactly what `REPLICA IDENTITY` allows, shown on one `UPDATE` under each setting.
  - *`restart_lsn` against `confirmed_flush_lsn`:* the oldest WAL the slot may still need against the position up to which the consumer has confirmed receipt, read live from `pg_replication_slots`; the gap between them is what the server must retain (topic 12 uses this).
  - *decoding on standbys (PG16):* PostgreSQL 16 allows logical decoding on a standby (release notes), needing `hot_standby_feedback`, with the caveat that if required catalog rows are removed the slot is invalidated and a physical slot between primary and standby is recommended; slot creation on a standby may wait for primary activity unless `pg_log_standby_snapshot` is called.
  - *failover-synchronised slots (PG17):* the `failover` property on a logical slot plus `sync_replication_slots` on the standby, a physical slot named in `primary_slot_name`, `hot_standby_feedback`, and `synchronized_standby_slots` on the primary so a logical consumer cannot get ahead of the standby; after a promotion, only slots with `synced = true` that had become persistent can resume (all from the PostgreSQL 18 documentation), and Debezium's `slot.failover` property uses it. ### 14. Incremental snapshots with watermark windows (`de.incremental-snapshots-watermark-windows`): full, 8.2

- **Folder:** `incremental-snapshots-watermark-windows`. About 15 min. Hosts the incremental-snapshot leg of the 8.2 chapter lab.
- **Scope:** "DBLog-style chunked snapshots interleaved with the live log: open/close watermarks around each primary-key chunk, drop buffered snapshot rows superseded by streamed changes; signal tables vs Kafka signalling vs read-only (transaction-id watermark) variants; re-snapshotting one table without stopping CDC" - the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *chunked snapshots interleaved with the log:* the algorithm of Andreakis and Papapanagiotou's DBLog paper (Netflix, 2019; arXiv 2010.12597): sort a table by primary key, read it in chunks, and interleave each chunk read with log processing, so log events "continue progress without stalling while processing selects", with no locks and with pause and resume at chunk granularity.
  - *open and close watermarks around each chunk:* write a low-watermark marker, select the chunk, write a high-watermark marker; between the two markers in the log, any streamed change to a key that is also in the chunk **supersedes** the chunk's copy, so the chunk row is dropped from the buffer; the surviving chunk rows are emitted at the high watermark.   - *Debezium's version:* a buffered window per chunk, the default chunk size of 1,024 rows, a READ event (`op: r`) per snapshot row, drop on a primary-key collision with a streamed event, resume after an interruption "at the point where it stopped", and the PostgreSQL limitation that it **does not support schema changes while an incremental snapshot is running** (Debezium's PostgreSQL page).

  - *re-snapshotting one table without stopping CDC:* `data-collections` selects tables, `additional-conditions` selects rows, `stop-snapshot` stops one, and a blocking snapshot is the alternative that pauses streaming; used when a table is added, a topic is deleted or data is corrupted.

### 18. CDC offsets, replay and exactly-once (`de.cdc-offsets-replay-exactly`): full, 8.2

- **Scope:** "two positions (source LSN/GTID vs Kafka offset) and how Kafka Connect stores source offsets; Kafka Connect exactly-once source support (KIP-618) used by Debezium; Debezium has no dedup layer of its own; recovery when the source log position has been purged (re-snapshot)" - the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *two positions:* the **source position** (an LSN, or a GTID or file-and-offset) that says how far the connector has read the database's log, and the **Kafka offset** of the record the connector wrote; they advance at different moments and are stored in different places, and a restart must reconcile them. ### 22. Landing CDC in lakehouse and warehouse (`de.landing-cdc-lakehouse-warehouse`): full, 8.2

- **Folder:** `landing-cdc-lakehouse-warehouse`. About 15 min. Hosts the landing leg of the 8.2 chapter lab.

### 24. Semantic integration: schemas, codes and units (`de.semantic-integration-schemas-codes`): full, 8.3

- **Folder:** `semantic-integration-schemas-codes`. About 15 min. Hosts the deterministic-crosswalk half of the 8.3 lab.
- **Scope:** "schema matching and mapping across heterogeneous sources; reference/lookup data and crosswalk tables (slowly changing); reconciling units, currencies and time zones; owning the mapping as versioned, tested data" - the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *schema matching and mapping:* matching attributes across sources (names, types, instances) and writing the mapping as a transformation; the survey of approaches to automatic schema matching (Rahm and Bernstein, *VLDB Journal* 2001: schema-level against instance-level, element against structure, linguistic against constraint-based) frames why a mapping is mostly human work with tool support; shown for the shop's `orders` against the legacy warehouse system's `ORD_HDR`.
  - *reference and lookup data, crosswalk tables:* a crosswalk maps one system's codes to another's (`MARA.MTART` to a product category; ISO 3166 country codes against a legacy two-letter set); a crosswalk is **slowly changing**, so it carries `valid_from` and `valid_to` (Chapter 3 topic 13's SCD2 shape) and a fact is joined to the crosswalk row in force at the event time.
  - *units, currencies and time zones:* units by a named standard (UCUM, whose specification defines unit strings and conversion), currencies by ISO 4217 codes with the **exchange rate as dated data** and the minor-unit exponent per currency (the integer-cents bug of Chapter 1 topic 12), and time zones by the IANA time zone database (a rule set that changes, so a local timestamp needs its zone and a version of the rules); a UTC instant is stored, the original local value and zone are kept.
  - *owning the mapping as versioned, tested data:* the mapping and crosswalk tables live in version control or a governed table, each with a test (every source code maps to exactly one target; no orphan after a source change), a version, an owner and a change process; a new source code that nobody mapped fails a test instead of falling into `UNKNOWN`.
- **Leaves out:** record linkage and entity resolution (the skipped topics); MDM styles are topic 23; the plant and ISA-95 data model is Chapter 16 topic 1 (named); time-series time handling is Chapter 16 topic 7; data contracts and CI enforcement are Chapter 3 topic 22 and Chapter 11 topic 19; dbt tests as the mechanism are Chapter 3 topic 24 (used).
- **Outcomes:** Map two schemas by name, type and instance evidence and write the mapping as a SQL transformation; build a slowly changing crosswalk and join a fact to the correct version; convert units and currencies with explicit, dated rates and store time as an instant plus a zone; add tests that fail when a source introduces an unmapped code.
- **Introduces:** `semantic-integration-crosswalk`, `unit-currency-timezone-reconciliation`. **Uses:** `scd-type-2`, `backdated-fact-scd2-mapping` (Chapter 3 topic 15), `decimal-precision-scale`, `timestamp-resolution` (Chapter 1 topic 12), `dbt-tests-data-unit` (Chapter 3 topic 24), `mdm-styles`. **Declared:** none beyond topic 23.
- **Bridge:** "Choosing a style decides where the master lives, not what its fields mean. Two systems can agree a customer exists and still disagree on its country code, its currency and the time zone of its timestamps. This topic is how meaning is reconciled and owned." (Answers topic 23's handoff.)
- **Handoff:** "This chapter moved data from where it is produced to where it can be read, row by row and change by change, and made two systems' records agree. The next chapter takes the other kind of source: not a database to be read, but a stream of events that someone is already appending to, and the partitioned log that carries them. - **Prerequisites panel:** Chapter 1 topic 12, Chapter 3 topics 13, 15 and 24, topic 23 (Before this). **Pre-notes terms:** schema mapping, crosswalk, reference data, ISO 4217, UCUM, time zone database, UTC instant.
- **Hook:** the legacy system reports revenue as integer cents and timestamps in local `Europe/Berlin` time, the shop's as decimals and UTC; summed together, one source is off by a factor of a hundred and the other by an hour on the night the clocks change; the figure is two columns of numbers refusing to add.
