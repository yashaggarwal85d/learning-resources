## Red team

- **Is "real-time, accurate" actually ambiguous on *completeness*, or is this topic inventing a cause to fit a predetermined answer?** Re-derived from the six-dimension checklist itself: "real-time" is a freshness/latency word on its face (how new is the data), while "accurate" is most naturally read as a correctness word (does the number match the true state), but neither one, as stated, says anything about whether in-flight/unsettled records should be counted. A streaming system reading the newest available rows will, by construction, include orders that haven't yet reached their final state (cancelled, refunded, still processing) unless told otherwise — that is a **completeness** gap (the fraction of expected final data present at a cutoff), not a latency or even a correctness-within-tolerance gap. The predict question's wrong answer (latency) is tempting precisely because "real-time" sounds like a speed claim; the fix is to show the reveal computing that both systems in fact met their respective speed and correctness-within-tolerance targets, and the only dimension either one left unstated was completeness.
- **Does "correctness as a specification" claim too much by implying any of four definitions is equally valid?** The source only states that correctness is important and usually not SRE's job to define — it does not itself enumerate "four definitions of correct." Those four (exactly-once effect, eventual completeness by T+1, bounded error, at-most-once) are this chapter's own framework (CHAPTER.md, built from Chapter 14's consistency/exactly-once vocabulary and Chapter 5's sketches), not a claim attributed to the SRE book. `OUTLINE.md` and the reel attribute the four-way framework to the chapter's own method, and only the "correctness is a property to define, not assume" framing to the SRE book — checked against step 5/6's narration draft to make sure no line puts words in the SRE book's mouth it didn't say.
- **Is "five or fewer SLI types" a hard rule this topic should state as universal?** The Workbook's own wording is "we recommend" and "small number (five or fewer)" — a recommendation, not a requirement. The post-notes (Production section) states it as a recommendation, not a rule the reel's six-dimension checklist must obey; the checklist itself has six *dimensions* (a fixed taxonomy to check against), which is a different thing from the Workbook's "five or fewer SLI *types* chosen for one critical function" (an authoring discipline). No step conflates the two.
- **Predict 2's trap ("agreeing on a word is enough").** The tempting wrong answer treats "make it reliable" as a vocabulary problem solvable by consensus. The reveal in step 7 shows the five-questions checklist failing at the very first question (no indicator proposed at all) regardless of whether the team agrees what "reliable" *means* in English — agreement on meaning doesn't manufacture a measurement point, a target, a window, or a stated consequence. This is the same error the chapter's rule calls out directly: "correct" (or "reliable") is a specification, not a feeling, and a feeling shared by everyone in the room is still not a specification.
- **Step 10's privacy-vs-availability trap.** "Shows the wrong order" reads to a user like something is broken (tempting toward availability), but availability measures whether the system responds at all, not whether what it returns is the right data for the right principal. The actual failure here is a correctness violation with a security consequence, which this topic's four-definitions framework forces to be stated as closer to an exactly-once-effect-on-access guarantee (the access decision itself must not be allowed to happen more than correctly, ever) rather than a bounded-error one (which tolerates a controlled, small, known rate of wrongness) — a privacy leak is not a case where "mostly right" is acceptable.

## Problem Solving

No implementation problems to report (this topic's `examples.py` is arithmetic/data assertions, not a running system) — the one real correction from this pass was tightening the predict-1 explanation so it doesn't imply the SRE book itself names "completeness" as the cause; it names the six-dimension checklist (this chapter's own tool, built on the SRE book's SLI/SLO vocabulary) as what catches it.
