## The chapter

### The problem it solves

Fourteen chapters taught how data systems work: the engines, the formats, the movement, the platform, the theory. A working data engineer is rarely asked to explain one of those. They are asked, in a meeting, "we want real-time dashboards for the plants", and expected to come back with a system that is correct, affordable and operable, and to defend it against a sceptical peer. The gap between knowing the parts and producing a defensible design is a skill with a method, and the method is learnable: **turn the ask into numbers, price the alternatives, write down what you chose and why, and say what would make you wrong.** The chapter teaches that method first (§15.1), as nine steps that each answer one question every design must answer: what does "correct" mean here, how big is it, what shape is the data flow, where does it live, what computes and serves it, what happens when data is late or replayed, what breaks and how do we recover, what does it cost and what is the one lever, and how do I argue it. It then teaches the part that comes *before* the method and decides how good the answer can be, **asking the right questions of the source and of the consumer** (§15.2). And it runs the method on **fourteen worked problems** (§15.3–§15.4) chosen so that each one forces a different decision to dominate: a billing-grade count that needs a batch reconciliation path, a warehouse where the choice is full versus incremental refresh, a CDC feed where the replication slot is the hazard, a lakehouse with concurrent writers, a five-year backfill that must not hurt production, the same pipeline built two ways, a platform split across regions, a platform shared by tenants, an erasure that must reach backups, a quality monitor that must not cry wolf, a 100-millisecond fraud decision, a feature store whose training and serving values must agree, a global factory telemetry platform, and a streaming anomaly monitor. The learner leaves able to take a vague request and produce, in an hour, a two-page design with an estimate sheet, a failure-mode table and three decision records, and to attack someone else's.

### Order of ideas, and why

1. **§15.1 The design method (topics 1–9).** The method is the chapter, so it opens with it, in the order a design is actually made. First *what must be true*: turn asks into measurable SLOs and define "correct" before choosing anything (1). Then *how big*: capacity arithmetic with the reference numbers to carry (2). Then the four structural choices in dependency order: the **data-flow shape** (batch, micro-batch, streaming, hybrid) driven by the freshness SLO (3), **storage, layout and lifecycle** driven by the dominant access path (4), **processing and serving** driven by state size, latency and query shape (5). Then the cross-cutting concerns that every choice must survive: **correctness under replay and late data** (6), **failure-mode analysis and recovery** (7), **the cost model and its dominant lever** (8). The method ends with the habit that makes the rest defensible, **articulating trade-offs and writing decision records**, with the 45-minute whiteboard pacing (9). Each topic's order is the earlier one's output: SLOs (1) set the estimate (2), the estimate and SLO choose the shape (3), the shape and access path choose the storage (4) and the engines (5), and so on.
2. **§15.2 Requirements interrogation (topics 10–11).** The method's step 1 assumed you had the requirements. These two topics are how you *get* them, from both ends of the pipeline: the **source side** (what arrives, how, how reliably, under what constraints) (10), then the **destination side** (who reads it, how fast, how often, under what access rules) (11). They come after the method, not before, because the interrogation's questions are *the method's questions asked of a person*, so the learner already knows why each one matters.
3. **§15.3 Analytical pipelines and platforms (topics 12–21).** Ten worked problems, in the syllabus's order, each chosen for the decision that dominates it: **clickstream and ad-click analytics** (12: exactly-once-effect streaming with a batch reconciliation path for billing-grade counts), **e-commerce warehouse with incremental loads** (13: dimensional model, merge loads, full versus incremental cost), **CDC from OLTP into a lakehouse** (14: the replication slot and the snapshot-to-stream handover), **concurrent-writer lakehouse with time travel** (15: optimistic commits, conflicts, snapshot expiry versus audit), **resumable multi-TB historical backfill** (16: idempotent chunks and a shadow swap), **Lambda versus Kappa for one pipeline** (17: the same pipeline built both ways), **multi-region analytical platform** (18: residency, egress, regional failover), **multi-tenant analytics with cost attribution** (19: isolation versus cost), **GDPR erasure across lakehouse and backups** (20: the Chapter 13 mechanisms composed into one process), and **data-quality monitoring platform** (21: check execution, blast radius, alert fatigue). The two skipped entries of this section (search indexing, distributed job scheduler) leave no gap in the order.
4. **§15.4 Real-time and ML serving systems (topics 22–25).** Four problems where latency, not throughput, dominates: a **sub-100 ms fraud-scoring pipeline** (22: a latency budget per hop), a **feature platform with point-in-time correctness** (23: one definition for offline and online), a **global manufacturing telemetry platform** (24: edge, MQTT and OPC UA ingestion, a hot TSDB plus lakehouse history, plant autonomy during WAN loss) and **real-time quality and anomaly monitoring** (25: streaming baselines, per-series state at high cardinality). Topics 24 and 25 are Chapter 16's subject matter (industrial and time-series data) applied *before* that chapter teaches its mechanisms; they are planned as **designs that name Chapter 16's mechanisms as forward pointers ("Goes deeper later")** and use only what Chapters 1–14 taught, so that no concept is used before it is introduced. Topic 25 hands off to Chapter 16, which then opens the domain these two designs previewed.

### Shared notation and terms

- **The design record** (topic 1 introduces the pieces; topic 9 the whole): for every design topic the fixed sections are *Requirements* (as SLOs), *Estimate*, *Alternatives* (three or more, each with its cost), *Decision* (an ADR), *Failure modes* (a table), *Cost lever*, *What would change our mind*, *Next bottleneck at 10×*. The reels use these as recurring labels so a learner learns to *read* a design by its sections.
- **SLO vocabulary** (Chapter 11 and Chapter 13 introduce SLIs, SLOs, error budgets and burn rate; topic 1 adds): a **requirement-to-SLO** translation, **"correct" defined before choice** (an exactly-once effect, completeness by T+1, a stated error bound), the six dimensions freshness, latency, completeness, correctness, availability, retention.
- **Estimate vocabulary** (topic 2 introduces): *events per second*, *peak-to-average ratio*, *bytes per day*, *replication and compaction overhead*, *Little's law* (`L = λW`), *reference numbers*.
- **Failure vocabulary** (topic 7 introduces): *blast radius*, *RPO* and *RTO* per data class, *degraded mode*, *poison record*.
- **Decision vocabulary** (topic 9 introduces): an **ADR**, an *alternative*, *what would make us change our mind*, *the next bottleneck at 10×*.
- **Spoken numbers:** narration has no digits, symbols or code names (same automatic check as every earlier chapter): volumes are said in words ("a hundred thousand events a second"), prices and dates in words, a product's setting name stays on screen and in the description, never in the narration.
- **Dates and versions to state, not assume:** every price, quota, limit and product feature a design leans on is re-verified at build against the current primary source and quoted with its version and date; a design that depends on one says so in its "what would change our mind".

### One visual language for the chapter's reels

Built from `site/src/reels/kit.tsx` and `DESIGN.md`'s briefing theme (black and white carry everything; colour only for failure, pale blue only for a second series), with the kit's moves (`Draw`, `Plot`, count, `Decode`, `Reticle`) doing the motion. - **The decision card** (topics 3–5, 9, and the ADR step of every design): three or more alternative cards in a row, each with its one decisive cost; the chosen card lifts and the others dim, with a second line underneath for *what would change our mind*. ### 1. Requirements into SLOs (`de.requirements-into-slos`): full, §15.1

- **Folder:** `requirements-into-slos`. About 15 min.
- **Scope:** "turn functional asks and non-functional constraints (freshness, latency, completeness, correctness, availability, retention) into measurable SLOs; define what 'correct' means for this system (exactly-once effect? eventual completeness by T+1?) before choosing anything" — the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *turn functional asks and non-functional constraints into measurable SLOs:* the translation step: a request in a stakeholder's words ("real-time dashboards", "accurate billing", "we must be able to delete a customer") becomes an SLI measured at a stated point and an SLO with a number, a window and a consequence; the SRE book's distinction between an indicator (what you measure), an objective (the target) and an agreement (the contract with a penalty) is the vocabulary, and Chapters 11 and 13 introduced SLIs, SLOs, error budgets and burn-rate alerting, so this topic does not re-teach them: it teaches **deriving** them from a vague ask, with the five questions that turn an adjective into a number ("real-time" for whom, measured where, at what percentile, over what window, and what happens when it is missed).
  - *the six non-functional dimensions (freshness, latency, completeness, correctness, availability, retention):* a short, fixed checklist applied to every ask; each gets a measurement definition for a data system (freshness is the age of the newest data a consumer can read; completeness is the fraction of expected data present at a cut-off; correctness is agreement with a defined truth within a stated tolerance; retention is how long data must and may be kept, with Chapter 13's erasure as its upper bound), so a requirement is not "done" until each dimension has a value or an explicit "not needed".
  - *define what 'correct' means for this system before choosing anything:* the step designers skip: **"correct" is a specification, not a feeling**, and the choices are different in kind: *exactly-once effect* (Chapter 14 topic 13: at-least-once delivery plus idempotent writes), *eventual completeness by T+1* (late data accepted until a cut-off, then a correction window), *bounded error* (an approximate answer within a stated bound, as Chapter 5's sketches), *at-most-once* (loss acceptable). The topic works one ask (the shop's "daily revenue dashboard") through all four definitions and shows the four systems they produce, which is the reason the step comes first.
- **Leaves out:** defining SLIs, SLOs, error budgets and burn-rate alerting themselves (Chapter 11 §11.3, Chapter 13 topic 3: built on, referred to by name); the capacity arithmetic the SLOs feed (topic 2); choosing the data-flow shape the freshness SLO drives (topic 3); the source and destination questions that *gather* the requirements (topics 10–11); the exactly-once mechanisms (Chapter 14 topic 13 and Chapter 9 §9.2: named as the cost of the stricter definition, not re-taught).
- **Outcomes:** Turn three vague asks into SLOs with an indicator, a measurement point, a target, a window and a consequence; apply the six-dimension checklist and mark each dimension valued or explicitly not needed; for one ask, write four definitions of "correct" and say how each changes the design; reject a requirement that cannot be measured.
- **Introduces:** `ask-to-slo-translation`, `six-dimension-requirement-checklist`, `correctness-as-specification`. **Uses:** `error-budget-burn-rate`, `schedule-aware-freshness-alert`, `actionable-page-test` (Chapter 13: the alerting an SLO implies), `end-to-end-argument`, `at-least-once-plus-idempotence` (Chapter 14: the exactly-once effect), `cap-theorem-formal-statement` and `consistency-model-map` (Chapter 14: what consistency an SLO can ask for), `latency-percentiles` (Chapter 1: a percentile target). Builds on Chapter 11 §11.3 "Data SLIs, SLOs and error budgets": look up its `introduces` id in `registry.ts` for `uses`. **Declared:** none.
- **Bridge:** "The theory is now complete enough to use: a model to reason in, an order without clocks, a ladder of consistency models, one impossibility result, and the mechanisms that buy what the result allows. This chapter does not add theory. It applies it: given a vague request for a data system, how do you turn it into measurable requirements first, so that every later choice can be defended. This first topic is that step: requirements into service-level objectives." (Answers Chapter 14's final handoff, quoted from it.)
- **Handoff:** "Now there is a list of numbers the system must meet. The next question is how big the thing is: how many events a second, how many bytes a year, and what that does to storage, network and cost. That is capacity estimation, and it is the arithmetic every later choice rests on."
- **Prerequisites panel:** Chapter 11's SLO topic and Chapter 13's burn-rate topic (Before this); Chapter 14's exactly-once topic (Before this). **Pre-notes terms:** requirement, SLI, SLO, percentile, freshness, completeness, correctness, retention, tolerance.

### 2. Back-of-envelope capacity estimation (`de.back-envelope-capacity-estimation`): full, §15.1

- **Folder:** `back-envelope-capacity-estimation`. About 15 min. Defines the shop constants every later estimate reuses.
- **Scope:** "events/s, bytes/day, storage/year with replication and compaction overhead, peak-to-average ratio, Little's law for in-flight work; the reference numbers to carry in your head (memory/SSD/network latencies, per-partition Kafka throughput, object-store request costs and limits, warehouse scan rates)" — the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *events/s, bytes/day, storage/year with replication and compaction overhead:* the chain of multiplications that turns a business volume into an engineering one: orders per day times events per order gives events per day, divided by 86,400 gives the average rate, times bytes per event gives bandwidth and daily volume, times 365 and a retention class gives yearly storage, times the replication factor (and a compaction or write-amplification overhead where the store has one, Chapter 5 topic 9's amplification) gives the disk to buy; every step shown as a row in the estimate sheet with its unit.
  - *peak-to-average ratio:* systems are sized for the peak, not the mean; the ratio (and its source, a daily curve or a campaign spike) turns the average rate into the design rate; an estimate that omits it is off by a small multiple that is the difference between coping and falling over.
  - *Little's law for in-flight work:* the average number in a system equals the arrival rate times the average time each spends in it (`L = λW`), so a pipeline with a hundred thousand events a second and a two-second processing time holds two hundred thousand events in flight, which sizes buffers, consumer counts and memory; the topic uses it for three things (consumers needed, queue depth, connection pool, Chapter 4's pool sizing) and states the stability condition (arrival rate below service rate).

### 25. Real-time quality and anomaly monitoring (`de.real-time-quality-anomaly`): full, §15.4

- **Folder:** `real-time-quality-anomaly`. About 15 min. **A forward-looking design** that closes the chapter and hands off to Chapter 16.
- **Scope:** "near-real-time line-quality dashboard plus streaming anomaly detection: streaming baselines (EWMA, seasonal decomposition, SPC rules), per-series state at high cardinality, alert deduplication and suppression, backtesting on history (Ch.16)" — the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *near-real-time line-quality dashboard plus streaming anomaly detection:* the design adds, on top of topic 24's platform, two consumers with different needs: a **dashboard** of line quality (aggregates over windows, tolerant of seconds to a minute, topic 5's pre-aggregation) and a **detector** that flags a measurement drifting out of spec as it arrives (low latency, per-series, correctness of the *flag* mattering more than the exactness of any number); the requirements interview questions of topic 11 ("what do you do when you see it": stop the line, call maintenance) set the latency and the cost of a false alarm versus a missed one.
  - *streaming baselines (EWMA, seasonal decomposition, SPC rules):* the three families of baseline: an **exponentially weighted moving average** (a running estimate that discounts old data, NIST's EWMA control-chart documentation gives the statistic), **seasonal decomposition** (a baseline that accounts for shift, day and week patterns so a normal Monday-morning ramp is not an alarm), and **statistical process control rules** (control limits and run rules from Shewhart charts, with the NIST handbook as the reference); each is **computable incrementally with O(1) state per series**, which is why they suit streaming, and the topic states each one's failure (an EWMA adapts to a slow drift that *is* the fault; a seasonal model needs history it does not have for a new machine; a fixed control limit assumes stationarity).
  - *per-series state at high cardinality:* 200,000 series (topic 24's count) each need a baseline state (a few numbers for EWMA, a seasonal profile for the decomposition): sized by topic 2's arithmetic, kept in the stream engine's keyed state (Chapter 9 §9.3 and Chapter 7 §7.4's RocksDB state), with the **churn** (a new series appears when a machine is added, an old one retires) handled by state TTL and a **cold-start policy** (no alarms until a series has enough history, or a borrowed baseline from a similar machine), and the memory cost of the per-series seasonal profile against a coarser model priced.
  - *alert deduplication and suppression:* a detector that fires per series per interval floods the operators: **deduplicate** repeated firings of one condition into one alert with a duration, **group** alerts from series that share a cause (one line, one machine, using the asset hierarchy of topic 24 as the grouping key, as topic 21 uses lineage), **suppress** during planned changeovers and maintenance (the schedule-aware idea of Chapter 13 topic 3), and measure the detector by **alert precision and the share acted on** (topic 21), because an unusable detector is worse than none.
  - *backtesting on history (Ch.16):* before a detector goes live, **replay it over history** (topic 6's replay as a first-class path, the lakehouse of topic 24 as the source) and score it against **labelled past incidents** (what it would have flagged, how early, how many false alarms), tuning thresholds on one period and testing on a later one (the same time-split discipline as topic 23's leakage); the backtest's cost (a scan of years) and its **limitation** (history under-represents rare faults) are stated, and the labelling effort is named as the real cost.
- **Leaves out:** statistical process control and quality data modelling, OEE and downtime events, sensor-data quality, time-series storage and compression, and the industrial data model (**Chapter 16**: forward pointers, none used as a concept before it is introduced there); machine-learned anomaly models beyond naming; the telemetry platform it runs on (topic 24); the monitoring-platform design for data-quality checks (topic 21, whose alert-precision and grouping ideas are reused); alert-routing mechanics (Chapter 13 topic 3).
