## The chapter

### The problem it solves

Every earlier chapter used a distributed-systems word as if it were settled: a *timestamp*, a *lease*, a *quorum*, *strongly consistent*, *exactly-once*, *the leader*. Production incidents live in the gap between what those words were assumed to mean and what the system actually guarantees. A "last write wins by timestamp" store silently drops a write because two machines disagree about the time; a lock service grants a lease and a paused client keeps writing after it expired; a "linearizable" read comes from a deposed leader; a "serializable" database returns a stale read to a user who just wrote; "exactly-once" is promised end to end and a payment is taken twice. This chapter replaces each word with a definition precise enough to *test*, and with the one result that explains why the obvious fix does not work. It starts from the **model**: what a protocol may assume about timing and failure, because every correctness claim is relative to one (§14.1). It then removes the one thing a programmer wrongly trusts, a shared clock, and builds **order** from messages alone: happens-before, scalar and vector clocks (§14.2). With order in hand it states the **consistency models** exactly, from linearizability down to causal consistency, and the transactional pair, serializable and strict serializable, with the map between them (§14.3). It states what no system can have, **CAP** as a theorem with its three formal conditions, and PACELC's everyday trade (§14.4). It then gives the **mechanisms** that buy the strong models under that limit: Raft's safety argument, leases with fencing tokens to survive a zombie leader, the coordination services and replicated logs that real data systems lean on (§14.6), 2PC and the blocking problem that consensus-replicated participants cure (§14.7), and finally why "exactly-once" is an *effect* assembled from at-least-once delivery and idempotence, by the end-to-end argument (§14.8). The learner leaves able to read a system's documentation and say *which model it assumes, which consistency it promises, what it costs, and exactly what breaks when the assumption fails*, and to refuse a design that rests on a word without its definition.

### Order of ideas, and why

1. **§14.1 System models (topic 1).** Everything after it is a statement of the form "protocol P guarantees X *in model M*", so the models come first: synchronous, partially synchronous and asynchronous timing; crash-stop, crash-recovery, omission and Byzantine faults; fair-loss and reliable links (1).
2. **§14.2 Time, clocks and ordering (topics 2–4).** The model has no shared clock to lean on, so the chapter next shows what physical clocks really give (2), then builds the order that does not depend on them: Lamport's happens-before and scalar clocks, which give a total order consistent with causality but cannot see concurrency (3), then vector clocks and version vectors, which capture causality exactly and detect concurrency at a metadata cost (4). The two skipped topics of this section (hybrid logical clocks, TrueTime and commit wait) are the engineering answers to topic 2's problem; they are named in topic 2's Deep dive only.
3. **§14.3 Consistency models (topics 5–7).** Order is now available, so the models can be stated: linearizability (5), then the weaker single-object models sequential and causal (6), then the transactional pair serializable and strict serializable, ending with the two-axis map of all of them (7). The skipped Adya-dependency-cycle and history-checking topics sit after 7; both are named in topic 7's Deep dive.
4. **§14.4 Impossibility and the trade-off space (topic 8).** With the strong models defined, the one result that bounds them: CAP stated precisely, and PACELC (8). The skipped FLP topic is named in topic 9's Deep dive, where it matters (Raft's randomised timeouts).
5. **§14.6 Consensus and coordination (topics 9–11).** The mechanisms that deliver the models the impossibility result permits: Raft at the conceptual level, its safety invariants (9); leases, fencing tokens and split brain, the guard against a deposed leader the safety argument itself does not give you (10); coordination services and replicated logs in data systems, where Kafka's ISR and table-format commits are placed in the quorum theory (11). The three skipped topics before 9 (the consensus problem and quorum systems, quorum intersection is not linearizability, Paxos) are named in topic 9's Deep dive; §14.5 is skipped entirely.
6. **§14.7–§14.8 Atomic commitment and convergence (topics 12–13).** Consensus decides one value among replicas; a transaction across *several* shards must make every shard decide the same *outcome*, which is 2PC, its blocking problem and the consensus-replicated answer (12). The last topic leaves coordination behind for the question every pipeline asks: "exactly-once" as an effect built from at-least-once and idempotence, by the end-to-end argument (13). It hands off to Chapter 15, which *applies* all of this under explicit requirements. §14.9 (verification) is skipped entirely.

### 1. Timing and failure models (`de.timing-failure-models`): full, §14.1

- **Folder:** `timing-failure-models`. About 15 min. Hosts the §14.1 chapter lab.
- **Scope:** "synchronous vs partially synchronous (Dwork–Lynch–Stockmeyer GST) vs asynchronous networks; crash-stop, crash-recovery, omission and Byzantine faults; fair-loss vs reliable links. Every protocol's correctness claim is relative to one model, and production incidents start where the real system leaves that model" — the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *synchronous vs partially synchronous (Dwork–Lynch–Stockmeyer GST) vs asynchronous networks:* a synchronous system has known upper bounds on message delay and relative processor speed; an asynchronous one has none; Dwork, Lynch and Stockmeyer's partial synchrony sits between, in the version where the bounds exist but are unknown, or hold only after an unknown global stabilisation time (GST) ("the message system respects the upper bound Δ from time GST onward", in the paper's words); a protocol that is *safe* always and *live* only after GST is the pattern Raft follows (topic 9).
  - *crash-stop, crash-recovery, omission and Byzantine faults:* the fault classes the DLS paper itself lists (processors that crash or "fail-stop", that "fail to send or receive messages when they should" (omission), or that "send erroneous messages" (Byzantine)), plus crash-recovery (a process that stops and later restarts, possibly having lost volatile state or, in the worst case, its disk), and what each model lets a protocol assume; the model hierarchy (Byzantine includes omission includes crash).
  - *fair-loss vs reliable links:* a fair-loss link may drop messages but a message sent infinitely often is eventually delivered; a reliable link delivers every message exactly as sent between correct processes; retransmission and deduplication turn the first into the second, which is why every "reliable" layer is built on retries and idempotence (the idea topic 13 closes on).
  - *every protocol's correctness claim is relative to one model, and production incidents start where the real system leaves that model:* the chapter's thesis, made concrete by the lab: a protocol correct under crash-stop that breaks under crash-recovery with a lost disk; a protocol correct on a reliable network that breaks under reordering; a "timeout means crashed" inference that is wrong in an asynchronous system.
- **Leaves out:** how events are ordered without a shared clock (topics 2–4); consensus and its impossibility in the fully asynchronous model (FLP, a *(skipped)* syllabus topic: named in topic 9's Deep dive); the **Two Generals** problem and common knowledge (a *(skipped)* syllabus topic: named once here, in the Deep dive, as the reason a reliable link over a lossy one never gives certainty of delivery, with the source, and not taught); failure *detectors* and gossip (the *(skipped)* §14.5); the Byzantine fault tolerance protocols themselves (outside this course's scope: Byzantine is named as the strongest fault class and as the reason blockchains need more than a majority).
- **Outcomes:** State each timing model and say what it lets a protocol assume; state each fault class and its place in the hierarchy; say what a fair-loss link must be wrapped in to behave as a reliable one; take a primary-backup register through message loss, reordering and a crash-recovery with a lost disk in the simulator and name the first invariant each breaks; rewrite one real incident description in terms of the model assumption that failed.
- **Introduces:** `timing-models-sync-partial-async`, `fault-models-crash-omission-byzantine`, `link-models-fair-loss-reliable`, `safety-vs-liveness-in-a-model`. **Uses:** `bandwidth-delay-product` and `throughput-vs-latency` (Chapter 1: a delay bound is a network fact), `signals` and `exit-status` (Chapter 1: a crash as the process an OS kills), `fsync` and `fsync-failure` (Chapter 1: what "lost disk" and a failed write mean), `replication-ack-modes` (Chapter 5: the primary-backup protocol the lab runs), `wal-lsn-fsync` (Chapter 5: what survives a crash-recovery). **Declared:** none.

### 2. Physical clocks and why they lie (`de.physical-clocks-why-they`): full, §14.2

- **Folder:** `physical-clocks-why-they`. About 15 min. Hosts part of the §14.2 chapter lab.
- **Scope:** "quartz drift, NTP synchronization error, leap-second smearing, VM pauses, wall-clock vs monotonic clocks; PTP and bounded-error clock services (e.g. AWS ClockBound). Why 'last write wins by timestamp' silently drops writes, and why lease-based protocols must budget for maximum skew" — the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *quartz drift:* a crystal oscillator runs at a frequency that varies with temperature and age, so two machines' clocks diverge continuously at a rate of parts per million; the arithmetic (a drift of 200 parts per million is 17.3 seconds a day, computed in `examples.py`; Spanner's paper applies a worst-case drift rate of 200 microseconds per second in its uncertainty bound, which is the same 200 parts per million, quoted from the paper) shows why clocks must be corrected continually.
  - *NTP synchronization error:* NTP estimates a clock's offset from the round trip to a server and corrects it, with an error bounded by the path's asymmetry and delay variation (the NTP documentation: "the shorter and more symmetric the round-trip time, the more accurate the estimate"); a time step or slew corrects the clock, so a wall clock can *jump*, including backwards.
  - *leap-second smearing:* a leap second is an irregular extra second, and the choices are to step the clock (an abrupt jump) or to spread it out; Google's page says it "uses a 'leap smear' to handle leap seconds, gradually adjusting time over 24 hours instead of abrupt clock steps", recommends a 24-hour linear smear from noon to noon UTC, and says "smeared time briefly deviates from UTC during the smear period but realigns after 24 hours", so a clock that smears and one that steps disagree during the window, which the builder demonstrates with the simulator (the size of the disagreement follows from the 24-hour linear smear and is computed, not quoted); Spanner's paper lists incorrect leap-second handling among GPS-source failure modes.
  - *VM pauses:* a process can be descheduled, stop-the-world garbage collected, or its VM live-migrated, for an unbounded time between *reading* the clock and *using* the reading, so even a perfectly synchronised clock does not make "now" safe to act on (Kleppmann's lock article's pause examples).
  - *wall-clock vs monotonic clocks:* the wall clock (Linux `CLOCK_REALTIME`) is settable and "affected by discontinuous jumps"; the monotonic clock (`CLOCK_MONOTONIC`) is nonsettable and only moves forward, so durations are measured with it and *instants shared between machines* cannot be: the monotonic clock has no meaning on another host.
  - *PTP and bounded-error clock services (e.g. AWS ClockBound):* the Precision Time Protocol with hardware timestamping tightens synchronisation; a bounded-error service returns an *interval* (earliest, latest) rather than a point (ClockBound's repository describes bounded timestamps and a daemon that keeps the clock synchronised from a PTP hardware clock); named from each project's own documentation and disclosed as sourced-only, since the sandbox has no such hardware.
  - *why 'last write wins by timestamp' silently drops writes:* with two writers whose clocks differ by more than the gap between their writes, the *later* write carries the *smaller* timestamp and is discarded; Cassandra's documentation states it "uses a simpler last-write-wins model where every mutation is timestamped", and Dynamo's paper names timestamp-based "last write wins" as a reconciliation choice, so the loss is the documented design, not a bug; shown on the shop's stock register with a 50 ms skew between two nodes.
  - *why lease-based protocols must budget for maximum skew:* a lease says "I am the leader until time T", which is only safe if the holder's clock and the granter's clock agree to within a *known maximum drift*, which the lease length must exceed by that bound; the consequence is topic 10's subject, the guard against a leader that outlives its lease.
- **Leaves out:** hybrid logical clocks (the *(skipped)* syllabus topic: Kulkarni et al.'s technical report is named in the Deep dive; the HLC lab leg is not built here); TrueTime and commit wait (the *(skipped)* syllabus topic: Spanner's TrueTime is cited here *only* as the one production example of a bounded-error clock, and its commit-wait protocol is not taught; Chapter 5's distributed-SQL topic named Spanner at the pattern level); logical clocks (topics 3–4); leases and fencing themselves (topic 10).

### 3. Lamport clocks and happens-before (`de.lamport-clocks-happens-before`): full, §14.2

- **Folder:** `lamport-clocks-happens-before`. About 15 min. Hosts part of the §14.2 chapter lab.
- **Scope:** "the happens-before partial order, scalar logical clocks and the clock condition; a scalar clock yields a total order consistent with causality but cannot tell you whether two events were concurrent" — the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *the happens-before partial order:* Lamport's definition: `a → b` if `a` and `b` are in the same process and `a` comes first, or `a` is the sending of a message and `b` its receipt, or there is a chain of such steps (transitivity); two events with no such chain are **concurrent** (`a ∥ b`), and "happened before" "is therefore only a partial ordering" (the paper's words); the picture is the chapter's space-time diagram.
  - *scalar logical clocks and the clock condition:* each process keeps a counter, increments it between events, stamps each message with it, and on receipt sets its own to one more than the maximum of its own and the stamp; this guarantees the **clock condition**: `a → b ⇒ C(a) < C(b)`.
  - *a scalar clock yields a total order consistent with causality:* break ties between equal timestamps by process identifier ("any arbitrary total ordering of the processes", per the paper) to get a total order that never contradicts causality, which is what makes it usable for ordering requests (Lamport's mutual-exclusion example, named here and not built).
  - *but cannot tell you whether two events were concurrent:* the converse fails: `C(a) < C(b)` does **not** imply `a → b`, so two concurrent events get ordered arbitrarily, and a system that treats the order as causality sees a dependency that is not there; this is topic 4's reason to exist. Lamport's paper also notes that anomalous behaviour can occur if the order differs from what users observe externally and shows how real clocks fix it, which connects to topic 2.
- **Leaves out:** vector clocks and concurrency detection (topic 4); physical clocks (topic 2); hybrid logical clocks, which fuse the two ideas (the *(skipped)* syllabus topic); distributed mutual exclusion and replicated state machines built on the total order (named only; the replicated log is topic 9, and the state-machine idea is Chapter 5's consensus topic).

### 4. Vector clocks and version vectors (`de.vector-clocks-version-vectors`): full, §14.2

- **Folder:** `vector-clocks-version-vectors`. About 15 min. Hosts part of the §14.2 chapter lab.
- **Scope:** "per-process counters that capture causality exactly and detect concurrency; version vectors (per replica) vs vector clocks (per process), dotted version vectors, sibling explosion and metadata growth; how Dynamo-style stores surface conflicts to the application" — the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *per-process counters that capture causality exactly and detect concurrency:* each process keeps a vector with one counter per process, increments its own entry on each event, merges by element-wise maximum on receipt; `V(a) < V(b)` (every entry ≤ and one <) **if and only if** `a → b`, and two vectors that are not comparable mean the events are concurrent, which is exactly what a scalar clock cannot say.
  - *version vectors (per replica) vs vector clocks (per process):* a vector clock tracks *events at processes*; a version vector tracks the *versions of one data item at replicas* (an entry per replica that has coordinated a write), the practical form stores use, with the same comparison rule.
  - *dotted version vectors:* the problem of falsely merging concurrent writes coordinated by the same replica, and the dot (a single event identifier) that distinguishes them, from the Dotted Version Vectors paper that Riak's documentation names as the second form of its causal context.
  - *sibling explosion and metadata growth:* concurrent writes that cannot be ordered are kept as **siblings** for the application to merge (Riak: with `allow_mult`, concurrent writes "create siblings"), and a client that never merges makes them multiply; the vector itself grows with the number of coordinators, which is why Dynamo **truncates** the clock by dropping the oldest pair beyond a threshold ("say 10", per the paper), at the cost of possibly treating ordered versions as concurrent.
  - *how Dynamo-style stores surface conflicts to the application:* the Dynamo paper's design: `get` returns all causally unrelated versions with their context, the application reconciles (the shopping-cart merge), and `put` carries the context back so the store knows what the write supersedes.

### 5. Linearizability (`de.linearizability`): full, §14.3

- **Folder:** `linearizability`. About 15 min. Hosts part of the §14.3 chapter lab.
- **Scope:** "single-object, real-time-respecting atomicity (Herlihy & Wing, 1990); locality/composability; why it costs at least a round-trip per operation (Attiya–Welch lower bound) and why 'read from the leader' is not linearizable without a lease or quorum read" — the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *single-object, real-time-respecting atomicity (Herlihy & Wing, 1990):* every operation appears to take effect instantaneously at some point between its invocation and its response ("the illusion that each operation... takes effect instantaneously at some point between its invocation and its response", in etcd's quoting of the original), in an order consistent with real time: if A completes before B begins, B must appear after A; defined over a *history* of invocations and responses, and the same notion Gilbert and Lynch call atomic consistency in CAP (topic 8).
  - *locality/composability:* linearizability is a **local** property (Herlihy and Wing): if each object's operations are linearizable, the whole system is, so objects can be reasoned about and implemented independently, and it is also a **nonblocking** property (a pending operation never forces others to wait in the definition).
  - *why it costs at least a round-trip per operation (Attiya–Welch lower bound):* Attiya and Welch compare linearizability with sequential consistency by the worst-case time of a data access and conclude that "under realistic timing assumptions it is strictly more expensive to provide linearizability than sequential consistency in distributed (message-passing) implementations"; their results are bounds in terms of the maximum message delay `d` and the clock/delay uncertainty `u` (for example, for sequential consistency the sum of the worst-case read and write times is at least `d`). The syllabus's one-line "at least a round trip" is a simplification of these bounds: the builder states the paper's exact theorems for the model it uses, not the simplified phrase, and says so.
  - *why 'read from the leader' is not linearizable without a lease or quorum read:* a leader that has been deposed and does not know it can answer a read with a stale value after a new leader has accepted a later write (the Raft paper: "the leader responding to the request might have been superseded by a newer leader of which it is unaware"), which violates the real-time rule; fixed by confirming leadership with a quorum before answering or by a lease that bounds the deposed leader's life (topic 10), and etcd's documented guarantee that its default reads are linearizable while it offers serializable reads that "may access stale data".

### 7. Serializability vs strict serializability (`de.serializability-vs-strict-serializability`): full, §14.3

- **Folder:** `serializability-vs-strict-serializability`. About 15 min. Hosts part of the §14.3 chapter lab.

### 8. CAP, stated precisely, and PACELC (`de.cap-stated-precisely-pacelc`): full, §14.4

- **Folder:** `cap-stated-precisely-pacelc`. About 15 min. Hosts the §14.4 chapter lab.
- **Scope:** "Gilbert–Lynch's formal statement (linearizability + availability of every non-failed node + arbitrary message loss) and proof sketch; the common misreadings ('pick two', treating CP/AP as a system label, ignoring latency); PACELC's else-branch latency/consistency trade-off (§5.3 gives the system classification); why Spanner is 'effectively CA' only because partitions are rare, not impossible" — the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *Gilbert–Lynch's formal statement:* the theorem as Gilbert and Lynch prove it: in an asynchronous network where messages may be lost, **no web service can implement an atomic read/write register that guarantees a response to every request** ("subject to communication failures, it is impossible for any web service to implement an atomic read/write shared memory that guarantees a response to every request"). The three conditions are precise: **atomic** (consistency) is linearizability of topic 5 (the paper: "for every operation, there is a single instant in between the request and the response at which the operation appears to occur"); **available** means "every request received by a non-failing node in the system must result in a response" (and the later survey describes availability as a liveness property: "eventually, every request receives a response"); **partition tolerance** means the network may lose arbitrarily many messages (the theorem is stated over "fair executions (including those in which messages are lost)").
  - *proof sketch:* split the nodes into two groups with all messages between them lost; a write to one group and a later read at the other cannot be told apart from a run in which nobody wrote, so the second group must either answer (and may violate atomicity) or not answer (violating availability); the argument is a picture on the chapter's space-time diagram.
  - *the common misreadings ('pick two', CP/AP as a system label, ignoring latency):* Gilbert and Lynch's own later perspective, and Brewer's "CAP Twelve Years Later", say the "2 of 3" formulation "was always misleading because it tended to oversimplify the tensions among properties"; partitions are not a property one gives up (a partition happens or not), so the real choice is consistency versus availability *while a partition lasts*; a system's behaviour differs by operation and is not one letter; and the theorem says nothing about latency, which is PACELC's contribution.
  - *PACELC's else-branch latency/consistency trade-off:* Abadi's formulation: if there is a partition (P), choose availability or consistency (A/C); *else* (E), even with no partition, choose latency or consistency (L/C), because replicating before answering costs time; it "unifies this tradeoff between consistency and latency"; Chapter 5 topic 20 classified real systems (PA/EL, PC/EC); this topic does not repeat the classification and defines the quadrants exactly.
  - *why Spanner is 'effectively CA' only because partitions are rare, not impossible:* Eric Brewer's own post on Spanner and CAP says Spanner is, "technically", not a CA system, "but 'yes' in effect and its users can and do assume CA" because Google's private network makes partitions rare and the system's design reduces their impact; the honest reading is that it is a CP system with a very high availability in practice, and that "effectively" is a statement about probability, not about the theorem.

### 9. Raft at the conceptual level (`de.raft-at-conceptual-level`): full, §14.6

- **Scope:** "terms, leader election with the up-to-date-log voting restriction, log matching property, commitment only for current-term entries, membership change (joint consensus / single-server changes), snapshots; what Raft adds over Multi-Paxos is understandability, not new power.   - *terms:* time is divided into terms numbered by increasing integers; "terms act as a logical clock in Raft, and they allow servers to detect obsolete information such as stale leaders" (the paper); a server that sees a higher term updates its own and steps down; a request with a stale term is rejected; this is the same idea as a fencing token (topic 10) and the same job as a Lamport clock (topic 3).
  - *leader election with the up-to-date-log voting restriction:* a server that stops hearing from a leader becomes a candidate, increments its term and requests votes; each server votes once per term; a vote is granted only if the candidate's log is "at least as up-to-date" as the voter's (compare last term, then last index); randomised election timeouts break split votes. **Election Safety**: at most one leader per term; the **voting restriction** gives **Leader Completeness**: any elected leader contains every committed entry.
  - *log matching property:* if two logs contain an entry with the same index and term, the logs are identical up to that index; maintained by the consistency check in `AppendEntries` (the previous entry's index and term must match), with the leader repairing a follower's divergent suffix by backing up to the last point of agreement (the repair's message format and edge cases are the Rust chapter's).

### 10. Leases, fencing tokens and split brain (`de.leases-fencing-tokens-split`): full, §14.6

- **Folder:** `leases-fencing-tokens-split`. About 15 min. Hosts the first part of the §14.6 chapter lab.

  - *fencing tokens / epoch numbers checked by the storage layer:* the fix is a number that **increases every time the lock is granted**, sent with every write, and **checked by the resource being protected**, which rejects any write with a token lower than one it has already seen (Kleppmann's diagram: client 1 gets token 33, pauses, client 2 gets 34 and writes, then client 1's write with 33 is rejected); the production forms: Chubby's **sequencer** (an opaque byte-string with "the lock generation number" that the lock holder passes to servers), Kafka's **producer epoch** for transactional producers (`ProducerFencedException` when another producer with the same transactional id has a newer epoch, in the producer API), ZooKeeper's **zxid** (a total order stamp on every change) as a natural token, and Raft's **term** (topic 9), all the same idea.
  - *why a distributed lock without fencing is not safe:* a lock is only a *hint* about who should be acting; mutual exclusion that depends on every participant's timing is not safe in the asynchronous or partially synchronous model of topic 1; safety must be enforced by the resource, which is why a lock service plus fencing tokens is correct and a lock service alone is not; Chubby additionally offers a **lock-delay** (the lock stays unavailable for a period after a holder fails without releasing it) as a defence for the cases where the resource cannot check a token ("While imperfect, the lock-delay protects unmodified servers").

### 12. Two-phase commit and its blocking problem (`de.two-phase-commit-blocking`): full, §14.7

- **Folder:** `two-phase-commit-blocking`. About 15 min. Hosts the §14.7 chapter lab.
- **Scope:** "prepare/commit, the coordinator as single point of blocking (participants in doubt hold locks), 3PC and why it fails under partitions; Paxos Commit (Gray & Lamport, 2006) / 2PC over consensus-replicated participants as the production answer (Spanner, CockroachDB parallel commits). Sagas and outbox patterns at the application level: §5.2, §8.2" — the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *prepare/commit:* the coordinator asks every participant to **prepare** (make the transaction durable and promise it can commit); if all vote yes it records the decision **commit** and tells everyone, otherwise **abort**; the participants' prepare votes and the coordinator's decision are logged durably (Chapter 5 topic 16 showed this in a real database and named the in-doubt state; this topic makes it exact and proves what it can and cannot do).
  - *the coordinator as single point of blocking (participants in doubt hold locks):* a participant that has voted yes is **in doubt**: it cannot unilaterally commit or abort because the decision may be either, so it must hold its locks until it hears the outcome; if the coordinator crashes after the prepare phase and before announcing, every prepared participant blocks, holding locks, until the coordinator recovers: the blocking problem, measured in the lab as lock-hold time.
  - *3PC and why it fails under partitions:* three-phase commit adds a pre-commit phase so that participants can finish without waiting for a crashed coordinator (Skeen's nonblocking commit protocols); the catch is the model it needs: it relies on timeouts to decide that a node has failed, which is only right when the network is not partitioned and delays are bounded (topic 1's synchronous end of the ladder), and Gray and Lamport state the consequence for the traditional protocols in so many words: Paxos Commit differs from them because in "traditional Three-Phase commit protocols... multiple coordinators can lead to inconsistency". The topic states Skeen's protocol, the timeout-as-crash assumption and Gray and Lamport's sentence, and it **does not claim more than they do**: the builder reads Skeen's and Gray and Lamport's exact statements before writing the claim that 3PC is unsafe under partition, and if the sources do not say it in those words, the topic says "relies on the assumption" and not "fails".
  - *Paxos Commit (Gray & Lamport, 2006) / 2PC over consensus-replicated participants as the production answer:* Paxos Commit runs a consensus instance for each participant's prepare vote, so the decision survives the loss of the coordinator, "makes progress if at least F+1 of [2F+1 acceptors] are working", has "the same message delay in the fault-free case as Two-Phase Commit" with more messages, and "the classic Two-Phase Commit algorithm is obtained as the special F = 0 case"; the production form is **2PC where each participant, and the coordinator's state, are themselves consensus-replicated groups**: Spanner stores each transaction manager's state "in the underlying Paxos group", and CockroachDB's Parallel Commits "promises to halve the latency of distributed transactions by performing all consensus round trips required to commit a transaction" in parallel (its engineering post, with the version stated); Chapter 5 topic 24 named these as the distributed-SQL pattern.
  - *sagas and outbox patterns at the application level (§5.2, §8.2):* named only, as the application-level alternative that gives up atomicity for compensation and eventual consistency; the transactional outbox's CDC half is Chapter 8's and the saga's compensation Chapter 5's pointer, not retaught.
- **Leaves out:** Chapter 5 topic 16's database-level 2PC and XA (built on); the Raft mechanism used to replicate the coordinator's decision (topic 9, reused); the *(skipped)* syllabus topic "Transaction architectures beyond 2PC" (Percolator's timestamp oracle and primary-lock commit, Calvin's deterministic ordering that removes commit-time coordination, FoundationDB's unbundled design: the topic names each in one sentence in the Deep dive with its paper and does not teach them); sagas and outbox implementations; TrueTime and commit wait (the *(skipped)* topics).
- **Outcomes:** Run 2PC in the simulator across three participants and show the in-doubt window; crash the coordinator after prepare and measure the lock-hold time of each participant; explain why 3PC does not rescue blocking under partition; replicate the coordinator's decision through topic 9's consensus log and show participants resolving without blocking after the coordinator dies; say what Paxos Commit changes (the decision survives the coordinator) and where Spanner and CockroachDB place the consensus groups.
- **Introduces:** `two-phase-commit-blocking-in-doubt`, `three-phase-commit-limits`, `paxos-commit-consensus-replicated-participants`. **Uses:** `two-phase-commit` (Chapter 5 topic 16: the practitioner view this topic makes exact), `raft-log-matching-and-commitment`, `raft-terms-election-voting-restriction`, `consensus-raft-paxos-in-systems` (Chapter 5), `distributed-sql-architecture` (Chapter 5), `timing-models-sync-partial-async`, `fault-models-crash-omission-byzantine`, `locking-deadlock-detection` (Chapter 5: the locks a prepared participant holds), `strict-serializability-definition`. **Declared:** none.

### 13. Exactly-once as an end-to-end property (`de.exactly-once-as-end`): full, §14.8

- **Folder:** `exactly-once-as-end`. About 15 min.
- **Scope:** "the end-to-end argument (Saltzer, Reed & Clark): delivery is at-least-once at best, so 'exactly-once' is an *effect* — at-least-once plus idempotent writes, deterministic replay or atomic offset+output commits (Kafka transactions, Flink two-phase-commit sinks, table-format commits); where the guarantee breaks at external side effects. Mechanics: §9.2; idempotency keys: → *Rust Systems Engineering* Ch.27–28" — the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *the end-to-end argument (Saltzer, Reed & Clark):* a function that must be correct for the application "can completely and correctly be implemented only with the knowledge and help of the application standing at the end points", so a reliability feature in a lower layer (a reliable transport, a broker's delivery guarantee) can only be a performance optimisation, never a substitute for the endpoints' own check (the paper's careful-file-transfer example); applied here: a "reliable" message layer cannot make the *effect* happen once.
  - *delivery is at-least-once at best:* with a fair-loss link (topic 1) the only way to make sure a message arrives is to retry, and a retry after a lost acknowledgement delivers it twice, so a sender that wants delivery must accept duplicates; **at-most-once** is the choice not to retry (and to risk loss); there is no third option on a lossy link (the reason the *(skipped)* Two Generals problem is named in topic 1).
  - *'exactly-once' is an effect — at-least-once plus idempotent writes:* if applying the same message twice has the same effect as once (an upsert keyed by a deterministic id, a set-to-value rather than add-delta, a deduplication table), duplicate delivery is harmless, and the combination *is* exactly-once in effect; the idempotency key's scope, lifetime and replay (the Rust track's topics) are named.
  - *deterministic replay:* if a computation is deterministic and its inputs are replayable, re-running it after a failure reproduces the same output, so the output can be overwritten rather than duplicated (the idea behind Flink's recovery from a checkpoint and the Spark idempotent-sink recipe in Chapter 7).
  - *atomic offset+output commits (Kafka transactions, Flink two-phase-commit sinks, table-format commits):* make "consume the input, produce the output, record the offset" **atomic** so a crash cannot leave one without the others: Kafka transactions commit output records and consumer offsets together (KIP-98; the producer's transactional id and epoch are topic 10's fencing); Flink's `TwoPhaseCommitSinkFunction` (introduced in Flink 1.4.0, December 2017, per its engineering post) implements the 2PC of topic 12 for sinks, whose external system must "provide a means to commit or roll back writes that coordinate with Flink's checkpoints"; the post describes the pre-commit at a checkpoint and the commit once the checkpoint is complete, which the builder quotes from the post; an Iceberg or Delta commit is topic 11's compare-and-swap, atomic by construction. The mechanics of each are Chapter 9 §9.2's; this topic places each in the end-to-end argument.
  - *where the guarantee breaks at external side effects:* an effect that leaves the system (an email, a card charge, a call to a non-idempotent API) cannot be rolled back or deduplicated by the pipeline's own transaction, so the guarantee holds only inside the boundary that the atomic commit covers; the fix is to make the external call idempotent (an idempotency key the payment provider honours) or to accept at-least-once there.
  - *the shop's duplicated payment:* a retried charge request after a lost acknowledgement, with and without an idempotency key, as the one worked example across the topic.
