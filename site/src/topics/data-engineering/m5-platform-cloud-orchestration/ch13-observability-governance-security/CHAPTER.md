## The chapter

### The problem it solves

Chapters 7 to 12 taught how a data platform computes, moves, schedules, tests and delivers data. This chapter asks the questions that begin after delivery: *is it healthy, what data is in it and where did it come from, and who may see which of it.* The three are one problem seen from three sides. A pipeline that emits no useful telemetry fails silently, so a stale table is found by a stakeholder; a platform that cannot say where a column came from cannot say who is affected when it changes; and a platform that cannot classify what it holds cannot protect it, because every access, masking and retention rule is a function of a classification. The chapter therefore runs in the order that each part is the input to the next. First **observability** (§13.1): the logs, metrics and traces a pipeline must emit, with OpenTelemetry's context propagation as the way to follow one record through Kafka and Spark, and SLO burn-rate alerting as the rule that turns telemetry into a page that is worth waking someone for. Then **governance and security** (§13.2), starting from what the data *is* (classification), the keys and the law that apply to it (envelope encryption, the pseudonymisation line), who may read which part (access-control models, in-engine row and column security), and the hardest case where the platform must forget (erasure in an immutable lakehouse). Then **governance tooling** (§13.3): the catalog that records what exists, OpenLineage as the standard for recording how it flows, and the quality frameworks that check what it contains. It ends with the **operating model** (§13.4): who owns what, and how lineage turns a proposed change into a list of the people it will hurt. The learner leaves able to instrument a pipeline so an incident is visible in minutes, trace a record across a message hop, choose and defend an encryption, masking and erasure design under a stated threat, and compute the blast radius of a change from the lineage graph.

### Order of ideas, and why

1. **§13.1 Observability (topics 1–3).** Telemetry first, because every later topic's evidence (a policy decision, a delete, a lineage event) is read through logs, metrics and traces: the three signals and the pipeline golden signals, with correlation identifiers as the glue (1) → OpenTelemetry's propagation, the way one record is followed across a hop that has no HTTP request, and how that differs from OpenLineage (2) → SLO burn-rate alerting, which consumes topic 1's metrics under Chapter 11's SLOs and decides what is worth a page (3). The skipped topic "Metric cardinality and observability cost" sits between 2 and 3 in the syllabus; its lab leg (adding a per-customer label and measuring the series explosion) is carried by topic 1's Deep dive.
2. **§13.2 Governance and security (topics 4–9).** Classification first, because every policy below is a function of it (4) → envelope encryption and key management, the mechanism that makes classified data unreadable at rest and, by key deletion, erasable (5) → pseudonymisation versus anonymisation, the legal and technical line that decides whether a transformed dataset is still personal data (6) → access-control models (7), then the engine-level enforcement of them (8), then the case where deletion collides with immutability, which uses 5 (crypto-shredding), 6 (pseudonymised keys) and Chapter 6's table mechanics (9). The five skipped topics here (tokenization, k-anonymity, differential privacy, audit logging, the global regulatory map) are named only where the lab legs require them (see the lab table).
3. **§13.3 Tooling for governance at scale (topics 10–12).** The catalog as the place metadata lives (10) → OpenLineage, the open standard by which jobs report what they read and wrote, and the lineage graph it builds (11) → data-quality frameworks, which attach assertions to the same datasets and run them in or after the pipeline (12). The skipped "Data observability: anomaly detection on metadata" sits after 12 and is only named.
4. **§13.4 Governance architecture and data ownership (topics 13–14).** Last, because the operating model assumes everything before it exists: who owns data products and which controls are computational versus human (13), then the lineage-driven impact analysis that is the operating model's most concrete payoff (14). The skipped "Multi-tenant isolation and compliance" sits between them and is named in topic 13's Deep dive. Topic 14 hands off to Chapter 14.

### One running example: the shop's customer-and-orders platform, with personal data in it

Same fictional online shop as Chapters 1–5 and 12 (no real company, no real person). ### One visual language for the chapter's reels

Built from `site/src/reels/kit.tsx` and `DESIGN.md`'s briefing theme (black and white carry everything; colour only for failure, pale blue only for a second series), with the kit's moves (`Draw`, `Plot`, count, `Decode`, `Reticle`) doing the motion. The chapter shares five conventions:

- **The pipeline strip** (topics 1–3, 11–14): the shop's three nodes in a row (`order-events → orders-rollup → analytics.daily_revenue`) as the chapter's recurring spine. Telemetry topics hang signals on it, lineage topics draw edges between its datasets, impact analysis lights nodes downstream of a change.
- **The trace lane** (topics 1–2): one horizontal lane per service, a span as a filled segment, a *link* as a thin arc from a producer span to a consumer span in another lane (the shape OpenTelemetry's messaging conventions specify for batch fan-in), so context propagation across Kafka is *seen*, not stated.
- **The key ladder** (topics 5, 9): a KEK block above, DEK blocks below, data blocks below those; rotation re-wraps one block, compromise re-encrypts a column of them, deletion removes a block and greys out everything under it, so the cost of each operation is the number of blocks it touches.
- **The tag flood** (topics 4, 7, 8, 11–14): a tag is a small chip on a column; propagation along lineage is the chip spreading downstream along the pipeline strip; a policy is a gate that reads chips. One figure shows classification, policy and impact at once.
- **The decision point** (topics 7–8): a request arrives at a *policy enforcement point*, which asks a *policy decision point*, which reads attributes and returns allow, deny, filter or mask; the bypass paths (a direct file read, an extract, a view) are drawn as arrows that skip the enforcement point, in red.

### 1. Telemetry for pipelines: logs, metrics, traces (`de.telemetry-pipelines-logs-metrics`): full, §13.1

- **Folder:** `telemetry-pipelines-logs-metrics`. About 15 min. Hosts part of the §13.1 chapter lab.
- **Scope:** "structured logs with correlation IDs (run, task, asset, partition), metric types (counter, gauge, histogram), the pipeline golden signals (throughput, latency, error rate, consumer lag, freshness, backlog), push vs pull collection" — the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *structured logs with correlation IDs (run, task, asset, partition):* a log record as a set of named fields rather than a sentence; the correlation key `run_id`, `task_id`, `asset`, `partition` stamped on every record of a run, so one run's records can be selected across processes; what an unstructured log costs when the pipeline has a hundred tasks. (OpenTelemetry's own Logs documentation describes the optional association of a log record with a trace and span; this topic names that as the bridge to topic 2 and does not teach propagation.)
  - *metric types (counter, gauge, histogram):* a counter only goes up (records processed), a gauge goes up and down (queue depth, last-success timestamp), a histogram records a distribution in buckets (task duration), and which question each can and cannot answer (a counter's rate; a histogram's quantile, estimated from buckets, with the caveat that averaging per-instance quantiles is wrong, which Chapter 5's quantile-sketch topic already showed).
  - *the pipeline golden signals (throughput, latency, error rate, consumer lag, freshness, backlog):* Google's four golden signals (latency, traffic, errors, saturation) translated for a data pipeline into the six the syllabus names, with the one new idea: **freshness** (how old is the newest data a consumer can read) is the signal a service has no analogue for, and is the SLI Chapter 11 and topic 3 build on. Kafka's consumer-lag metric (`records-lag-max` in the consumer's fetch-manager metrics) is the concrete lag signal.
  - *push vs pull collection:* Prometheus pulls from long-lived targets; a batch job that finishes before a scrape cannot be pulled, which is the limited case Prometheus' own guidance gives for the Pushgateway, with the pitfalls it lists (a single point of failure and bottleneck, loss of automatic instance health through `up`); the alternative, an OpenTelemetry Collector receiving pushed OTLP, is named and left to topic 2.

### 2. OpenTelemetry across batch and streaming (`de.opentelemetry-across-batch-streaming`): full, §13.1

- **Folder:** `opentelemetry-across-batch-streaming`. About 15 min. Hosts part of the §13.1 chapter lab.
- **Scope:** "OTel signals and the Collector, W3C trace-context propagation through Kafka headers, span links for fan-in batch steps, semantic conventions for messaging and databases, following one record from CDC to warehouse; OTel (operational telemetry) vs OpenLineage (lineage metadata) as complements (§13.3)" — the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *OTel signals and the Collector:* the signals OpenTelemetry defines (traces, metrics, logs, baggage, and profiles per its documentation), and the Collector's role as a receiver, processor and exporter between instrumented code and a backend, so applications export once (OTLP) and routing is configuration.
  - *W3C trace-context propagation through Kafka headers:* the `traceparent` header's `trace-id` and `parent-id` (W3C Trace Context), injected by the producer into the record's headers and extracted by the consumer, because a Kafka hop has no HTTP request to carry it; what survives a hop and what does not.
  - *span links for fan-in batch steps:* a consumer or a Spark task that receives a batch of messages cannot have one parent; OpenTelemetry's messaging conventions use **span links** by default to correlate producers and consumers (the specification says it is the only consistent structure across messaging models), and a batch receive span links to every message's creation context; a nightly aggregation that reads a million events is the extreme case, and the OpenTelemetry SDK specification makes the limit explicit: span limits include a configurable link count (`LinkCountLimit`), so links beyond the limit are dropped and a design must decide which to keep or summarise.
  - *semantic conventions for messaging and databases:* the standard attribute names (for example `messaging.destination.name`, `messaging.destination.partition.id`, and the Kafka-specific attributes) so a trace from Kafka and one from Postgres are queryable the same way.
  - *following one record from CDC to warehouse:* a change captured by Debezium, carried through Kafka, aggregated by Spark and landed in the warehouse, followed as one trace with links at each fan-in; the honest edge is where propagation breaks (a batch job that reads files, an enrichment that drops headers), and Debezium's own documentation names OpenTelemetry tracing support, so the builder verifies what it propagates at the version in use.
  - *OTel vs OpenLineage as complements (§13.3):* OpenTelemetry says *what happened to this request and how long it took* (operational, per execution); OpenLineage says *which datasets this job read and wrote* (metadata, per run); they answer different questions and share only the idea of a run identifier. The lineage standard itself is topic 11.
- **Leaves out:** the three signals' basics, the correlation key and metric types (topic 1); burn-rate alerting (topic 3); Kafka internals and exactly-once (Chapter 9); the OpenLineage event model and facets (topic 11); sampling strategy and observability cost (the *(skipped)* cardinality topic).
- **Outcomes:** Draw the path of a trace context through a produce, a consume and a batch step and say where each header is injected and extracted; explain why a batch receive uses links and not a parent; name the semantic-convention attributes a Kafka consumer span should carry; run a record from a CDC source to a warehouse write and show the trace; say in one sentence what OpenTelemetry records that OpenLineage does not, and the reverse.

- **Bridge:** "Logs and metrics are written by one process and read in one place. A single order, though, crosses a Kafka topic, a Spark job and a warehouse, and no process sees the whole journey. This topic is how a trace follows one record across those hops." (Answers topic 1's handoff.)

- **Hook:** one order is wrong in the warehouse. It entered through a database change, crossed a Kafka topic, was folded into a nightly Spark aggregate and landed in a table. ### 6. Pseudonymisation vs anonymisation (`de.pseudonymisation-vs-anonymisation`): full, §13.2

- **Folder:** `pseudonymisation-vs-anonymisation`. About 15 min. Hosts part of the §13.2 chapter lab.
- **Scope:** "the legal and technical line: pseudonymised data remains personal data for whoever can re-identify; the CJEU's relative approach in *EDPS v SRB* (C‑413/23 P, Sept 2025: may be non-personal for a recipient with no means to re-identify) and the pending GDPR changes in the Digital Omnibus; keyed hashing (HMAC) vs plain hashing (dictionary attacks on emails and phone numbers), linkage attacks" — the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *the legal and technical line: pseudonymised data remains personal data for whoever can re-identify:* GDPR Article 4(5) defines pseudonymisation as processing that leaves data no longer attributable to a specific person "without the use of additional information", provided that information is kept separately and protected; Recital 26 says pseudonymised data "which could be attributed to a natural person by the use of additional information should be considered to be information on an identifiable natural person", and that identifiability turns on "all the means reasonably likely to be used"; anonymous information is outside the regulation. The engineering consequence: a pseudonymised table is still personal data in the hands of whoever holds the key or the dictionary, so it still needs a legal basis, security, retention and erasure.
  - *the CJEU's relative approach in EDPS v SRB:* the judgment of 4 September 2025 (C‑413/23 P) confirmed that pseudonymised data "must not be regarded as constituting, in all cases and for every person, personal data" (the Court's press release), so for a recipient with no reasonable means to re-identify it may not be personal data; the same judgment held that the duty to inform data subjects is assessed "at the time of collection of the data and from the point of view of the controller" (paragraph 111), so the controller cannot escape its transparency duty by pointing to the recipient's position. The builder reads and quotes the judgment's own text (mirrored at ipcuria; the Court's press release is the official summary) and states the limits: the case is decided under Regulation (EU) 2018/1725, the EU institutions' data-protection regulation, so the builder checks in the judgment how far the Court itself ties its reasoning to the GDPR's Article 4(1) concept before saying it carries over, and the recipient's *actual* means are a question of fact.
  - *the pending GDPR changes in the Digital Omnibus:* the Commission's Digital Omnibus proposal of 19 November 2025 would redefine personal data so that pseudonymised data is not personal data for an entity without means to re-identify (as the European Parliament's legislative-train page summarises it); the EDPB and EDPS issued Joint Opinion 2/2026 on it and recommended changes; as of the Parliament tracker's 20 September 2026 update, committee amendments were tabled and the Council's negotiating mandate vote had been cancelled. It is a **proposal**, not law: the topic states its status with that date and says what would change if adopted, and builds no design on it.
  - *keyed hashing (HMAC) vs plain hashing (dictionary attacks on emails and phone numbers):* a plain hash of an email is deterministic and public: anyone with a list of candidate emails computes the same hashes and matches them (a dictionary attack; phone numbers have a tiny space, brute-forced in seconds); a keyed hash (HMAC) under a secret key is not reproducible without the key, so the key is the "additional information" of Article 4(5) and must be stored separately; the EDPB's pseudonymisation guidelines (adopted 16 January 2025, in the consultation version the builder fetched) discuss brute-force searching of pseudonyms and recommend keyed or otherwise resistant constructions.

### 9. Right to erasure in immutable lakehouses (`de.right-erasure-immutable-lakehouses`): full, §13.2

- **Folder:** `right-erasure-immutable-lakehouses`. About 15 min. Hosts the §13.2 chapter lab.
- **Scope:** "deletes vs time travel (snapshots must expire), MOR delete files until compaction, `VACUUM`/`expire_snapshots`/orphan removal, replicas, backups and Kafka topics (compaction tombstones, retention), per-subject key crypto-shredding and its key-management cost, proving deletion to an auditor; retention policies as code" — the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *deletes vs time travel (snapshots must expire):* a `DELETE` in Iceberg or Delta writes a new snapshot; the old snapshot still references the old files, so a time-travel query still reads the erased person; erasure is complete only when the old snapshots expire (Iceberg `expire_snapshots`) or the table is vacuumed (Delta `VACUUM`, which is not run automatically and has a default 7-day retention threshold per Delta's documentation).

  - *`VACUUM`/`expire_snapshots`/orphan removal:* the maintenance operations that physically remove unreferenced files, and the orphan-file removal that finds files no snapshot references (a failed write, a rewritten file), each with its retention window and the safety trade-off (shorten it and you can break concurrent readers and time travel).

### 13. Federated governance operating model (`de.federated-governance-operating-model`): full, §13.4

- **Folder:** `federated-governance-operating-model`. About 15 min. Hosts the §13.4 chapter lab.
- **Scope:** "domain teams own data products, a platform team supplies self-serve tooling, central policy is applied computationally; reverse Conway's law; what must stay human stewardship (criticality, retention exceptions, ambiguous PII classification) vs what to automate; links to §3.4 (data mesh) and §19.1 (team topology)" — the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *domain teams own data products, a platform team supplies self-serve tooling, central policy is applied computationally:* the data-mesh operating shape as Chapter 3 introduced it (domain ownership, data as a product, a self-serve platform, federated computational governance as Dehghani's four principles); this topic's contribution is the *governance* part: the central body defines policy once (a classification scheme, retention classes, access rules over tags, quality floors) and the platform enforces it in code (topics 7, 8, 12), so a domain team cannot ship a table that violates it.
  - *reverse Conway's law:* Conway's 1968 thesis, in his own words, is that an organisation that designs a system produces a design that copies its communication structure; the reverse is to choose the team structure that produces the system architecture you want, so governance follows ownership boundaries instead of fighting them.
  - *what must stay human stewardship vs what to automate:* automate what is a rule over an attribute (apply a mask to a tag, block a table with no owner, fail a missing contract field); keep human what needs judgement (a table's business criticality, a retention exception with a legal reason, an ambiguous classification such as a free-text column, topic 4's false-negative residue), with a named steward and a review cadence.

### 14. Lineage-driven impact analysis (`de.lineage-driven-impact-analysis`): full, §13.4

- **Folder:** `lineage-driven-impact-analysis`. About 15 min. Hosts part of the §13.3 chapter lab.
- **Scope:** "blast-radius scoring before a schema change or deprecation, stale-table propagation, automated notification of dependent owners, deprecation workflows, lineage as the backbone of incident triage (§11.3) and contract enforcement (§11.4)" — the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *blast-radius scoring before a schema change or deprecation:* given a proposed change to a column or table, walk the lineage graph downstream (transitive dependents) to list everything affected and score it (by number of dependents, their tier and criticality, usage from the catalog, whether the dependency is direct or only indirect in column lineage); a change that touches nothing downstream scores zero and may proceed; the score decides review depth.
  - *stale-table propagation:* when a table goes stale or a run fails, mark every downstream dataset as potentially stale along the graph, so consumers see "stale because upstream X" instead of discovering it; the propagation is a graph walk again, bounded by lineage coverage (topic 11's gaps).
  - *automated notification of dependent owners:* resolve each affected node to its owner from the catalog (topic 10) and send one message per owner, listing what of theirs is affected; deduplicate so an owner of ten affected tables gets one notice (the topic 3 deduplication idea, now with a graph).
  - *deprecation workflows:* announce, mark deprecated in the catalog, track remaining readers via usage and lineage, escalate unreplied owners, and remove only when the dependent list is empty or each owner has signed off; the step that fails in practice is the reader lineage cannot see.
  - *lineage as the backbone of incident triage (§11.3) and contract enforcement (§11.4):* Chapter 11's incident-response topic derives a blast radius from lineage and its contract-enforcement topic notifies downstream owners through it; this topic is the common mechanism both use, and the builder cross-references them by name.
