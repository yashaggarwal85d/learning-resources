## The chapter

### The problem it solves

Milestones 1-4 built pipelines that read, transform, move and stream data correctly. None of them asked where any of it actually runs, who is allowed to touch it, what it costs, or which country's law applies to it. This chapter, opening Milestone 5, is the discipline a pipeline meets the moment it leaves a laptop: a region can fail without the account noticing, an object store's "it just works" has exact rules a lost-update bug hides behind, a role with one wildcard permission is a breach waiting for an audit, and a correct pipeline that costs ten times what it should is still a production incident, just a quiet, recurring one. And in Europe specifically, "it runs" is not the same question as "it is allowed to run here, on this provider, holding this data" — a distinction every other chapter in this course can ignore and this one cannot.

It teaches, in order, the **cloud primitives everything else rests on** (10.1): failure domains and blast radius, the exact consistency and write semantics of object storage (because every table format in Ch.6 is built on top of exactly these guarantees), storage tiers and the lifecycle trap that corrupts a table by deleting a file a live snapshot still references, workload identity as the replacement for static keys, where secrets live, and what a cloud bill is actually made of. It then teaches **the managed data services themselves, as mechanisms, across all three major clouds at once** (10.2): a cross-cloud map so the same mechanism is recognised under three different product names, the catalog-as-policy-and-credential-broker pattern that every governed lakehouse now uses, managed table maintenance as a service, and the economics of serverless against provisioned compute. It then spends a full section on **the platform the syllabus expects a working data engineer to actually touch**, Databricks and managed lakehouses (10.3), because "cloud data architecture" in a job description means this specific layer more often than raw cloud primitives. It teaches **infrastructure as code and FinOps** (10.4) as the two disciplines that keep a platform reproducible and affordable. It then turns to **the regulatory architecture that is not optional once a pipeline touches a person who lives in the EU** (10.5): GDPR by design, and the harder, less-taught distinction between where bytes sit and who can be legally compelled to hand them over. It closes with the two settings the syllabus insists a data engineer will actually meet and a purely-cloud-native education skips: **SAP and industrial-system integration** (10.6), because an enormous amount of the world's operational data still lives behind SAP's own extraction rules, and **hybrid and on-prem architecture** (10.7), because "cloud-only" is a choice, not a default, and the chapter's closing lab makes a learner design for a real constraint set (a German manufacturer) rather than a green-field cloud account.

The learner leaves able to look at a cloud data design and name its blast radius, its actual write-consistency guarantee, who can read what and why, what it costs and where the money really goes, which of three clouds' product names map to which mechanism, and whether EU law lets it run where it is proposed to run at all.

### Shared notation and terms

- **Three positions every service claim is checked against** (introduced topic 2, reused through 10.3): is it **zonal** or **regional**, is its write **strongly or eventually consistent**, and is it **billed by capacity reserved or by request/byte consumed**. Every cross-cloud comparison in the chapter answers these three questions before naming a product.
- **The credential-vending pattern** (introduced topic 8, reused in 13): a **catalog** (policy), an **engine** (trusted to enforce it), and a **short-lived, down-scoped credential** vended per request instead of a long-lived, bucket-wide one; the failure mode is always the same, an engine reading storage directly and bypassing the catalog.
- **The three-ledger cost model** (introduced topic 6, reused through 10.4 and 10.7): **storage** (GB-month plus per-request), **compute** (capacity reserved vs consumed), **network** (same-AZ free in most clouds, cross-AZ and egress charged, NAT processing charged per GB); every cost claim in the chapter is placed on one of these three ledgers, never left as an unlabelled "it's expensive."
- **Residency vs sovereignty vs localisation** (introduced topic 18, reused in 10.6-10.7): where the bytes physically sit, who can be legally compelled to produce them regardless of where they sit, and a law requiring the first as a precondition for operating.
- **Spoken numbers:** narration has no digits, symbols or code names, the automatic check inherited from Chapters 1-9: "If-None-Match", "object lock", "data processing unit" are said as plain words; any number is spoken aloud. Setting names, SKU names and header names stay on screen and in the description, never in the narration.

### 1. Failure domains: regions, zones, cells (`de.failure-domains-regions-zones`): refresher, 10.1

- **Folder:** `failure-domains-regions-zones`. About 5 min.
- **Scope:** "which data services are zonal vs regional (block volumes zonal, object storage regional, managed Kafka multi-AZ), what an AZ outage actually takes down, cross-AZ replication cost, cell-based architectures as blast-radius limits" - clause by clause:
  - *zonal vs regional:* a block volume (EBS-style) lives in one zone and cannot attach across zones; object storage (S3-style) is a regional service replicated across zones by the provider; a managed streaming service (MSK-style) is deployed across multiple AZs by configuration, not by nature — three different defaults, shown side by side.
  - *what an AZ outage takes down:* every zonal resource pinned to that zone (block-attached compute, a single-AZ database instance) stops; a regional, multi-AZ service keeps serving from its other zones; shown as the map-with-dots figure losing one dot.
  - *cross-AZ replication cost:* cross-AZ traffic is billed per GB in every major cloud (named, sourced), the one cost a single-AZ design avoids and a multi-AZ design accepts for availability.
  - *cell-based architectures:* partitioning a service into independent cells (each with its own full stack and a bounded set of customers) so one cell's failure cannot reach another's customers, as a deliberately *smaller* blast radius than "the whole region."
- **Leaves out:** object storage's actual consistency and write guarantees are topic 2; the cost ledger in full is topic 6; a real multi-region failover drill is out of scope for this course (named as sourced-only in the Deep dive).
- **Outcomes:** Say, for a named data service, whether it is zonal or regional and what survives a single-zone outage; price a cross-AZ replication link from its GB volume; explain what a cell buys over "the whole region" as the unit of blast radius.
- **Introduces:** `failure-domain-blast-radius`. **Uses:** none. **Declared:** basic cloud vocabulary (a region, an availability zone, a virtual machine).
- **Bridge:** "Milestones 1-4 built correct pipelines without asking where they run. This chapter is that question, starting with the most basic one: when a piece of the cloud disappears, how much of the system goes with it?" (Opens Milestone 5 and the chapter; holds under any plausible Ch.9 ending per the assumption note above.)
- **Handoff:** "Object storage is called 'regional' in that map, which sounds safe. What it actually promises about a read right after a write, and about listing a prefix, is a separate and much more exact question — and every table format later in this course is built on the exact answer."

### 2. Object storage semantics for data engineers (`de.object-storage-semantics-data`): full, 10.1

- **Folder:** `object-storage-semantics-data`. About 15 min.
- **Scope:** "strong read-after-write and list consistency (S3 since Dec 2020), atomic single-object PUT but no atomic rename (copy+delete) unlike ADLS Gen2 hierarchical namespace, conditional writes (`If-None-Match` Aug 2024, `If-Match` Nov 2024, CopyObject Oct 2025) as compare-and-swap for catalog-free commits and leases, per-prefix request-rate limits and `503 SlowDown`; why table formats exist on top of these guarantees" - clause by clause:
  - *strong consistency since Dec 2020:* before that date S3 was eventually consistent for overwrite PUTs and list-after-write; AWS's own announcement is quoted for the date and the guarantee change, with the practical meaning (no more "read-your-own-write" workarounds needed) measured against a local MinIO server configured to behave this way.
  - *atomic PUT, no atomic rename:* a single PUT either fully lands or doesn't, but there is no primitive that renames object A to B atomically — only copy-then-delete, which is two operations with a window between them; contrasted with ADLS Gen2's hierarchical namespace, which does offer an atomic rename (sourced from Azure's own documentation), explaining why Azure-native pipelines can lean on a pattern S3-native ones cannot.
  - *conditional writes as compare-and-swap:* `If-None-Match: *` (fails if the key already exists — "create if absent") and `If-Match` (fails unless the current ETag matches — "update if unchanged"), the exact dates these shipped on S3 (named precisely, sourced), and why this turns an object store into a usable compare-and-swap primitive for a catalog-free commit log or a lease, demonstrated end to end against MinIO.
  - *per-prefix rate limits and 503 SlowDown:* S3's documented per-prefix request-rate scaling and what triggers throttling, reproduced by hammering one key prefix locally and then fixing it with a sharded key-space design, counting the throttled requests before and after.
  - *why table formats exist on these guarantees:* a one-paragraph forward pointer (Ch.6, not re-taught) that Iceberg/Delta's atomic commit is exactly a conditional-write-shaped compare-and-swap on a pointer file, so this topic's mechanism is the floor every table format's correctness argument stands on.
- **Leaves out:** storage tiers and lifecycle rules are topic 3; the catalog layer that sits on top of conditional writes (credential vending) is topic 8; table-format commit protocols themselves are Ch.6 (named, not re-taught, and not put in `uses` since Ch.6 is unconfirmed-live per the assumption note).
- **Outcomes:** State object storage's exact read-after-write and list guarantee and the date it changed; explain why rename needs two operations and what window that opens; build a conditional-write compare-and-swap commit log and a lease; reproduce and fix a hot-prefix throttle.
- **Introduces:** `object-store-consistency-guarantee`, `conditional-write-compare-swap`, `prefix-throttling-hot-key`. **Uses:** `failure-domain-blast-radius`. **Declared:** basic HTTP, what a PUT/GET/DELETE request is.
- **Bridge:** "That map called object storage 'regional,' which sounds safe, but safety here means something exact: what a read sees right after a write, and whether two objects can swap places atomically. Neither answer is what most engineers assume." (Answers topic 1's handoff.)
- **Handoff:** "A conditional write protects one key. It says nothing about how long that byte should even exist, or what it costs to keep it — which is the next question: storage tiers, and the lifecycle rule that can quietly delete a file a live table still points at."
- **Prerequisites panel:** topic 1 (Before this). **Pre-notes terms:** PUT, ETag, read-after-write consistency, compare-and-swap, rate limit, 503.
- **Hook:** two workers race to write the same commit key with a naive "list, see it's missing, then PUT"; both see it missing, both write, and the loser's write silently overwrites the winner's with nothing in any log to say so — until the same race run with `If-None-Match` makes the loser's write fail instead, loudly and correctly.
- **Visual idea:** two timeline lanes (worker A, worker B) each sending a PUT at nearly the same instant; in the first pass both PUTs land green (the overwrite is invisible); in the second pass one PUT turns `C.bad` red with a 412 and the other stays green, with the key's single final value shown underneath both times to make the first pass's silent loss visible.
- **Lab:** none of its own; this topic's conditional-write mechanism and hot-prefix fix are built here and priced in topic 6's lab.
- **Sources:** AWS's "Amazon S3 now delivers strong read-after-write consistency automatically" (Dec 2020) announcement, AWS S3 documentation on conditional writes (`If-Match`/`If-None-Match`, `CopyObject` conditional support) and on request-rate performance guidelines, Azure's ADLS Gen2 "Hierarchical namespace" documentation (atomic rename) — all to be fetched and quoted at research time; MinIO's own S3-API-compatibility documentation read to confirm which of these semantics MinIO actually reproduces locally, with any gap disclosed.

### 3. Storage classes and lifecycle economics (`de.storage-classes-lifecycle-economics`): full, 10.1

- **Folder:** `storage-classes-lifecycle-economics`. About 15 min.
- **Scope:** "hot/infrequent/archive tiers across S3/GCS/Azure Blob, minimum-duration and retrieval charges, Intelligent-Tiering-style automation, lifecycle rules vs table-format snapshot expiry (a lifecycle rule deleting files a live snapshot references corrupts the table), versioning plus Object Lock/WORM for ransomware and regulatory retention" - clause by clause:
  - *hot/infrequent/archive tiers:* the named tiers in each of the three clouds (S3 Standard/Standard-IA/Glacier family, GCS Standard/Nearline/Coldline/Archive, Azure Blob Hot/Cool/Cold/Archive), each with its own minimum storage duration and early-deletion charge, and its retrieval charge per GB plus (for archive tiers) a retrieval *delay* measured in hours, not seconds — the trade-off stated as a formula (cheaper to store, more expensive and slower to read back), priced on a synthetic dataset.
  - *automated tiering:* S3 Intelligent-Tiering (and the equivalent automatic-tiering features of the other two) moving an object between tiers based on observed access, and the monitoring fee that automation itself costs.
  - *lifecycle rules vs snapshot expiry:* a lifecycle rule is time-based and knows nothing about a table format's own metadata; if it deletes a data file that a live table snapshot still references (because the table's own `expire_snapshots`/`VACUUM` hasn't run, or a time-travel query is in flight), the table silently corrupts — reproduced against a minimal local stand-in (a JSON "manifest" pointing at files, a lifecycle-style deletion script run against the files directly, and the manifest left dangling).

  - *cross-WAN CDC and streaming, buffering, ordering across link failure:* MirrorMaker 2 (or a cloud-native equivalent's cluster-linking/replication feature) as the standard way to replicate a stream across a WAN link, what it buffers during a link outage, and what ordering guarantee survives a failure and reconnection — demonstrated against a local two-broker setup with the link between them deliberately cut and restored.
  - *latency budgets for near-real-time pipelines:* working backward from a stated end-to-end latency requirement to how much of that budget the WAN hop itself is allowed to consume, leaving the rest for processing.
  - *deciding where compute lives (data gravity):* topic 21's data-gravity argument, applied here as the actual decision rule — when the WAN link's cost and latency make moving compute to the data cheaper than moving data to the compute, and vice versa.
