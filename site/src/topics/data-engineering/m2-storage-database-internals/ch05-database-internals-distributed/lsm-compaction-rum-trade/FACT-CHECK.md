Role: adversarial fact-checker, not the author. Every claim re-derived from the fetched source text in `sources/`, not from memory of writing `OUTLINE.md`.

## Currency check

- The RUM conjecture paper is EDBT 2016; its definitions (read/write/space amplification as ratios, the conjecture itself) are foundational vocabulary that the field has used unchanged since, including in RocksDB's own documentation (which uses the identical "write amplification"/"read amplification"/"space amplification" terms). No currency risk.
- Both RocksDB wiki pages (`Compaction`, `Write-Stalls`) were fetched on 2026-10-24 and describe the current, maintained engine; nothing taught here (leveled vs. tiered vs. FIFO, the two write-stall triggers, the delayed-write-rate response) depends on a version-specific knob that has since changed. The `Compaction` page cross-references the Dostoevsky paper's "Leveled-N"/Fluid LSM as a newer middle ground, named here only in `post-notes.md` → Frontier, not taught as the base mechanism.
- This sandbox's own Postgres cluster is 16.15; nothing in this topic's Postgres-side measurement (WAL bytes, `pg_total_relation_size`, a plain `SELECT ... WHERE id = $1`) depends on a version-specific feature. No currency risk.

## Red team

1. **"Is claim 2's 'usually' doing too much work — could tiered actually cost more write amplification than leveled in some configurations, making the directional claim in step 2's reveal too strong?"** No: the claim is deliberately hedged ("usually worse... with leveled than with tiered"), matching the source's own hedge, and `OUTLINE.md`'s step 2 caption states the trade-off as a mechanism (why), not as an unconditional ranking. The measured example (claim 10) shows the *file-count* structural difference, a real and unhedged fact about this one workload, not the write-amplification ranking itself — the two are kept separate on purpose.
2. **"Step 2's file-count comparison (3 vs. 4) — is that actually caused by the compaction style, or could it be noise from background-thread timing (the `time.sleep(2)` wait)?"** Tested by rerunning the experiment twice with the same 2-second settle time; both runs gave the identical 3/4 split. The gap is also structurally expected: leveled's own one-sorted-run-per-level design merges more eagerly than universal's, which is exactly the documented mechanism being measured, not an artifact of the wait being too short or too long. Held.
3. **"Is the FIFO experiment actually testing FIFO's own compaction logic, or just testing that `rocksdict`'s Python bindings correctly expose a RocksDB feature?"** The assertion is about engine behavior (the oldest key is gone, the newest is present, total size stays near the cap) reached by writing real data through the real engine and reading it back — `rocksdict` is a thin Rust-backed binding to RocksDB itself, not a reimplementation, so this is testing RocksDB's own FIFO compaction, the same way every other example in this chapter uses `rocksdict` as a window onto real RocksDB internals. Held.
4. **"Step 8's read-latency comparison — doesn't showing RocksDB as dramatically faster risk teaching 'LSM trees have faster reads than B-trees,' which claim 14 explicitly disclaims?"** `OUTLINE.md`'s step 8 caption and claim 14 both state the caveat directly, on screen and in the narration (not only in `post-notes.md`): the gap mixes an embedded, in-process call against a real client-server round trip over a socket, so it is not purely a read-amplification comparison. This was checked specifically to avoid overclaiming; the step's own on-screen text says "but that's not the whole story" rather than presenting the number as architecture-clean.
5. **"Could the write/space amplification numbers (claim 13) actually be dominated by fixed per-connection or per-table overhead rather than the workload itself, especially at only 2,000 rows?"** Partially a fair concern — at a much larger scale, Postgres's own fixed per-table/per-WAL-record overhead would matter proportionally less, and RocksDB's own write amplification is known to grow with deeper LSM trees (more compaction rounds) at larger scale, a direction this small lab cannot show. This is exactly why step 8's claim is scoped to "on this workload," not "LSM trees always write/space-amplify less than B-trees," and why `post-notes.md` → Frontier names the scale caveat directly rather than letting the lab's numbers imply a universal ranking.

## Problem Solving

- **Postgres's own write-amplification measurement was not deterministic across runs** (2.61×, then 2.82× on a rerun), because a checkpoint landing in the middle of the workload forces extra full-page WAL writes that have nothing to do with the workload itself. **Root-caused** by re-running the same workload three times and noticing the WAL delta varied specifically around checkpoint boundaries, not randomly. **Fixed** by issuing an explicit `CHECKPOINT` immediately before starting the WAL measurement, which made the result stable (2.61×, 2.61×, 2.63× across three further runs) — and widened the assertion's own tolerance slightly as a safety margin, rather than relying on exact equality for a wall-clock-adjacent measurement.
- **The first leveled-vs-tiered attempt used sequential, monotonically increasing keys**, which let RocksDB's leveled style use "trivial moves" (relocating a file between levels without rewriting it, since key ranges never overlap) — making leveled compaction look artificially cheap and producing a write-amplification comparison that didn't match the documented direction at all. **Fixed** by shuffling the key order before writing (`random.Random(42).shuffle`), forcing genuine overlapping merges, and by scoping the measured claim to *file count after settling* (a real, structural, deterministically reproducible fact) rather than to an exact write-amplification multiplier, which proved too workload-scale-sensitive to pin reliably at this sandbox's achievable scale (see red-team item 5).
- **The first write-stall attempt tried to catch an actual slowed write in progress** (timing a burst of writes before and after crossing `level0_slowdown_writes_trigger`), but no stall was ever observed — `rocksdb.stall.micros` stayed at 0 even with the trigger configured low and L0 files well past it, likely because this single-threaded Python workload's own write rate never got far enough ahead of the (disabled) compaction to trip RocksDB's internal timing-sensitive stall logic the way a real concurrent write-heavy application would. **Fixed** by measuring the real, documented *trigger condition* directly instead (`rocksdb.num-files-at-level0` against the configured `level0_slowdown_writes_trigger`), which is deterministic and still teaches the true mechanism — RocksDB watching that same number — without depending on catching an actual stall's timing in a single-threaded sandbox script.
- **`rocksdict`'s Python bindings don't expose RocksDB's `max_table_files_size` FIFO option directly on `Options`**; it required discovering `rocksdict.FifoCompactOptions`, a separate options object passed to `Options.set_fifo_compaction_options()`, rather than a flat setter method like the other options used in this chapter's examples. Found by listing `dir(rocksdict.Options)` for anything FIFO-related and finding `set_fifo_compaction_options` plus a correspondingly named options class.
