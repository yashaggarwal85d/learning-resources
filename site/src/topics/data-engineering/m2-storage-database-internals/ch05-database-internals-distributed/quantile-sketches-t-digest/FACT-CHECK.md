# Fact-check — de.quantile-sketches-t-digest

## Claim 1: t-digest clusters real-valued samples by mean and count, giving particularly high accuracy near the tails, and supports a real buffer-and-merge construction
Source: Dunning & Ertl, the t-digest paper (SOURCES.md #1).
> "A t-digest is generated by clustering real-valued samples and retaining the mean and number of
> samples for each cluster. This clustering can then be used to estimate quantile-related
> statistics with particularly high accuracy near the tails of a distribution."
> "One version keeps
> a buffer of incoming samples. When the buffer fills, the contents are sorted and merged
> with the centroids computed from previous samples. This merging form of the t-digest
> algorithm has the virtue of allowing all memory structures to be allocated statically."
Verdict: Confirmed directly from the paper's own description, and reproduced in `examples.py`:
the `Digest` class clusters raw values into `[mean, count]` centroids and merges two digests by
combining their centroid lists and re-compressing, the same real mechanism the paper names.

## Claim 2: a rank-error guarantee can translate into an arbitrarily large relative error on heavy-tailed data; DDSketch is the first fully-mergeable, relative-error sketch and is used by Datadog at wide scale
Source: Masson, Rim, Lee, the DDSketch paper (SOURCES.md #2).
> "Unfortunately, for data sets with heavy tails, rank-error guarantees can return values with
> large relative errors. Consider again the
> histogram of 2M request response times in Figure 3. If we have a
> quantile sketch with a rank accuracy of 0.005, and ask for the 99th
> percentile, we are guaranteed to get a value between the 98.5th and
> 99.5th percentile. In this case this is anywhere from 2 to 20 seconds,
> which from an end-user's perspective is the difference between an
> annoying delay and giving up on the request."
> "We present the first fully-mergeable, relative-error
> quantile sketching algorithm with formal guarantees. The sketch is
> extremely fast and accurate, and is currently being used by Datadog
> at a wide-scale."
Verdict: Confirmed directly from the paper's own introduction.

## Claim 3: KLL gives a rank-error guarantee — an additive bound on how far a query's estimated rank can be from its true rank
Source: Karnin, Lang, Liberty, the KLL sketch paper (SOURCES.md #3).
> "This paper resolves one of the longest standing basic problems in the streaming computational
> model. Namely, optimal construction of quantile sketches. An ε approximate quantile
> sketch receives a stream of items x1 , . . . , xn and allows one to approximate the rank of any
> query
> up to additive error εn with probability at least 1 − δ."
Verdict: Confirmed directly from the paper's own abstract — a rank-error guarantee (bounding how
far an estimated rank is from the true rank), contrasted directly with DDSketch's own
relative-error guarantee (bounding how far an estimated value is from the true value) above.

## Claim 4: a real warehouse engine ships a t-digest-backed percentile function directly, with its own documented performance-vs-precision trade-off
Source: ClickHouse documentation, "quantileTDigest" (SOURCES.md #4).
> "Computes an approximate quantile of a numeric data sequence using the t-digest algorithm."
> "The performance of the function is lower than performance of quantile or quantileTiming. In
> terms of the ratio of State size to precision, this function is much better than quantile."
Verdict: Confirmed directly from a real, shipped engine's own documentation — the "which engines
... use which" half of this topic's own scope line, naming t-digest specifically.

## Claim 5 (this course): averaging per-partition p99 values diverges measurably from the true combined p99, while a single digest merged from per-partition digests stays close to it
Source: `examples.py`, run directly against a real, partitioned, heavy-tailed (lognormal)
dataset of 200,000 points across 20 partitions, with one partition given a real extra burst of
500 unusually slow points.
Measured: true global p99 (exact, computed by sorting every point) is 483.0 ms. A naive average
of the twenty partitions' own true p99 values is 456.0 ms — 5.6% off the true value. A single
digest merged from the twenty partition digests (built with 2,000 centroids each, real
buffer-and-merge construction) estimates 485.8 ms — 0.6% off the true value, roughly nine times
closer than the naive average.
Verdict: Confirmed by direct computation, not asserted from a source. Deterministic (fixed
seeds): re-running produced byte-identical output.

## Problem Solving
- The KLL paper's abstract uses the Greek letters ε and δ directly as UTF-8 characters (not a
  garbled glyph); quoted exactly as extracted, since `sources.mjs check`'s own comparison matches
  the literal UTF-8 text and these characters need no special handling.
- The initial `Digest` implementation used a fixed `max_centroids` as the direct target for
  compression (`target_size = total / max_centroids`), which coarsens badly once many
  partition-level digests are merged together and the combined total weight grows well past any
  single partition's own total — a merged digest's own error at p99 was initially *worse* than
  the naive average (10.8% vs. 5.6%) until `max_centroids` was raised enough (2,000) that the
  merged digest's own resolution stayed fine even at the combined total weight. This mirrors the
  real t-digest paper's own point that a fixed-size digest's resolution is a real, tunable
  parameter, not a free guarantee — confirmed by re-running the measurement after the fix rather
  than assumed.
- The Datadog engineering blog post describing DDSketch (a secondary, more readable
  explanation) returned only navigation boilerplate when fetched in this sandbox (a
  JavaScript-rendered page `sources.mjs check`'s own static fetcher cannot execute) — resolved by
  using the DDSketch paper's own primary statement that the sketch "is currently being used by
  Datadog at a wide-scale" instead, which is itself a primary source for the same fact.
