## The chapter

### The problem it solves

Chapter 15 designed a global manufacturing telemetry platform and a real-time quality monitor, and in doing so borrowed vocabulary it could not stop to teach. This chapter is that vocabulary, taught as mechanism. It starts with what a plant's data **means**: the ISA-95 levels that say which system owns which data, the equipment hierarchy that every reading joins to and that changes over time, the production entities (work orders, batches, lots) and their genealogy, the quality and OEE calculations that the plant's own managers run, and the energy meters whose counters are not readings. It then turns to the **shape of the data**: a time series is not a table with a timestamp column. Its clocks disagree, its samples come at different rates and are missing in ways that matter, its aggregates are only valid if they stay mergeable, its float values compress twelve-fold or barely at all depending on the codec, its label sets can explode, and the engines that store it (Prometheus, TimescaleDB, InfluxDB 3, ClickHouse, a lakehouse) each make a different bet. Finally it follows the data **from the machine**: OPC UA's typed address space, MQTT with Sparkplug and the Unified Namespace, the historian whose swinging-door compression keeps only the points that matter, the gateway that buffers through a WAN outage, and the Asset Administration Shell that describes a machine to the outside world. The learner leaves able to read an OEE number and say whether to trust it, to say which timestamp is the event time, to choose a rollup that will not lie, to estimate what a codec will save, to size a label set before it becomes an outage, and to trace a reading from a valve to a lakehouse row with its meaning intact.

### Order of ideas, and why

1. **§16.1 Manufacturing data model (topics 1-6).** Everything else in the chapter joins to the equipment hierarchy, so it comes first: the ISA-95 levels and the hierarchy as a dimension that changes (1) → the production entities that move through that hierarchy, runs, batches and lots, and the genealogy that connects them (2) → quality data on those entities, with the statistical rules that read it (3) → OEE and downtime, the KPI computed from machine-state events on the hierarchy (4) → energy and utility metering, the data whose raw form is a counter and not an event (5) → the IT/OT boundary that decides who owns each copy and how it is extracted without touching control (6).
2. **§16.2 Time-series engineering (topics 7-15).** The data is now modelled; this section is about its time axis. The timestamps themselves, three clocks and a time zone (7) → regularising irregular series by resampling and gap filling (8) → aligning two series that were never sampled together (9) → aggregating over time so that the result can be merged and re-aggregated, with retention tiers (10) → compressing the stored points (11) → the label sets that identify series and the index over them (12) → the storage engines that put those pieces together (13) → choosing among a time-series database, a warehouse and a lakehouse (14) → closing the section with the data-quality checks that sensors need before any of the rest can be trusted (15).
3. **§16.3 Industrial connectivity and historians (topics 16-20).** Now follow the data upstream to its source: OPC UA, the typed model machines publish (16) → MQTT, Sparkplug and the Unified Namespace, the message layer above it (17) → the process historian and its lossy compression (18) → the edge gateway that buffers, replays and corrects clocks (19) → the Asset Administration Shell, the standardised description of the asset itself and its role in EU data spaces (20), which hands off to Chapter 17.

Topic 1 draws it; topics 2-6 hang entities on it; topics 7-15 store its readings; topics 16-20 bring the readings in. It continues Chapter 15's scenes: the coating line on which a thickness reading drifts, the three-plant telemetry platform. The sandbox generates its data synthetically with seeded generators, so every number is reproducible.

### Shared notation and terms

- **The hierarchy path:** `enterprise → site → area → line → equipment → sensor`, written `RUH/coat/L4/oven1/T3` in drawings (`site/area/line/equipment/sensor`, the enterprise implied). The syllabus's "line/cell" level is written **line**. A **tag** is a named sensor or controller value as a PLC or SCADA system exposes it.
- **Three clocks:** `t_device` (stamped by the sensor or PLC), `t_gateway` (stamped when the edge gateway received it), `t_ingest` (stamped when the platform stored it); the **event time** is whichever the topic-7 decision names, and every topic after 7 says which it means.
- **A series** is one metric name plus one label set (Prometheus's data model, quoted in topic 12); a **sample** is a (timestamp, value) pair.
- **Signal types:** a **continuous** signal (temperature) is interpolated between samples; a **step** (state) signal holds its value until the next change; a **counter** only increases (an energy meter), with resets. Topic 8 owns the distinction and every later topic uses its words.
- **Error band `ε`:** the tolerated reconstruction error of a lossy compressor (topic 18's swinging door, topic 16's deadband).
- **Units are written in SI** (W, kW, kWh, J) and a topic says the unit of every number on screen.
- **Spoken numbers:** narration has no digits, symbols or code names (same automatic check as Chapters 1-5).
- **Versions to state, not assume:** DuckDB, pandas, PostgreSQL, Prometheus (the docs checked: the 3.x line, 2-hour blocks), TimescaleDB, InfluxDB 3 Core (3.12 at planning), MQTT 5.0, Sparkplug 3.0 (ISO/IEC 20237:2023), OPC UA Part 1 v1.05.06 (22 October 2025), IEC 63278-1:2023 and IDTA AAS specifications v3.x.

### 1. ISA-95 hierarchy and the automation pyramid (`de.isa-95-hierarchy-automation`): full, §16.1

- **Scope:** "enterprise → site → area → line/cell → equipment → sensor; levels 0-4 (sensors, PLC/SCADA, MES, ERP) and which system owns which data; the equipment hierarchy as the dimension every telemetry fact joins to, and how it changes over time (re-tooling, moved assets)" - the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *enterprise → site → area → line/cell → equipment → sensor:* the physical hierarchy as a tree and as a path; the course notation (above) is the syllabus's generic path. The **formal role-based equipment names of ISA-95 Part 2 (IEC 62264-2) are confirmed from the standard at research time and stated with their source**; the topic does not assert them from memory, and says plainly where the course's generic names differ.
  - *levels 0-4 (sensors, PLC/SCADA, MES, ERP) and which system owns which data:* ISA's own description of the model: level 0 physical processes, level 1 sensing and manipulating (sensors, smart devices, valves), level 2 monitoring and supervising (PLCs, DCSs and other control devices), level 3 manufacturing operations management (MES, and, as ISA's page words it, SCADA), level 4 business planning and logistics (ERP); ISA states the levels divide activities "depending on the time horizon of the functionalities" (microsecond-to-millisecond control cycles at the bottom, weeks and days at the top) and that the standard "primarily deals with the interface between levels 3 and 4". **A naming trap the topic states:** ISA's page places SCADA at level 3 while many practitioner diagrams put supervisory SCADA at level 2; the topic quotes the primary source, shows both placements, and teaches the stable idea (each level owns data with a different time horizon and a different system of record). It also states that ISA itself says the hierarchy is a functional model and, in the Industry 4.0 setting, the activities can be implemented in a network architecture without losing compliance.
  - *the equipment hierarchy as the dimension every telemetry fact joins to:* the hierarchy is a **dimension** in the Chapter 3 sense (`conformed-dimension`) that every reading, event, inspection and meter joins to; the grain of a telemetry fact is (sensor, time); the join key is a **durable equipment key**, not the tag name (`key-types-natural-surrogate-durable-hash`).

### 3. Quality data and SPC (`de.quality-data-spc`): full, §16.1

- **Folder:** `quality-data-spc`. About 15 min.
- **Scope:** "inspection measurements, specification limits, control charts and Western Electric/Nelson rules, Cp/Cpk; non-conformance and CAPA records; why quality facts must carry the exact process context (recipe version, tool, shift) to be analyzable" - the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *inspection measurements, specification limits:* an inspection fact (a measurement, the characteristic measured, the unit, the sample, the inspector or gauge), and the **specification limits** (USL, LSL) that say what the *customer* needs; NIST's handbook states the distinction the topic hangs everything on: "Control Limits are used to determine if the process is in a state of statistical control (i.e., is producing consistent output). Specification Limits are used to determine if the product will function in the intended fashion."
  - *control charts and Western Electric/Nelson rules:* Shewhart's model (centre line the process mean, control limits at plus and minus k standard deviations with the 3-sigma convention); the **WECO rules** as NIST states them (one point beyond 3 sigma; two of three beyond 2 sigma; four of five beyond 1 sigma; eight in a row on one side; trend rules), the probability reasoning behind them (about 0.3 percent for a point outside plus or minus 3 sigma) and the **price**: NIST's figure that false alarms occur about every 371 points under the 3-sigma rule and about every 91.75 points when the WECO rules are added; the **Nelson rules** as the eight-rule variant (the rule list is confirmed from Nelson's original paper, located at research time, with the Wikipedia summary used only to find it; the eight rules include nine points on one side of the mean, six in a row trending, fourteen alternating) and where they differ from WECO.
  - *Cp/Cpk:* the capability indices as NIST defines them (Cp = (USL - LSL)/(6 sigma); Cpk = min[(USL - mu)/(3 sigma), (mu - LSL)/(3 sigma)]), what each says (spread against tolerance, then centring), and the traps: they presuppose a stable, in-control process, they are estimates with their own sampling error, and a high Cp with a low Cpk is a centring problem, not a spread problem.
  - *non-conformance and CAPA records:* a **non-conformance** (what failed, which lot, which limit) and a **CAPA** (corrective and preventive action: cause, action, owner, due date, verification) as the records a quality system keeps; the data-model point is that they **reference** lots, runs and inspections by key.
  - *why quality facts must carry the exact process context (recipe version, tool, shift) to be analyzable:* a measurement without the recipe version, the tool, the shift and the lot cannot be stratified, so a drift cannot be attributed; the topic shows a control chart that looks out of control until it is split by tool and then shows two in-control processes with different means (a **stratification** result computed in `examples.py`).

### 11. Time-series compression (`de.time-series-compression`): full, §16.2

- **Folder:** `time-series-compression`. About 18 min. **Hosts the compressed-size leg of the §16.2 lab and the Gorilla "Systems in Production" case study.**

  - *delta-of-delta timestamps and XOR-encoded floats (Gorilla):* **Gorilla** (Pelkonen et al., VLDB 2015) is the Milestone 7 case study: Facebook's monitoring needed recent data in memory, held at 16 bytes per point it would have taken 16 TB of RAM, so it compressed to an average of **1.37 bytes per point, a 12x reduction**, with reads that "succeed in under one millisecond" and a 73x lower query latency than the HBase-backed store, as the paper reports; **timestamps**: store the delta of deltas (for deltas 60, 60, 59, 61 the delta of deltas is 0, -1, 2), with a one-bit '0' for zero, '10' plus 7 bits for [-63, 64], '110' plus 9 bits for [-255, 256], '1110' plus 12 bits for [-2047, 2048], and **about 96 percent of timestamps compress to a single bit**; **values**: XOR with the previous value, a single '0' bit when identical, and otherwise a control bit then the leading-zero count and the meaningful bits, **roughly 51 percent of values compress to a single bit**; the topic's worked figure of the paper's own example (a delta of delta of -2 stored in 9 bits; an XOR with one meaningful bit stored in 14) is reproduced exactly; the trade-offs the paper accepted (in-memory cost; lossy regional failover) are stated, and its codecs' legacy in Prometheus (Chapter 16 topic 13) is the pointer. **The topic builds a working Gorilla-style encoder in `examples.py` and measures it** rather than quoting the 12x as a promise.

### 18. Process historians and swinging-door compression (`de.process-historians-swinging-door`): full, §16.3

- **Folder:** `process-historians-swinging-door`. About 18 min. **Hosts the swinging-door leg of the §16.3 lab.**
- **Scope:** "historians (e.g. AVEVA PI System) store tags with exception (deadband) filtering then swinging-door trending compression that keeps only points needed to reconstruct within an error band; interpolated vs recorded value retrieval; extracting historian data into a lakehouse without losing compression semantics" - the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *historians (e.g. AVEVA PI System) store tags with exception (deadband) filtering then swinging-door trending compression:* a **process historian** is a time-series store built for plant data (decades old, before the TSDBs of §16.2); in the PI Data Archive's own model (an AVEVA user-conference deck, 2023): **exception** is "a simple deadband to filter noise" performed at the interface, which "reduces events sent to Data Archive Snapshot" and "conserves network bandwidth"; **compression** then uses a "swinging door" algorithm "to eliminate data that can safely be recreated with interpolation", whose goal the deck states as "to filter out instrument and process noise and still record significant process changes", with per-tag **CompDev** (compression deviation) and CompMax/CompMin attributes and a note that **out-of-order data bypasses compression**; the **exact algorithm details and the relation of ExcDev to CompDev (a documented recommendation that exception deviation be well below compression deviation) are confirmed from AVEVA's PI documentation, and the topic does not state either from memory** (the AVEVA documentation pages are client-side rendered and could not be fetched at planning, so the research step fetches the AVEVA PDF or an alternative primary source). **PI is sourced-only** in the sandbox.
  - *swinging-door trending compression that keeps only points needed to reconstruct within an error band:* the algorithm (Bristol's swinging door trending, a published technique), taught from the description and **implemented from it in `examples.py`**: anchor at the last stored point, open two "doors" (the upper and lower bounds of slope that stay within `±ε` of every point seen since), keep the new point **only when the doors' slopes cross**, i.e. when no single straight line from the anchor stays within `ε` of all the points since; the stored points are the **knees** of a piecewise-linear approximation; the guarantee (**every discarded point is within `ε` of the interpolated line**) is **verified** by the code, along with the error against a deadband-only filter at the same point count.
  - *interpolated vs recorded value retrieval:* a historian query asks either for **recorded values** (the points actually stored: after compression these are the knees, unevenly spaced) or for **interpolated values** at a regular interval (computed between knees), and the two give **different answers for the same question**; an average over recorded values is **not** a time-weighted average (the points are not uniformly informative), which is why historians offer **time-weighted** retrieval; the topic reproduces the bias of averaging recorded knees against the true time-weighted mean; for a **step** signal (topic 8) interpolation must be a step, and compression must be configured accordingly.
  - *extracting historian data into a lakehouse without losing compression semantics:* if you extract **recorded** values you inherit the compression and **its error band and uneven spacing** (a flat period has few points, which a naive mean over rows misreads); if you extract **interpolated** values you create points that were never recorded; the right extraction **carries the compression metadata** (the deviation setting, a flag that the series is compressed, the quality status) and lands the values as an **irregular series** with a recorded/interpolated flag so that topic 8's `filled-value-is-a-claim` holds; the **lossy-before-lake** consequence is stated: you cannot recover detail the historian discarded, so the compression setting is a permanent analytic decision made by someone else (topic 6's ownership).
- **Leaves out:** exception reporting at the OPC UA subscription (topic 16's deadband, mentioned as the same idea); lossless codecs (topic 11); the historian's archive file structure and licensing; PI AF asset framework in depth (the asset model is topic 20 and topic 1); other historians (named only, claims sourced).
- **Outcomes:** Apply a deadband filter and then swinging-door compression with a stated `ε` to a synthetic temperature series and measure the point reduction and the maximum and RMS reconstruction error, confirming the `ε` guarantee; show that recorded-value averages differ from time-weighted averages on compressed data; design a historian-to-lakehouse extraction that carries compression metadata and flags interpolated points; state what cannot be recovered.
