5. **"Application writers should note that these abbreviations are ambiguous in practice: e.g., CST means one thing in China and something else in North America, and IST can refer to time in India, Ireland or Israel. None of the input numbers (the twenty-reading fleet, the offsets, the 2026 calendar dates) are a measurement of any real device; CHAPTER.md's own rule treats figures like these as this chapter's fictional illustration.

## Things initially gotten wrong, and the correction

- The first version of the spring-forward and fall-back `examples.py` cases used the transition day itself as the shift's start date, which produced a plain 8-hour result for both — Europe/Berlin's transitions happen at 02:00 local, hours after a shift starting at 22:00 on that same calendar date would already be running, so that shift never crosses the transition. Running the script and getting 8.0 instead of the expected 7.0 caught this; corrected by starting each shift the evening *before* the transition date, which is also the realistic case (a night shift spans midnight, so the transition always falls inside the previous day's shift).
- The naive-vs-correct OEE percentage in step 7 was deliberately computed, not asserted from CHAPTER.md's own "114 percent" figure: `examples.py` divides a hard-coded constant by the real, tz-database-derived 7-hour shift length and gets `114.29` on its own, which happens to match CHAPTER.md's hook number — a stronger form of the claim than hand-asserting that exact figure would have been.

## Red team

- **Does "measured clock offsets" (RFC 5905) actually support this topic's device-to-gateway offset measurement, or is that a different kind of offset?** Checked: RFC 5905's quoted sentence is about NTP's own protocol design (comparing a local clock against a time source over the network), the same general concept this topic's `transit_delays()` measures (one clock's reading against another's), but the topic does not claim RFC 5905 specifies this exact device-to-gateway measurement — it is cited only for the "offset" framing, and the measurement itself is `examples.py`'s own code, confirmed in `SOURCES.md`'s "Contents" line.
- **Does OPC UA's "source timestamp never changes once assigned" claim conflict with this topic's NTP-step example, where a device timestamp effectively gets corrected?** No: OPC UA's guarantee is about a *given value instance*'s own timestamp being immutable after assignment, not about a device's clock never being corrected between readings. The NTP step in this topic changes what time the *next* reading's device clock reports, not the stored timestamp of a reading already assigned one. The two claims are compatible and about different things; checked they are never conflated in `reel.tsx` or `narration.json`.
- **Could the "naive OEE percentage exceeds 100 percent" result be an artifact of which two hours were chosen (22:00-06:00), rather than a general truth about DST shifts?** No — the mechanism is general: any shift whose true length is shorter than the constant it's divided by (here, 7 real hours against a hard-coded 8) produces a ratio over 1.0, regardless of which two clock hours bound the shift. The specific hours (22:00, 06:00) were kept only to match CHAPTER.md's own Munich hook scenario, not because the result depends on them.
- **Is naming IEEE 1588-2019 by its IEEE SA page's abstract defensible, given IEEE Xplore's own abstract page returned empty on a direct fetch?** Checked: the standards.ieee.org page quoted is IEEE's own standards-association product page for this exact standard (confirmed by its heading "IEEE 1588-2019" and PAR-approval metadata matching the standard's known history), not a reseller or third-party summary; the IEEE Xplore page's failure to render was a client-side rendering issue on that different page, not a reason to doubt the text actually fetched from IEEE SA's own domain.
