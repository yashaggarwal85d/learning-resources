# OUTLINE: Quality data and SPC (`de.quality-data-spc`)

## Learning outcomes
1. State the difference between control limits and specification limits, and show a real measurement that is in control and out of spec at once.
2. Apply the Western Electric zone rules to a seeded series and measure the real false-alarm rate with and without them, against NIST's own stated figures.
3. Measure the real, further cost of adding the eighth zone rule (eight consecutive points on one side), and disclose that it is not folded into NIST's headline figure.
4. Compute Cp and Cpk for a centred and an off-centre process of identical spread, and state why a high Cp with a low Cpk is a centring problem, not a spread problem.
5. Show a stratification that turns one out-of-control chart into two honestly in-control ones, and model inspection, non-conformance and CAPA records keyed to lots, runs and recipe versions.

## Changes from the chapter plan
CHAPTER.md asks for "the Nelson rules ... confirmed from Nelson's original paper, located at research time, with the Wikipedia summary used only to find it." A dedicated search (see FACT-CHECK.md's red team) confirmed the 1984 paper (DOI 10.1080/00224065.1984.11978921) sits behind a Taylor & Francis paywall with no legitimately fetchable full text, PDF mirror, or securely-attributed verbatim quote anywhere found — the one near-miss (SAS's documentation) explicitly attributes its quoted phrases to Nelson's separate 1985 JQT paper, not the 1984 one, so it cannot stand in. This topic therefore names Nelson's eight-rule superset as a historical fact (existence, general shape, numbered-test convention), sourced to secondary consensus, and does not assert any verbatim text of the 1984 paper — exactly the caveat CHAPTER.md's own allowance for secondary sourcing covers when a primary source is genuinely inaccessible. The topic's own measured false-alarm figures come entirely from WECO's four zone rules (confirmed verbatim from NIST), not from Nelson's rules, so this substitution affects only the reel's one sentence naming Nelson, not any computed claim.

A second, more interesting change: NIST's headline "91.75" false-alarm figure, confirmed by direct Monte Carlo simulation, turns out to match combining only rules 1 ("1 point beyond 3 sigma"), 2 ("2 of 3 beyond 2 sigma") and 3 ("4 of 5 beyond 1 sigma") — adding rule 4 ("8 consecutive on one side") on top measurably shortens the average run length further (roughly in half, in this simulation), rather than leaving NIST's figure unchanged. This is disclosed as a real finding (not hidden or smoothed over), and is itself a teaching point: the eight-rules-and-two-trend-rules list NIST draws is not committed to a single, fixed "headline" false-alarm number once you choose which subset to combine.

## Reel steps (storyboard)

1. **Hook** — fig: a coating-thickness chart trips a run rule four times in a week and supervisors stop believing it; split by tool, it's two machines, each perfectly in control, centred apart by a real offset. Visual: two Rows, "the chart" / "trips, again" fading to "split by tool" / "two in-control machines?". Narrator opens with the moment supervisors start ignoring a chart that's crying wolf, not with the definition of a control chart.
2. **Reveal: control limits vs. specification limits** — fig: control limits ask "is this process behaving the way it usually does"; specification limits ask "will this part actually work." Visual: two Rows, "control limits" / "is the process stable" and "spec limits" / "will the part work." Narrator's line: these are two different questions about the same number, and a process can answer one well and the other badly.
3. **Reveal: a single inspection, in control and out of spec** — fig: the real computed example — one coating measurement sits inside the process's own historical band, yet above the customer's own upper limit. Visual: a Row showing "in control" true, a Row showing "in spec" false, same measurement. Takeaway: "A single real measurement sat inside the process's own control limits while sitting outside the customer's specification limits, at the same time."
4. **Predict** — "Western Electric's extra zone rules catch patterns a lone three-sigma check misses — two of three past the second band, four of five past the first. Does adding them to a chart ever cost anything?" Options: No, more rules just mean more drift caught, for free / Yes, a chart that signals on more patterns will also signal falsely more often (correct). Explain: every rule that's sensitive enough to catch a real drift sooner is also sensitive enough to misfire on a run of ordinary noise sooner.
5. **Reveal: the measured price, matching NIST** — fig: simulated false-alarm points between signals, three-sigma alone against the three WECO zone rules added, matching NIST's own 371-and-91.75 figures almost exactly. Visual: two Rows, "three-sigma alone" / "~371 points" fading into "with the zone rules" / "~92 points." Takeaway: "Adding Western Electric's zone rules cut the measured false-alarm gap from about 371 points to about 91.75, matching NIST's own figures almost exactly."
6. **Reveal: the eighth rule's own, separate cost** — fig: adding the fourth zone rule (eight consecutive points on one side) on top shortens the gap again, measurably, in this same simulation. Visual: a third Row, "add the eighth-rule" / "shorter again," in focus colour. Takeaway: "Adding the eighth zone rule caught more drift but shortened the false-alarm gap again — a real, measured cost, not a bug to smooth away."
7. **Reveal: Cp and Cpk, the centring trap** — fig: two synthetic processes with the identical spread; one centred on the spec midpoint, one shifted toward the upper limit; their Cp values are close, but the shifted one's Cpk trails its own Cp by a real margin. Visual: two Rows per process, Cp then Cpk, the shifted process's Cpk in focus colour. Takeaway: "A high Cp with a low Cpk is a centring problem, not a spread problem — the exact failure the two figures are built to expose."
8. **Reveal: one chart, split by tool, becomes two in-control charts** — fig: the mixed series trips several WECO rules; split by tool, each tool's own series trips far fewer. Visual: "mixed" / "trips rules" fading into "split by tool" / "each stays in control." Takeaway: "Splitting the same chart by tool turned one out-of-control signal into two honestly in-control processes."
9. **Reveal: non-conformances and CAPAs, keyed to the exact process context** — fig: the inspection that was out of spec creates a non-conformance record, which a CAPA record references by key, with its own cause, action, owner and due date; none of this is analyzable without the run's own recipe version, tool and shift attached to the inspection. Visual: three Rows, "inspection" / "measurement + context," "non-conformance" / "keyed to the inspection," "CAPA" / "keyed to the non-conformance." Takeaway (optional extra, if room): a quality fact without its recipe version, tool and shift can be flagged but never stratified.
10. **Recap** — control limits against spec limits, the WECO price measured and matched to NIST, the eighth rule's own further cost, the Cp/Cpk centring trap, and a chart that becomes two in-control ones once it's split by tool.
11. **Prove it** — "A supervisor silences the chart's zone-rule alerts, keeping only the lone three-sigma check, because the zone rules 'cry wolf too often.' Does turning them off make the chart's three-sigma signal any less trustworthy than it already was?" Options: Yes, fewer rules watching means the remaining one is weaker / No, the three-sigma rule's own false-alarm rate doesn't depend on which other rules are switched on beside it (correct).

## Examples to execute and assert

## Claims list
1. Control limits determine if the process is in a state of statistical control; spec limits determine if the product will function as intended — NIST pmc32.htm. Confirmed verbatim.
2. WECO stands for Western Electric Company Rules — NIST pmc32.htm. Confirmed verbatim.
3. The WECO rules are based on probability (about 0.3% for a point beyond 3 sigma) — NIST pmc32.htm. Confirmed verbatim.
4. The 3-sigma rule alone gives false alarms every 371 points on average; adding the WECO rules increases that to about every 91.75 points (Champ and Woodall, 1987, as NIST cites it) — NIST pmc32.htm. Confirmed verbatim.
5. Cp = (USL-LSL)/(6 sigma); Cpk = min[(USL-xbar)/(3s), (xbar-LSL)/(3s)] — NIST pmc16.htm. Confirmed verbatim (formulas).
6. Cpk <= Cp always — NIST pmc16.htm. Confirmed verbatim.
7. Nelson's eight rules exist as a named historical superset of the WECO zone-and-trend tests, codified by Lloyd S. Nelson in a 1984 Journal of Quality Technology column — bibliographic fact only; the primary paper is paywalled and could not be fetched or quoted (confirmed again by a dedicated search; see FACT-CHECK.md). Not asserted verbatim.
8. The measured false-alarm figures (ARL for rule 1 alone, rules 1+2+3, and all four rules) and the inspection/non-conformance/CAPA demonstration — this topic's own `examples.py`, executed and asserted.
