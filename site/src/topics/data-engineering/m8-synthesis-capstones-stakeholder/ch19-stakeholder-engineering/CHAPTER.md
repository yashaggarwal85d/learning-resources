## The chapter

### The problem it solves

Chapters 1–18 ended every topic with something an engineer could verify: a schema, a query plan, a benchmark number. None of that, by itself, tells anyone outside engineering whether the right thing was built, what it will cost to keep a promise about it, or how to say "we were slower than you wanted, and here is why" without the conversation turning into blame. This chapter teaches the other half of shipping a data product: turning a vague ask into a contract both sides can hold each other to, pricing the trade-off a stakeholder is actually choosing between, writing the decision down so it survives the person who made it leaving the room, and talking about a failure in a way that gets it fixed instead of hidden. This closes Milestone 8 and the course's own technical arc; the Systems-in-Production section and its exercise (not reel topics) are the milestone's own closing reading.

### Order of ideas, and why

1. **Negotiating data contracts and SLOs (topic 1).** Opens, as Chapter 18's handoff promised, with the first piece of the non-technical half: an agreement a producer and a consumer can both point to. A contract is only honest if each tier it offers has a real, stated cost and a named owner for what happens when it breaks, so this topic is where "done" first gets a measurable definition.
2. **Communicating trade-offs to decision-makers (topic 2).** A contract's own tiers are a trade-off curve; this topic is how that curve gets explained to someone who will never read a percentile, in the vocabulary (cost per tier, the dominant lever, a one-page summary before the deep dive) a decision-maker actually uses to choose.
3. **Design docs, RFCs and ADRs (topic 3).** A negotiated contract and a trade-off explained once are still only as durable as the paper trail behind them; this topic is how the decision itself gets written down so it survives past the meeting it was made in, and how a later team tells a live decision from one that was quietly superseded.
4. **Blameless post-mortems and incident communication (topic 4).** Closes the chapter, and the course, with what happens when a contract from topic 1 gets broken anyway: naming the impact in the data terms topic 2 taught, writing it down the way topic 3 taught, without naming a person to blame for it.

### One running example: a vague request for real-time dashboards

Same fictional online shop as Chapters 1–18 (no real company, no real person), now stepping outside engineering into the conversation around it. A plant-operations stakeholder asks for "real-time production dashboards for all plants" with no further detail, and each topic turns that one vague ask into one more piece of a real, defensible answer:

- **Topic 1** turns "real-time" into three priced SLO tiers (e.g. five-minute, one-hour and next-day freshness) the stakeholder can actually choose between, each with a named owner for a breach.
- **Topic 2** writes the one-page executive summary and the cost-per-tier comparison that gets the stakeholder to actually pick one, naming the dominant cost lever (the thing that makes the fast tier expensive) in plain business terms.
- **Topic 3** writes the requirements doc and the ADR recording which tier was chosen, why the alternatives were rejected, and what would make the team revisit the decision later.
- **Topic 4** writes a blameless post-mortem for a simulated incident where the chosen tier's own data went stale for an hour, quantifying the impact in affected plants and decisions, not server metrics.

Every artifact in this chapter is a worked example written out in full inside the topic's own `post-notes.md` or a dedicated lab file, stated plainly as a disclosed, classroom-scale exercise, never presented as a real company's actual internal document.

### One visual language for the chapter's reels

Built from `site/src/reels/kit.tsx` and `DESIGN.md`'s briefing theme (black and white carry everything; colour only for failure, pale blue only for a second series). The chapter shares three conventions:

- **The tier ladder** (topic 1; reused in 2): three stacked bars of differing height and shade (fast/expensive at top, slow/cheap at bottom), each labeled with its own freshness and a small cost tag that **Count**-s up as the tier gets faster.
- **The one-pager, then the deep dive** (topic 2; reused in 3): a single, short block of text (the executive summary) **Decode**-d away to reveal a far longer, denser block behind it (the engineering deep dive), making the size difference between the two audiences' own documents visible rather than described.
- **The paper trail** (topic 3; reused in 4): a short vertical stack of dated cards (a design doc, an RFC comment, an ADR), with a later card's own **Reticle** pointing back at an earlier one it supersedes rather than replacing it outright.
- Every topic keeps the full-topic reel shape of earlier chapters (hook, a prediction within the first four steps, mechanism in steps, at least three takeaways, recap, "Prove it").

### 2. Communicating trade-offs to decision-makers (`de.communicating-tradeoffs-decision-makers`): full, §19.1

- **Folder:** `communicating-tradeoffs-decision-makers`. About 15 min.
- **Scope:** "translating cost/latency/correctness trade-offs into business terms (cost per freshness tier, the price of a wrong number); naming the dominant cost lever; justifying 'boring' choices; bottom-line-up-front executive summaries vs engineering deep-dives for the same decision" — the full syllabus line, clause by clause:
  - *translating cost/latency/correctness trade-offs into business terms (cost per freshness tier, the price of a wrong number):* takes topic 1's own three priced tiers and restates each one's cost in the terms a decision-maker weighs (dollars, risk of a wrong number reaching a plant manager), not the terms that built it (compute, storage, pipeline complexity).
  - *naming the dominant cost lever:* for the shop's own three tiers, names explicitly which single factor (e.g. how often a full recomputation runs) drives most of the cost difference between the fast tier and the others, rather than listing every contributing factor as equally important.
  - *justifying "boring" choices:* states, and defends in business terms, why the medium tier — not the flashiest, fastest option — is very often the right one to recommend, the same "boring technology" instinct engineers have to translate for an audience that doesn't share it by default.
  - *bottom-line-up-front executive summaries vs engineering deep-dives for the same decision:* cited from a real, documented practice of writing a short, conclusion-first summary for decision-makers and a separate, much longer document for the engineering review of the identical decision — the topic's own worked example writes both, for the same tier recommendation, side by side.
- **Leaves out:** proving data-platform value through usage telemetry and adoption metrics *(skipped)*, which this topic assumes is a separate, later conversation from recommending one tier for one specific request.
- **Outcomes:** Restate a priced SLO tier's own cost in business rather than engineering terms; name the one dominant cost lever behind a stated price difference; write a one-page, bottom-line-up-front executive summary and a separate engineering deep dive recommending the same tier, and state what belongs in each.
- **Introduces:** `cost-per-freshness-tier`, `dominant-cost-lever`, `executive-summary-vs-deep-dive`. **Uses:** `slo-tiering`, `data-contract` (topic 1). **Declared:** none beyond topic 1's own declared knowledge.
- **Bridge:** "Three priced tiers on a cost curve are a negotiation finished, not a decision made — someone outside engineering still has to actually pick one, and this topic is how that choice gets explained to them in the terms they use to decide, not the terms that built the curve." (Answers topic 1's handoff.)
- **Handoff:** "A tier gets chosen, and the one-page summary that got it chosen is read once and then forgotten the moment the meeting ends. The decision itself — which tier, why, and what would change the team's mind later — still has to survive the person who made it leaving the room, which is the next topic's own subject."
- **Prerequisites panel:** topic 1 (Before this). **Pre-notes terms:** cost lever, bottom-line-up-front, executive summary, deep dive.
- **Hook:** two documents recommend the exact same decision — the medium freshness tier — to two different audiences; the one-page version gets the budget approved in a five-minute meeting, while the twelve-page version sent to the same meeting by mistake gets the decision tabled for two weeks.
- **Visual idea:** the one-pager, then the deep dive (a single short block of text **Decode**-d away to reveal the far longer, denser engineering document behind it), making the size difference between the two documents the visual point rather than a stated fact.
