# Fact-check — Inmon vs Kimball warehouse philosophies

| # | Claim | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Inmon created the accepted definition of a data warehouse: a subject-oriented, non-volatile, integrated, time-variant collection of data in support of management's decisions. | Confirmed, quoted directly (Wikipedia, "Bill Inmon"). |
| 2 | Compared with Kimball, Inmon's approach is often characterized as top-down. | Confirmed, quoted directly (same source). |
| 3 | Dimensional modeling is part of the Business Dimensional Lifecycle methodology developed by Kimball, which focuses on identifying key business processes and modeling them first, as a bottom-up approach. | Confirmed, quoted directly (Wikipedia, "Dimensional modeling", citing Connolly & Begg). |
| 4 | An alternative approach from Inmon advocates top-down design of the model of all enterprise data using tools such as ER modeling. | Confirmed, quoted directly (same source). |
| 5 | Conformed dimensions achieve efficiency and consistency across business process areas by using one copy of a shared dimension across subject areas. | Confirmed, quoted directly (same source, tagged citation-needed on Wikipedia itself — still Wikipedia's own stated claim, standard and uncontested dimensional-modeling vocabulary). |
| 6 | The enterprise data warehouse bus architecture provides an incremental approach to building the enterprise DW/BI system, decomposing planning by business process while delivering integration via standardized conformed dimensions. | Confirmed, quoted directly (Kimball Group's own site). |
| 7 | The bus architecture is technology and database platform independent; both relational and OLAP dimensional structures can participate. | Confirmed, quoted directly (same source). |
| 8 | Historically ETL made more sense because compute, storage and bandwidth were expensive; cloud data warehouses (Snowflake, Redshift, BigQuery) drove down storage cost and increased processing power, making ELT (post-load transform) viable. | Confirmed, quoted directly (Fivetran blog). |
| 9 | This shift made it much less of a concern to store raw data in the warehouse, opening up post-load transformation as an alternative to pre-load transformation. | Confirmed, quoted directly (same source). |
| 10 | Time to value favors Kimball's incremental, process-first approach for a single urgent deadline and source system; integration cost favors Inmon's single normalized model when many source systems must agree on shared entities; tolerance to change is genuinely contested between the two, each having a real claim. | Reasoned, not independently sourced: this is the topic's own synthesis of the two methodologies' well-documented, sourced characteristics (top-down vs. bottom-up, incremental vs. upfront) applied to three named scenarios, not itself a quotable external claim. | 11 | ELT on a columnar engine narrows, rather than erases, the original Inmon-vs-Kimball tradeoff, since cheap post-load transforms let a team start with incremental marts and fold in integration work later. | Reasoned: a direct synthesis of claims 8–9 (the ELT economics shift) connected to claims 1–7 (the original methodologies' tradeoffs), since no single fetched source makes this exact connection by name; the chapter's own CHAPTER.md scope asks for this specific synthesis. |
