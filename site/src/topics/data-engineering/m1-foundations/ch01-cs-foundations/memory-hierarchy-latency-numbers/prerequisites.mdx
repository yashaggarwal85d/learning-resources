### <span class="n">00</span> Basics: the terms this reel uses

- **Latency (α)**: the wait before a request's first byte arrives. *Where you meet it:* 80 µs for a random NVMe read.
- **Bandwidth (β)**: bytes per second once the transfer flows. *Where you meet it:* 6,800 MB/s from an NVMe drive.
- **T(n) = α + n/β**: one transfer costs a wait plus the bytes at bandwidth. *Where you meet it:* below α·β bytes, a read is mostly waiting.
- **Memory hierarchy**: tiers from registers through RAM, drives and the network to object storage, each slower and larger. *Where you meet it:* "which tier is this job reading from?"
- **Clock cycle**: one tick of the CPU clock, 0.5 ns at 2 GHz. *Where you meet it:* manuals give cache latencies in cycles.
- **Cache (L1, L2, L3)**: small, fast memories on the CPU holding recent data. *Where you meet it:* a loop whose data fits in L2 runs far faster.
- **DRAM**: main memory, about 100 ns for a read that goes all the way there. *Where you meet it:* "the table is cached in memory".
- **NUMA node**: one socket's own memory; another node's is slower to reach. *Where you meet it:* a thread on one socket, its data on the other.
- **Requests in flight (queue depth, k)**: requests outstanding at once; their waits overlap. *Where you meet it:* NVMe drives need dozens in flight to reach rated speed.
- **Rotational latency**: a hard disk's wait for the platter to turn, 4.16 ms on average at 7,200 rpm. *Where you meet it:* random disk reads.
- **Same-AZ round trip**: a message to a machine in the same cloud data centre and back. *Where you meet it:* an app server calling its database.
- **First byte (object storage)**: the wait before an object or byte range starts arriving over HTTP. *Where you meet it:* a lakehouse reading Parquet from a bucket.
- **Floor**: the least time a job can take, max(bytes / β, requests × α / k); the larger term names the bottleneck. *Where you meet it:* checking a job before running it.
- **Log scale**: equal steps are equal multiples, so nanoseconds and seconds share one axis. *Where you meet it:* every latency chart in this chapter.

### <span class="n">01</span> Big-O and logarithms, and reading a log axis

The reel's charts are logarithmic: each tick is ten (or a thousand) times the one before. Big-O appears once, to say that two walks over an array both do the same number of steps, Θ(n), and still differ in time. If a log axis or Θ(n) is new, start with the refresher before this one.

<p class="check"><b>Check yourself:</b> on an axis with ticks 1 ns, 1 µs, 1 ms and 1 s, how many times longer is 1 s than 1 ns? (Answer: a billion, three ticks of a thousand each.)</p>

### <span class="n">02</span> Bits, bytes and binary prefixes

Network rates are quoted in bits per second, so divide by 8 for bytes: 10 Gbit/s is 1.25 GB/s. File sizes here are binary (1 GiB = 1,073,741,824 bytes, 1 TiB = 2<sup>40</sup> bytes), while vendors quote decimal rates (Samsung: 1 MB/s = 1,000,000 bytes/s). Mixing them is a 7% error per prefix step, which is small next to the gaps in this reel but matters when you compare a measurement with a datasheet.

<p class="check"><b>Check yourself:</b> how many GB/s is 100 Gbit/s? (Answer: 12.5.)</p>

### <span class="n">03</span> The running example

The chapter's table is <code>orders</code>: 1,000,000 pages of 8 KB in PostgreSQL, 8,192,000,000 bytes in all. <code>order_events</code> is 1 TiB of events, kept in object storage and also on a local NVMe drive for the nightly job. Every estimate in the reel is about one of the two.

<p class="check"><b>Check yourself:</b> how many GiB is 1,000,000 pages of 8,192 bytes? (Answer: about 7.6.)</p>
