## The chapter

### The problem it solves

Every algorithm in Milestone 2 came with a guarantee: here is a procedure, here is its cost. This chapter is about the other kind of knowledge, the knowledge that no such procedure is known and that, short of one of the great open problems, none will be found. A senior engineer meets it as a month spent on a clever search that was never going to scale: an exact join order for forty tables, a perfect packing of files into splits, a schedule that minimises the finishing time, a register assignment. The skill is to *recognise the hard problem on sight*, to know what "hard" precisely claims, and to know what you may still do (Chapter 34's approximation, Chapter 35's structure, Chapter 23's pruning, a heuristic). Two ideas carry it. First, **a problem is easy to *check* long before it is easy to *solve***: the class NP is the set of yes/no questions for which a yes can be verified quickly given a certificate, and the whole theory of intractability is about the gap between verifying and finding. Second, **hardness is transferred by reduction**: a fast algorithm for problem B would give a fast algorithm for problem A, so B is at least as hard as A. The direction of that sentence is the most-botched thing in the subject, and topic 107 is built around getting it right. Topic 108 then removes a second, subtler misunderstanding: that a problem with numbers in it is hard or easy depending on *how the numbers are written*, which is why knapsack (NP-complete) has a fast-looking dynamic program while bin packing (also NP-complete) does not, and why "polynomial in the input size" has to be read with the encoding in hand.

### Order of ideas, and why

1. **§33.1 P, NP and polynomial reductions (107).** The vocabulary and the one proof technique, together, because neither is usable without the other: the three flavours of problem (decision, search, optimisation) and why the theory works on the first; P and NP defined by *a procedure that finds* against *a procedure that checks*; the certificate and the verifier; the Karp reduction as a polynomial-time map f with x ∈ A ⇔ f(x) ∈ B; its direction (to show B is hard, reduce *from* a known-hard problem A *to* B); and the fact that bootstraps everything (Cook–Levin gives a first NP-hard problem; the skipped topic builds the chain of gadgets). One worked reduction is shown end to end (independent set and vertex cover, a one-line map that can be checked by exhaustion), and the syllabus lab (3-SAT to independent set) is in the post-notes.
2. **§33.1 Pseudo-polynomial time and strong NP-hardness (108).** After the framework, the first place it bites back. A running time polynomial in a number's *value* is exponential in its *length*, and NP-hardness is defined against length. Knapsack is NP-complete and has an O(nW) dynamic program, so its hardness lives entirely in W being written in binary; bin packing stays NP-complete when every number is bounded by a polynomial in the input size, so no such dynamic program can exist unless P = NP. That distinction (weak and strong NP-hardness, and the 3-partition problem that anchors the strong side) is the one a practitioner uses when reading a numeric problem: "are my numbers small, or only my instance?".

### Shared notation and terms

- **Instance, decision problem, language:** an *instance* is one input; a *decision problem* maps instances to yes or no. The chapter says "problem" and "instance" and never writes the formal word *language*.
- **A ≤ₚ B (A reduces to B):** there is a polynomial-time function f, computable on any instance of A, with x ∈ A ⇔ f(x) ∈ B. Written "A reduces to B", never "B reduces to A" for the same sentence; the visual shows the arrow pointing *from the known-hard problem to the new one*, and the reel never reuses an arrow's meaning. Karp reduction = many-one reduction (Erickson, read at planning time, defines it as such and calls Turing reductions "Cook reductions"; the topic names Cook once, as the more permissive kind, and uses Karp throughout).
- **NP-hard, NP-complete:** NP-hard: at least as hard as every problem in NP (every NP problem Karp-reduces to it); NP-complete: NP-hard and in NP. The chapter also says NP-hard for optimisation problems, as the syllabus does, and states once that the formal definition applies to decision problems (Erickson: "formally, no optimization or construction problem is Karp-NP-hard"); it never calls an optimisation problem NP-complete.
- **Size of the instance, length, value:** the *length* of an instance is the number of bits to write it down; a number's *value* is what it is; Chapter 1's topic 1 taught the difference and the chapter uses it.
- **Spoken numbers:** narration has no digits, symbols or code names (automatic check). Say "polynomial time", "yes or no", "certificate", "verifier", "reduces to", "NP-complete", "three partition", "three S A T" (the builder confirms the spoken form against the narration checker); speak every number in words (QUALITY.md #29). Names: Cook, Levin, Karp, Garey, Johnson, Sipser (if used), Backurs, Indyk: the builder checks them against `site/narration/voice.json` aliases and adds missing ones.

### 107. P, NP and polynomial reductions (`dsa.p-np-polynomial-reductions`): full, §33.1

- **Folder:** `p-np-polynomial-reductions`. About 16 min.
- **Scope (syllabus clause → what it delivers):**
  - "decision vs search vs optimization": the three flavours of one problem (bin packing in three forms) and why the theory is stated for decision problems: yes or no is the simplest answer to compare and compose, and the other two are polynomially reducible to it *for the problems of this chapter*: the self-reduction that builds a witness from a decision procedure with n + 1 calls (one to ask whether any set exists, then one per vertex) (the independent-set construction of the running example, checked in `examples.py`); the optimisation form reduces to the decision form by trying k = 1, 2, … or by binary search on k when the objective is polynomially bounded. ### 108. Pseudo-polynomial time and strong NP-hardness (`dsa.pseudo-polynomial-time-strong`): full, §33.1

- **Folder:** `pseudo-polynomial-time-strong`. About 14 min.
- **Scope (syllabus clause → what it delivers):**
  - "why knapsack has an O(nW) DP while bin packing has no pseudo-polynomial algorithm unless P = NP": the argument in three steps. (1) *Knapsack is NP-complete and has a dynamic program with n·W cells* (the DP built in Chapter 22 topic 75, used here, not rebuilt; the decision form of knapsack is NP-complete (Wikipedia, read at planning time; the primary source is Karp 1972 for the closely related subset sum, to read at research time), and the O(nW) DP is "pseudo-polynomial", the definition Chapter 1 gave). These do not contradict each other because the DP's cost is polynomial in the *value* W and exponential in the *length* b = ⌈log₂ W⌉ of W: n·2^b cells, 1.02 × 10⁵ at 10 bits and 1.1 × 10¹⁴ at 40 (the running example, a count rising). So the problem is hard exactly when W is large; hardness that goes away if the numbers are small is **weak** NP-hardness (the topic's word for NP-hardness only in binary encoding; Wikipedia, read: "weakly NP-hard … may have a pseudo-polynomial time algorithm"; example: partition). (2) *A problem that stays NP-hard when every number is bounded by a polynomial in the input length is strongly NP-hard* (Garey and Johnson 1978, "'Strong' NP-completeness results: motivation, examples, and implications", J. ACM 25(3), pp. 499–508, citation read on the Wikipedia page, paper **not read**; the topic states the definition as Wikipedia states it, "remains NP-complete even when all of its numerical parameters are bounded by a polynomial in the length of the input", and the equivalent unary-encoding formulation, and confirms both against a primary source). (3) *A strongly NP-hard problem has no pseudo-polynomial algorithm unless P = NP*: if it had, the bounded-numbers instances would then be solvable in time polynomial in the length, contradicting NP-hardness of exactly those instances. The topic gives this one-line argument (read at planning time in Wikipedia: "If a problem is strongly NP-hard, then it does not even have a pseudo-polynomial time algorithm", assuming P ≠ NP) and does not claim it as a deep result. **Bin packing** as the strong example (Wikipedia: strongly NP-complete, by reduction from the strongly NP-complete 3-partition problem; to confirm in Garey and Johnson before it is said), and the instructive contrast from the same page: with the number of bins K *fixed*, bin packing *is* solvable in pseudo-polynomial time, and with the bin capacity fixed it is solvable in polynomial time, so the strong hardness is about the interaction of many bins with many different sizes (the builder shows the K-fixed DP and counts its table). What a strong NP-hardness result also rules out, stated with its condition: no fully polynomial-time approximation scheme for a strongly NP-hard problem with a polynomially bounded objective unless P = NP (Wikipedia, read; Chapter 34's skipped PTAS/FPTAS topic is where knapsack's FPTAS lives, and the topic names it once as the *payoff* of weak hardness; no more).
  - "reading input encodings carefully": the working habit. For any claim of the form "polynomial time", ask what the input *is* and how it is written: a graph as an adjacency matrix or as lists differ polynomially and do not matter; a number in binary against in unary differs exponentially and decides everything; a sum written as a list of items against one "repeat this item 10⁹ times" line differs by 10⁹. The Chapter 1 trial-division example (O(√v) for a b-bit number is 2^(b/2)) as the first time the reader met the effect, now given its name in this chapter and its consequence: an algorithm's "polynomial" claim is only comparable to a hardness claim when both measure the same length. Three checks the topic teaches the reader to run, each demonstrated: *is the running time polynomial in the numbers' bit lengths or in their values?*; *do the hard instances need huge numbers?* (3-partition does not); *is the number of parts part of the input, or fixed?* (bin packing's K). The 3-partition problem itself: n = 3m numbers, find a partition into m triples with equal sum; hard with numbers bounded by a polynomial in m, which is why it, not partition, anchors strong hardness.
  - What this topic **does not** do, and where it goes: a *proof* of 3-partition's strong NP-hardness (it follows the skipped gadget topic's chain; cited, not proved); knapsack's FPTAS by profit scaling (the *(skipped)* "PTAS and FPTAS" topic of Chapter 34; the topic names the connection once, since "weak hardness buys an approximation scheme" is the natural afterthought); the DP itself (Chapter 22); and fine-grained complexity (SETH, 3SUM, APSP; the *(skipped)* §33.2 topic: the post-notes' "Where this goes next" states Backurs and Indyk's result that edit distance has no strongly subquadratic algorithm under SETH (the syllabus's own words), with the source read at research time before any figure is stated: a conditional lower bound of a kind this chapter has not built).
- **Outcomes:**
  - Explain why an O(nW) algorithm is not a polynomial-time algorithm in the input length, and compute how its running time grows with each extra bit of W.
