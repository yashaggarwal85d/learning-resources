## The chapter

### The problem it solves

Chapter 33 ended on bad news with a deadline attached. The job still ships. This chapter is the engineer's three honest answers to an NP-hard problem, and a fourth to a problem that is not hard at all but unfair: the input arrives one piece at a time and the decision cannot be taken back. Each answer is a different kind of promise, and the skill is to know what each one actually promises. An **approximation algorithm** runs in polynomial time and carries a *proof* that its answer is within a stated factor of the best possible, on every instance (topics 109 and 110: greedy set cover, vertex cover by matching, first-fit-decreasing, longest-processing-time-first). A **heuristic with no guarantee** (topic 111: local search, simulated annealing, genetic search) gives up the proof, and is the right call when the search space is vast, the objective is only an estimate anyway, and a good answer in a millisecond beats a provably decent one in a minute: PostgreSQL plans a join of twelve or more tables this way. An **online algorithm** (topic 112: caching) cannot see the future, and its promise is a *competitive ratio*: against the best algorithm that did see it, you pay at most this factor, whatever the input. The thread through all four: **a guarantee is a ratio between your answer and a benchmark you cannot compute** (the optimum, or the clairvoyant), and every proof in the chapter is a way of bounding your cost against that benchmark by *comparing both to a third quantity you can compute* (the number of edges in a matching, a harmonic sum over the uncovered elements, the size of the largest item, the number of distinct pages in a phase).

### Order of ideas, and why

1. **§34.1 Approximation ratios and greedy set cover (109).** The vocabulary and the first two proofs, together, because a ratio is only meaningful with a proof. What an approximation ratio *is* (a worst-case multiplicative gap, for minimisation, against the optimum, on all instances), then set cover's greedy algorithm, whose analysis is the model for everything after it: the cost of each newly covered element is bounded by the optimum's average, and the sum is a harmonic number, so ln n. The matching lower bound (it is essentially optimal: no polynomial algorithm does better than (1 − ε) ln n unless P = NP) is stated with its source, not proved. Then the sharpest contrast, vertex cover: a 2-approximation from a *maximal matching*, in three lines, where the proof compares the answer to a quantity (the matching's size) that is itself a lower bound on the optimum. Taken together: *a lower bound on OPT that you can compute is the whole trick*.
2. **§34.1 Bin packing and makespan scheduling (110).** The two guarantees every data platform meets, on the problems Chapter 33 named: first-fit-decreasing for bin packing (at most 11/9 of the optimum plus 6/9 bins, and the instance that shows it tight) and LPT for scheduling on identical machines (the factor 4/3 − 1/(3m), and the instance that shows it tight). The engineering half: the syllabus names packing files into splits and tasks onto executors (Data Engineering Chapter 7). The topic reads Spark's own file-splitting code for what it actually does (it sorts splits by size descending and packs next-fit-style, which is a *relative* of the algorithm the theory analyses, not the same one) and says plainly which guarantee, if any, carries over.

### Shared notation and terms

- **Approximation ratio ρ:** for a minimisation problem, an algorithm is a ρ-approximation if on *every* instance its cost is at most ρ times the optimum's (ρ ≥ 1); for a maximisation problem the same with the reverse inequality. The chapter says "ratio" and "factor" and writes ALG and OPT for the algorithm's cost and the optimum's. **Absolute** (a ratio on every instance) is distinguished from **asymptotic** (a ratio plus an additive constant, as in FFD's "11/9 · OPT + 6/9"); the topic says which each result is.
- **Competitive ratio:** for an online algorithm, ALG(σ) ≤ c · OPT(σ) + b for every request sequence σ, where OPT is the *offline* optimum that sees all of σ in advance; for randomized algorithms the left side is an expectation, and the adversary is **oblivious** (it fixes σ in advance without seeing the algorithm's coin flips; Plotkin's CS369 notes, read at planning time, define it this way). The chapter keeps "approximation ratio" (a polynomial-time algorithm that sees everything) and "competitive ratio" (an algorithm that cannot see the future) apart in every caption: the *resource* that is limited is different.
- **Lower bound on OPT:** any quantity computable in polynomial time that no solution can beat (the size of a matching for vertex cover, the average load or the longest job for scheduling, the total size over C for bin packing, the number of distinct pages in a phase for paging). The chapter's thread. It is always written "OPT ≥ …" and its source is named.
- **Local optimum, neighbourhood, move:** a *move* changes a solution slightly; the *neighbourhood* is the set of solutions one move away; a *local optimum* has no better neighbour; written as the local-search literature writes them.
- **Spoken numbers:** narration has no digits, symbols or code names (automatic check). Say "approximation ratio", "H sub n, the harmonic number", "natural log of n", "eleven ninths of the optimum plus six ninths", "four thirds minus one over three m", "k-competitive", "log k"; speak every number in words (QUALITY.md #29). Names: Chvátal, Johnson, Lovász, Dinur, Steurer, Graham, Sleator, Tarjan, Bélády, Kirkpatrick, Gelatt, Vecchi, Croes, Lin, Kernighan, Utesch, Whitley, Fiat: the builder checks them against `site/narration/voice.json` aliases and adds missing ones ("Bélády" is said "Bay-lah-dee", the builder confirms the form in the lexicon).

### 109. Approximation ratios and greedy set cover (`dsa.approximation-ratios-greedy-set`): full, §34.1

- **Folder:** `approximation-ratios-greedy-set`. About 16 min.
- **Scope (syllabus clause → what it delivers):**
  - "the ln n-approximation for set cover": the problem (a universe of n elements, a family of sets, choose the fewest sets whose union is the universe; NP-hard, one of Karp's 21 problems, to confirm in the primary source) and the greedy algorithm (repeatedly pick the set covering the most still-uncovered elements). The analysis, line by line, and it is the model for the chapter: when greedy has covered all but r elements and the optimum's k sets cover those r, some set covers at least r/k of them, so the cost of covering each new element is at most OPT/(number of elements then uncovered) and the sum over elements is OPT · (1 + 1/2 + … + 1/n) = OPT · H_n ≤ OPT · (ln n + 1) (the harmonic sum bound, read at planning time on Wikipedia's set-cover page; the proof is Chvátal 1979 and Johnson 1974, Lovász 1975, to read at research time before attribution). The finer statement (ratio H(s′) where s′ is the largest set) with its source. The growth example from the running example (greedy picks 3, 5, 8, 12 sets against an optimum of 2, ratios 1.5 to 6, rising with ln n) shown, and checked, so that "ln n" is not a loose upper bound but behaviour that really occurs.
  - "and why it is essentially optimal": the hardness side, stated with its sources and scope. Wikipedia (read at planning time): Lund and Yannakakis (1994) showed set cover cannot be approximated within ½·log₂ n unless NP has quasi-polynomial algorithms; Feige (1998) improved the bound to (1 − o(1)) ln n under the same kind of assumption; **Dinur and Steurer (2013) showed it is NP-hard to approximate within (1 − ε) ln n for every ε > 0**, and their paper's corollary was read at planning time (arXiv 1305.1979, Corollary 1.5: "For every ε > 0, it is NP-hard to approximate set cover to within (1 − ε) ln n, where n is the size of the instance", with the sentence that this strengthens Feige's quasi-NP-hardness). So the topic's sentence is: *greedy's ln n is tight in the exponent of the leading term, unless P = NP*. The topic does **not** prove the hardness (it rests on the PCP theorem and parallel repetition) and says that it does not; it explains in one frame why a hardness-of-approximation result is a reduction of the kind topic 107 taught, now with a *gap* (a reduction that maps yes-instances to ones with a small optimum and no-instances to ones with a large one), and does not go further.
  - "2-approximate vertex cover via maximal matching": the problem (the smallest set of vertices touching every edge; NP-hard; the complement of a maximum independent set, the fact from topic 107), the algorithm (repeat: take any uncovered edge, put *both* endpoints into the cover, delete every edge they touch; equivalently find a maximal matching and take its endpoints; Wikipedia, read: "The endpoints of any maximal matching form a vertex cover", and the factor-2 statement) and the three-line proof: the matched edges share no vertex, so any cover must contain a distinct endpoint of each, so OPT ≥ |M|; the algorithm returns 2|M| vertices; so ALG ≤ 2·OPT. The proof's *shape* is the lesson: **a polynomial-time lower bound on OPT** (the matching) stands in for the unknown optimum. K_{m,m} shows the factor 2 is attained by this algorithm. What is known beyond it, with each statement sourced: no better constant than 2 is known and, if the Unique Games Conjecture holds, none exists (Wikipedia, read; Khot and Regev 2008), and unconditionally Dinur and Safra (2005) showed 1.3606 is NP-hard (Wikipedia, read; Annals of Mathematics 162(1), pp. 439–485; the paper to read before the figure is stated).
  - Approximation ratio *defined* here (the chapter's first topic), with the three vocabulary distinctions made visible: absolute against asymptotic ratio, minimisation against maximisation, polynomial-time against exponential-time benchmark; and what an approximation guarantee does not say (a ratio is a worst case; on typical instances greedy set cover is far better; the guarantee is *not* tight for every instance, only for some).
  - What this topic **does not** do, and where it goes: LP relaxation and rounding, and duality as a certificate of quality (the *(skipped)* topic; the post-notes name the fact that vertex cover also has a 2-approximation by rounding a linear-programming solution with the threshold ½, Wikipedia, read: "A 2-approximate vertex cover can be obtained from this fractional solution by selecting the subset of vertices whose variables are nonzero", and nothing more); PTAS and FPTAS (topic 110's post-notes); weighted set cover and its greedy ratio (named once, with no claim unless the builder reads it); maximum-matching algorithms (Chapter 20, earlier in the order: topic 109 needs only "a maximal matching, found greedily", and uses Chapter 20's distinction between maximal and maximum; the topic notes that König's theorem gives the *exact* minimum vertex cover on bipartite graphs, so the factor 2 is a statement about general graphs).
- **Outcomes:**
  - State what an approximation ratio guarantees and does not, and say which of two given guarantees (ln n and 2) is stronger and for what.
  - Run greedy set cover and the matching-based vertex cover by hand, and prove each ratio by comparing the algorithm's cost to a computable lower bound on the optimum.
  - Say in one sentence why greedy's ln n cannot be beaten by any polynomial algorithm unless P = NP, and what sort of theorem that sentence is.

### 112. Online paging (`dsa.online-paging`): full, §34.2

- **Folder:** `online-paging`. About 16 min.
- **Scope (syllabus clause → what it delivers):**
  - "LRU and FIFO are k-competitive (Sleator & Tarjan, 1985)": the paging problem as Sleator and Tarjan pose it (a two-level memory, n pages of fast memory, a request for a page in slow memory costs one fault and moves it in, evicting another; minimise faults; read at planning time, §4 of the CACM paper) and the *online* constraint (the eviction is chosen without knowing future requests). The competitive ratio defined in the chapter's own words and kept distinct from the approximation ratio (shared notation above), and the *adversary argument* that no deterministic online algorithm does better than k: given any deterministic algorithm A with a cache of k pages, an adversary that uses only k + 1 pages and always requests the one A just evicted makes A fault on every request, while the offline optimum faults at most once every k requests (Plotkin's CS369 notes, read at planning time: "Let A be an algorithm for the paging problem. Consider a malicious program that uses only k + 1 pages. At each step, the program requests the one page that is not in A's cache"; the optimum evicts the page requested furthest in the future, so faults are at least k apart). Sleator and Tarjan's own statement, read at planning time: "Let A be any on-line algorithm. Then there are arbitrarily long sequences s such that F_A(s) ≥ (n_A/(n_A − n_MIN + 1)) · F_MIN(s) …" (with the theorem's inequality rendered only partially by the text extraction; **the exact statement is taken from the PDF at research time, and the plan states only that the paper compares an online algorithm with n_A pages against the optimum with n_MIN pages, and that the bound is tight to within an additive term for LRU and holds for FIFO by the same argument**, as the paper says: "Essentially the same argument shows that Theorem 6 holds for FIFO"). The upper bound for LRU by the *phase* argument (a phase is a maximal stretch in which LRU faults exactly k times; the optimum must fault at least once per phase; Plotkin's notes read: "during each phase, the optimal algorithm (OPT) must fault at least once", with the two cases), which the topic proves in full for the case that the optimum has the same size k. The related-resource-augmentation fact (competitive ratio k/(k − h + 1) against an optimum with a *smaller* cache h, so with a cache twice the optimum's the ratio is about 2; Plotkin's notes, read: "For k = 2h, this is 2-competitive", and Wikipedia's marking-algorithm statement, read) shown as the practical answer to "k is terrible": *give the online algorithm somewhat more memory than the benchmark and the ratio collapses*. What the adversary argument does not cover and the topic flags: LIFO and LFU are not competitive at all (the paper's own counterexamples, read: a sequence on which LIFO faults on every request while MIN faults a constant number of times), which is the sentence that explains why "least recently used" is not an arbitrary choice.
