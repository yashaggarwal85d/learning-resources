## The chapter

### The problem it solves

Three things a working engineer does constantly are "pick one at random", "pick a few at random from something that does not fit", and "put these in a random order", and each has a famous wrong implementation that passes every casual test. Most of the code shipped in the last thirty years for these three jobs was wrong in a way nobody noticed for years: a shuffle whose first position is more likely to be the wrong item, a "sample" that is not uniform when the stream is longer than you thought, a comparator that returns random numbers. The chapter asks one question three times: **how do you prove that a randomized procedure produces exactly the distribution you meant?** The answer is always the same kind of argument: count the ways, or follow one item and multiply its survival probabilities, and let *linearity of expectation* add up the rest without tracking dependence.

The syllabus's own chapter title promises concentration bounds ("proving the results hold with high probability"), and its §29.1 lists three tools: expectation and indicators (this chapter, a refresher), Las Vegas versus Monte Carlo, and Markov-to-Chernoff concentration bounds; the last two are marked *(skipped)* and get no reel. That is a real gap in what this chapter can teach, and this plan says so rather than hiding it: the chapter teaches the first tool in full as a refresher and uses **only what it teaches** inside the two algorithm topics. Every probability statement made in topics 96 and 97 is either an *exact* probability (a reservoir item survives with probability exactly k/n, a shuffle outcome has probability exactly 1/n!) or an *expectation* (expected number of random draws, expected number of fixed points). No topic here asserts a tail bound it has not been taught. Markov's and Chebyshev's inequalities, Chernoff–Hoeffding and the median-of-means trick are named once, in topic 95's post-notes, as what Chapter 30's sketches rest on and as the point where the skipped lines would sit; Chapter 30 states and uses what it needs from them (see its plan).

### Order of ideas, and why

1. **§29.1 Expectation and indicator variables (95, refresher).** The tool first, because both algorithms are proved with it. Linearity of expectation (the expectation of a sum is the sum of the expectations *whether or not* the terms are independent), the indicator variable (a 0/1 variable whose expectation is the probability of its event), then three worked uses that are the syllabus's own list: the expected number of comparisons of randomized quicksort (the key pair argument: elements ranked i < j are compared iff one of them is the first pivot chosen among the j − i + 1 elements between them, probability 2/(j − i + 1), which sums to 2(n + 1)Hₙ − 4n), the coupon collector (n·Hₙ ≈ n ln n draws), and one new one the chapter needs (the expected number of fixed points of a random permutation is exactly 1). A refresher: about 6 minutes, brisk, one predict question.
2. **§29.2 Reservoir sampling (96).** A uniform sample of k items from a stream of unknown length in one pass and O(k) memory. Algorithm R by induction on the stream (the invariant: after n items every item is in the reservoir with probability exactly k/n), Algorithm L (skipping ahead: the number of random draws drops from n to about k(1 + ln(n/k))), weighted sampling by Efraimidis–Spirakis keys, and the merge of two reservoirs taken on different machines.
3. **§29.2 Random shuffling (97).** The permutation counterpart: Fisher–Yates (the Durstenfeld in-place form) as the algorithm whose outcome count is exactly n!, the classic wrong versions (swap with any index; sort with a random comparator; modulo or too-small seeds) each shown wrong by counting, Sattolo's variant (a uniform *cyclic* permutation: (n − 1)! of the n! orderings, and the reason Chapter 1's pointer-chasing benchmark uses it), and shuffling data too large for memory (the buffered streaming shuffle used in data loaders, and its honest limit). 96 before 97: Fisher–Yates is *also* a reservoir-style argument (item at position i is placed by a draw among the i remaining), and the sample-without-replacement of k items is a *partial* shuffle; seeing the streaming case first makes the in-memory case a special case, not the other way round.
4. **Skipped in this chapter** (stay in the curriculum, get no reel, per `syllabus/review-notes/dsa-cuts.md`, "Chapter 29 (2 of 8)" and "(3 of 8)"): Las Vegas vs Monte Carlo, Concentration bounds: Markov to Chernoff, Balls into bins and two choices, Fingerprinting and identity testing, Karger's randomized min cut (§29.1, §29.2). Each is named once in the post-notes of the topic nearest to it, with only what a primary source opened at research time says; none is built. The syllabus's *Lab* names two of them (balls into bins, Karger): see "Notes for the release run".
5. **Handoff to Chapter 30.** Topic 97 ends on shuffling data too large for memory, where the only honest tool is a buffer and the algorithm sees each item once. Chapter 30 takes the "one pass, memory far below the input" setting as its subject: what can and cannot be computed about a stream in that model, and the summaries (sketches) that do it.

### Shared notation and terms

- **Pr[A]** the probability of event A; **E[X]** the expectation of a random variable X; **I_A** the indicator of A (1 if A happens, else 0), so E[I_A] = Pr[A]; **Hₙ = 1 + 1/2 + … + 1/n** the n-th harmonic number, Hₙ ≈ ln n + 0.5772 (stated in topic 95 and checked in `examples.py`, not assumed). These are the symbols CLRS and Mitzenmacher–Upfal use.
- **n** the number of items seen (stream length), **k** the sample or reservoir size, **w** an item's weight, **N** the number of random numbers a procedure draws (reported because the cost of a randomized algorithm is partly its randomness).
- **Uniform** means *every outcome has exactly the same probability*, the property the two algorithm topics prove. "Looks random" and "passes a few tests" are not the claim, and the topics say so in those words.
- **Spoken numbers:** narration has no digits, symbols or code names (automatic check). Say "linearity of expectation", "indicator variable", "harmonic number", "k over n", "n factorial", "the chi squared statistic"; every number in words (QUALITY.md #29). Names (Waterman, Vitter, Li, Efraimidis, Spirakis, Fisher, Yates, Durstenfeld, Knuth, Sattolo, Pearson) are added to the pronunciation lexicon on first use.

### One visual language for the chapter's reels

The chapter builds from `site/src/reels/kit.tsx` and `DESIGN.md`'s briefing theme: black and white carry everything, colour is rare and means something. On top of that this chapter shares one grammar:

- **A random choice is drawn as a die or a pointer that lands:** a short `Draw` from the chooser to the chosen cell, one per beat, always ending on a real value. Randomness is seeded; the reel plays the same every time, and says so once.
- **Colour:** `crema` an item or cell that exists; `latte` secondary labels (a probability, a slot index, a weight); `taupe` an item not yet seen or not chosen; `caramel` **the thing to look at**: the item whose survival or placement is being followed (the solid shape, plus a `Reticle` on the decisive draw); `cinnamon` (rare) **the alternative being compared**, always the same side of a pair: the wrong algorithm's histogram beside the right one's; `cherry` with `cherryText` **the bad outcome**: a bar that is too tall or too short against the uniform line, and a draw that makes a result biased.

- **A proof by following one item** is drawn as that one item (`caramel`) carried along the timeline with its survival probability multiplying beat by beat (k/n → (k/n)·(1 − 1/(n+1)) → …) until the product telescopes to k/N.

### 95. Expectation and indicator variables (`dsa.expectation-indicator-variables`): refresher, §29.1

- **Folder:** `expectation-indicator-variables`. About 6 min.
- **Scope (syllabus clause → what it delivers):**
  - "linearity of expectation": E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] for any random variables on the same space, **with no independence needed** (the property the whole chapter leans on, so it is shown by a case where the terms are visibly dependent: two items drawn without replacement). The definition of expectation as a weighted average is one line; a refresher assumes it.
  - "indicators": write the quantity you want as a sum of 0/1 variables, each the indicator of an event, so the expectation is a sum of probabilities. The technique shown on the problem that motivates it: the expected number of fixed points of a random permutation (n indicators, each with probability 1/n, so exactly 1, however the indicators depend on each other), measured on shuffled arrays.
  - "expected comparisons in randomized quicksort": the standard indicator proof. Let the keys have ranks 1…n; X_ij is 1 if the elements of rank i and j are ever compared. They are compared iff one of them is the first pivot chosen from the j − i + 1 elements ranked i…j, so Pr[X_ij = 1] = 2/(j − i + 1). Summing, E[comparisons] = 2(n + 1)Hₙ − 4n, which is Θ(n log n); the topic checks the exact value against a simulation (planning table). Chapter 1's topic derived the same number from a recurrence (`asymptotic-notation` quotes 2(n+1)Hₙ − 4n and its recurrence); this topic says "the second derivation" aloud and shows what each buys: the recurrence needs the algorithm's structure, the indicators need only a probability per pair.
  - "the coupon collector": n coupons, draw uniformly with replacement until all are seen: expected n·Hₙ draws, by splitting the time into n phases, phase i (with i − 1 distinct coupons seen) a geometric variable with success probability (n − i + 1)/n and expectation n/(n − i + 1), summed by linearity. The same shape as a hash table that must fill every bucket, the number of random probes before every slot is touched, or why `n ln n` keeps appearing. The expectation only: its *concentration* (the tail of the number of draws) is exactly what the skipped concentration-bounds topic would add, and the topic says that in one sentence.
  - The one new tool the chapter's algorithms need, stated here because it is not in the syllabus line but is the same technique: **the harmonic number** Hₙ ≈ ln n + γ (γ = 0.5772…), because it appears in three of the four results (quicksort, coupons, and Algorithm L's draw count in topic 96). It is defined and checked numerically; its approximation is cited, not proved.
- **Leaves to other topics:** the definition of a random variable and of expectation, independence → pre-notes (a refresher assumes them); Las Vegas versus Monte Carlo (skipped), the topic names the difference between "expected running time" (quicksort: always correct, random time) and "always fast, probably correct" in one sentence and says which one is skipped; **tail bounds** (Markov, Chebyshev, Chernoff, the union bound, median of means) → skipped (Chapter 29, "Concentration bounds: Markov to Chernoff"), named once in post-notes as the next tool, with the one fact `asymptotic-notation`'s post-notes already state (Markov gives only Pr[T ≥ k·E[T]] ≤ 1/k); quicksort's partitioning schemes and pivot rules → Chapter 15 (earlier in the course order, though not live yet, which is why this topic uses the algorithm by description without teaching it); `with-high-probability` → Chapter 1 (used, not re-taught); expected values of hashing (chain length) → Chapter 3 (one example only).
- **Outcomes:**
  - Write a count as a sum of indicator variables and compute its expectation without caring how the indicators depend on each other.
  - Reproduce the pair argument for randomized quicksort's expected comparisons and the phase argument for the coupon collector, and state each result.
  - Say what linearity does *not* give (a tail or a variance), and name the tool that would.
- **Introduces:** `linearity-of-expectation`, `indicator-variable`, `harmonic-number`, `quicksort-expected-comparisons`, `coupon-collector`, `expected-fixed-points`. **Uses:** `expected-vs-average`, `with-high-probability`, `best-worst-average-case` (Chapter 1: what an expected bound promises), `big-o-theta-omega` (Chapter 1), `hash-function`, `separate-chaining` (Chapter 3: one example, a chain's expected length). **Declared:** `expectation`, `probability-basics`, `logarithms`.

The opening step shows one concrete case the sweep chapter could not state: a random pivot makes quicksort's cost a random variable, and "O(n log n)" is then a statement about its expectation.
- **Handoff:** "Linearity of expectation turns a messy random count into a sum of probabilities, and it worked even where the pieces depend on each other. The next question is not a running time but a distribution: you are handed a stream you can only read once, you do not know how long it is, you can keep k items, and you must be able to say that every item has the same chance of being among them. Reservoir sampling, next."
- **Prerequisites panel:** Chapter 1 "Asymptotic notation and case analysis" (what expected, average and with-high-probability bounds promise; Before this). Data Engineering "Asymptotic notation and logarithms" (another track, link only if live). An outside probability refresher (random variables, expectation, independence), links verified at research time. On-site: none new.
- **Pre-notes terms:** random variable, expected value (a probability-weighted average), independent events, uniform random choice, harmonic number (the sum 1 + 1/2 + … + 1/n; one line), geometric random variable (the number of trials until the first success), randomized quicksort in one line (pick a pivot uniformly at random, partition, recurse).
- **Hook:** randomized quicksort picks pivots by coin flip, so its cost is different every run. How many comparisons does it make, on average, on a thousand keys? The answer is 10,986, computed exactly in a line with no recurrence, and a simulation of two hundred runs lands on 11,002. Predict first: is it closer to n log₂ n, 2n ln n, or n²/4? The punch line is that the *pairs* are the whole proof: two elements are compared at most once, and the chance depends only on how far apart their ranks are.
- **Visual idea:** a row of ranked elements; one pair lights up (`caramel`) and the elements between them are the set from which the first pivot decides (`Reticle` on the first pivot drawn); the probability 2/(j − i + 1) counts as a number under the pair; then the triangle of all pairs fills row by row and the sum counts up to 10,986 (the surprising number) against the simulated cloud of runs landing on it as `Plot`ted points. The coupon collector is the same strip with coupons seen turning on one by one and the waiting times between them drawn as widening gaps; the harmonic sum is the total gap.

### 96. Reservoir sampling (`dsa.reservoir-sampling`): full, §29.2

- **Folder:** `reservoir-sampling`. About 15 min.
- **Scope (syllabus clause → what it delivers):**
  - "Algorithm R": keep the first k items; for item i > k, draw j uniformly from 1…i and, if j ≤ k, replace slot j with item i. The correctness invariant, **proved by induction on the stream length**: after item n every item seen so far is in the reservoir with probability exactly k/n (the proof the sources give: for the (n+1)-th item, the reservoir was a uniform k-subset of the first n; an item survives the step with probability (k/n)·(1 − 1/(n+1)) = k/(n+1) … written out so the product telescopes). The algorithm's attribution (Waterman's Algorithm R; Vitter 1985 is the analysis, to verify against the sources: the Wikipedia article states Waterman discovered it independently of an earlier method) is checked at research time. Costs: O(n) time, O(k) space, one random draw per item after the k-th (999,900 draws for n = 1,000,000, k = 100). **The tempting wrong versions** shown once: "sample each item with probability k/n" needs n, which a stream does not give; "keep the first k" is not uniform; "replace a *random* slot with probability 1/2" is not either. The exact inclusion frequency from simulation (0.298–0.303 for 0.3) is the evidence.
  - "the skip-based Algorithm L": the observation (Vitter 1985, Li 1994) that the *number of items skipped* before the next replacement can be drawn directly, so the algorithm touches only the items that enter the reservoir, in expected O(k(1 + log(n/k))) random draws against Algorithm R's n − k. Li's version, with a running value W and the skip ⌊ln(random)/ln(1 − W)⌋ + 1 (verified against the source at research time), is the one taught: three random numbers per item *that enters the reservoir* and none for items that do not (the Wikipedia text states "three random numbers for each item that becomes part of the reservoir, and does not spend any time on items that do not"). Measured: 2,783 draws against 999,900. What the skip buys depends on being able to *skip*: on a stream you must read item by item (a file you must parse) the saving is the random draws only, not the reading; on an indexed or seekable source it saves the reads too. The topic states this once, because it is the first thing an engineer asks.
  - "weighted sampling (Efraimidis–Spirakis)": item i with weight wᵢ gets key kᵢ = uᵢ^(1/wᵢ) with uᵢ uniform in (0,1); the sample is the k items with the **largest keys**, held in a min-heap of size k (Chapter 6's heap: a new item replaces the heap's smallest key if its key is larger). The paper's statement (Information Processing Letters 97(5), 2006, pp. 181–185; citation checked against Crossref at planning time; the paper itself not read) is what is attributed: the algorithm A-Res, with A-ExpJ the exponential-jumps variant that skips like Algorithm L (named, not built). **What "weighted" means is the subtle point:** it is sampling *without replacement* with probability proportional to weight *at each draw*, not "item i is included with probability proportional to wᵢ" (those differ once k > 1: the planning example's heavy item has weight 10 of 14 and is included 0.934 of the time, not 10/14 × 2 = 1.43, which is not even a probability); the topic works the three-item example by hand and by simulation and states the exact meaning from the source. The arXiv note by Efraimidis (arXiv:1012.0256, "Weighted Random Sampling over Data Streams", read at planning time: it distinguishes two interpretations of weights and describes algorithms for each) is cited for the two readings.
  - "distributed merging of reservoirs": two reservoirs R₁ and R₂ sampled uniformly from streams of n₁ and n₂ items merge into one uniform sample of the union by choosing each of the k output slots from R₁ with probability n₁/(n₁ + n₂) *without replacement from the two reservoirs* (shown by the exact case n₁ = 7, n₂ = 3, k = 3: all ten items have inclusion frequency 0.30). The merge needs n₁ and n₂, so each reservoir carries its stream length with it; a reservoir without its count cannot be merged (the cherry frame). Weighted reservoirs merge by keeping the k largest keys of the union, since keys are independent of which machine drew them (stated and demonstrated; the proof is the keys' own). The topic does not claim more than it demonstrates. **→ Data Engineering §5.6**: the "Stream sampling: reservoir and weighted" topic there is *skipped*, so no Data Engineering reel covers it; the practitioner's view (stratified sampling for skewed data, sampling against sketching as different tools) is therefore named in this topic's post-notes only, with its source, and nothing is promised on its behalf.
  - A real implementation, read as it is: Apache Spark's `SamplingUtils.reservoirSampleAndCount` (Algorithm R: "There are k elements in the reservoir, and the l-th element has been consumed. It should be chosen with probability k/l"; read at planning time from the `master` branch; version to record at research time) and its use in `RangePartitioner.sketch` ("Sketches the input RDD via reservoir sampling on each partition", returning for each partition its item count *and* its sample, read at planning time): the per-partition **count travels with the sample**, and the partitioner uses it to give a sample from a partition that is larger than the others' a weight of n/(sample size), "1 over the sampling probability", when it picks range boundaries. That is the merge-needs-the-count point, in a real system, stated as the source states it: it is a count-weighted combination of samples, not the two-reservoir merge above, and the topic does not call it that. Spark's `takeSample` is *not* a reservoir sample (it counts the RDD, computes a sampling fraction that succeeds with high probability, samples with that fraction and retries if the sample is too small; read at planning time): the topic uses it as the contrast between the two ways to get "exactly k" and states which is which.
- **Leaves to other topics:** expectation, indicators and the harmonic number used in Algorithm L's draw count → topic 95; shuffling and sampling *without replacement* as a partial shuffle → topic 97; sampling versus sketching, stratified sampling, sampling in query engines → Data Engineering §5.6 (skipped there; named in post-notes only); the skipped fingerprinting, Karger and balls-into-bins topics → named once in post-notes; concentration of the *sample statistics* (how close a sample mean is to the population mean) → skipped (Markov to Chernoff), named; Efraimidis–Spirakis's proof of correctness is cited, not rebuilt; A-ExpJ → named; sliding-window sampling → named in post-notes (the chapter-30 line on sliding windows is also skipped).
- **Outcomes:**
  - Prove by induction that Algorithm R leaves every item in the reservoir with probability exactly k/n, and say why any simpler-looking rule fails.
  - Explain how Algorithm L skips items and what it saves (random draws, and reads only where reads can be skipped).

- **Handoff:** "A reservoir answers 'give me k items, each equally likely' from a stream you read once. Its cousin does not want some of the items, it wants all of them, in an order where every ordering is equally likely. - **Pre-notes terms:** stream (items read once, in order, without going back), uniform sample (every k-subset equally likely), sampling with versus without replacement, probability that an item survives a sequence of independent draws (the product), mathematical induction, min-heap (one line), weight of an item (a positive number saying how much to favour it).
- **Hook:** you have a log of unknown length and memory for a thousand lines, and you want a sample in which every line of the whole file has exactly the same chance of being kept. You cannot know the file's length until the end, so you cannot decide at line one with what probability to keep it. Predict: when line n arrives, with what probability should it replace something? The tempting answers (a half, or a fixed small number) make the early lines over-represented; the right one, k/n, is the only one that makes every line equally likely, and it shrinks as the stream grows.
- **Visual idea:** a stream of numbered lines flowing past a reservoir of k slots; each arriving line draws a number (`Draw` from the line to a slot, or to nothing); one early line (`caramel`) is followed as its survival probability multiplies beat by beat and the product telescopes to k/n, counted on screen. Then the same stream run a hundred thousand times as a histogram of how often each line index ended up in the sample (a flat bar at 0.3 with the wrong rule's tilted bars beside it in `cinnamon`). Algorithm L is the same stream with skipped stretches drawn as a single long hop; a counter compares 999,900 draws to 2,783. The weighted case is five tokens of unequal size and their keys landing on a number line; the merge is two reservoirs with their counts flowing into one.

### 97. Random shuffling (`dsa.random-shuffling`): full, §29.2

- **Folder:** `random-shuffling`. About 15 min.
- **Scope (syllabus clause → what it delivers):**
  - "Fisher–Yates": for i from n − 1 down to 1, draw j uniformly from 0…i and swap positions i and j (the in-place form of Durstenfeld, 1964; Fisher and Yates described the pencil-and-paper original in 1938, and Knuth popularised the computer version as Algorithm P: attributions read at planning time in the Wikipedia article and checked against Crossref for Durstenfeld, "Algorithm 235: Random permutation", Communications of the ACM 7(7), 1964, p. 420; the books themselves not read). Correctness by **counting**: the algorithm makes n·(n − 1)·…·1 = n! equally likely random choices and every choice sequence yields a different permutation, so each of the n! permutations has probability exactly 1/n!; equivalently, position i receives each of the i + 1 remaining items with probability 1/(i + 1). O(n) time, O(1) extra space, n − 1 random draws.
  - "the classic biased implementations": three, each shown wrong by *the same counting argument* plus a measurement. (a) **Swap each position with a random position in 0…n − 1** (the "naive" shuffle): it makes nⁿ equally likely choices; nⁿ is not divisible by n! for n ≥ 3 (the planning table), so no assignment of choices to permutations can be uniform; on three items the six outcomes occur 4, 5, 5, 5, 4, 4 times out of 27, and over 60,000 runs the chi-squared statistic is 710 against 2.8 for the correct algorithm (critical value 11.07 at 5% for five degrees of freedom). Bostock's "Fisher–Yates Shuffle" visualisation page (read at planning time) draws the bias as a matrix; the topic uses its own measured matrix. (b) **Sort by a random comparator** (`array.sort(() => 0.5 - Math.random())`): a comparator that is not a consistent ordering makes the sort's output depend on the sort algorithm's internals; the real incident is Microsoft's browser-choice ballot (2010), whose order came from exactly this comparator in JavaScript, and Rob Weir's analysis (read at planning time) measured it on 10,000 runs with a chi-squared test: some browsers were over-represented in the first three positions and Internet Explorer was last about half the time. The topic reports what Weir's page says and, where it can, reproduces the shape of the bias in `examples.py` on the same comparator with a named sort (the builder records which engine and version). **The correct "shuffle by sorting"** is to attach an independent random key to each item and sort by the key, with ties broken by a second draw (named, not built). (c) **A generator that cannot reach every permutation**: a pseudo-random generator with 2^b possible states reaches at most 2^b of the n! permutations; a 32-bit generator cannot even cover the 52! ≈ 2^225.6 orderings of a deck; Python's documentation states that a sequence of length 2,080 is the largest that fits inside the Mersenne Twister's period (read at planning time; the topic reproduces the arithmetic: 2,081! is the first factorial above 2^19937). Modulo bias (using `rand() % n` when the generator's range is not a multiple of n) is named as the fourth cause, with a one-line example and the standard remedy (rejection sampling), and is *measured*, not asserted.
  - "Sattolo's cyclic variant": the same loop with j drawn from 0…i − 1 (strictly below i), which produces a uniformly random **cyclic** permutation (one cycle of length n, so no fixed points): (n − 1)! of the n! orderings, each with probability 1/(n − 1)!. Sattolo, "An algorithm to generate a random cyclic permutation", Information Processing Letters 22(6), 1986, pp. 315–317 (citation checked against Crossref at planning time; the paper not read). Two consequences worth a step each: it is the way to build the single cycle Chapter 1's pointer-chasing benchmark needs (`measuring-memory-hierarchy` already introduces Sattolo's algorithm for that purpose: this topic *explains why it works* and says so), and it is **the classic accidental bug** run in reverse: write Sattolo's loop where Fisher–Yates was meant and the result is silently never the identity and has no fixed point (0 fixed points in 20,000 four-item runs against 1.0 expected for a uniform shuffle: the fixed-point expectation of topic 95 is the test that catches it).
  - "shuffling data too large for memory": when the data does not fit, Fisher–Yates' random access is not available. The honest options, each stated only as far as a primary source says: (1) **assign every record a random key and external-sort by the key** (Chapter 15's external merge sort is the mechanism, which this chapter does not re-teach; the cost is a sort), which is uniform if the keys are independent and ties are broken uniformly; (2) the **distribution-then-in-memory-shuffle** method: send each record to one of m buckets chosen uniformly at random, then shuffle each bucket in memory (a Rao–Sandelius-style or "distribute and conquer" scheme; the topic states the property it needs: that the bucket sizes are random and the within-bucket shuffles are uniform, giving a uniform whole; Sanders, "Random permutations on distributed, external and hierarchical memory", Information Processing Letters 67(6), 1998, pp. 305–309, citation checked against Crossref at planning time, paper not read: attribute only what it says); (3) the **buffered streaming shuffle** used in data loaders: hold a buffer of b records, repeatedly emit a random buffer element and replace it with the next input record. It is a *reservoir-style* argument and **not uniform** unless b ≥ n: TensorFlow's `tf.data.Dataset.shuffle` documents exactly this ("fills a buffer with `buffer_size` elements, then randomly samples elements from this buffer, replacing the selected elements with new elements. For perfect shuffling, a buffer size greater than or equal to the full size of the dataset is required", read at planning time from the v2.16.1 page), and Hugging Face `datasets`' iterable shuffle documents the same limitation (the first ten thousand examples of a million fill the buffer; read at planning time). The topic *measures* how far a buffer of b is from uniform on a small case: for a stream of n items, the probability that the item at input position p is emitted at output position q, tabulated for b = 2, 3 and n = 6 (exhaustively, by enumerating every random choice), against the uniform 1/n. The structural fact to find in that table, and to derive and assert in `examples.py` rather than state from this plan, is that an item can be emitted *earlier* than its input position by at most about b − 1 places and *later* by any amount with falling probability, so a late item is never emitted near the front when b is small, and the first item of a long stream is almost always emitted early. The table is the evidence; the claim is whatever it shows. It also names the sharded-then-shuffled pattern (shuffle the order of shards, then a buffer inside each) as what real loaders do and says what it gives up. MergeShuffle (Bacher, Bodini, Hollender, Lumbroso, 2015: "n log₂ n + O(1) time, in-place, … parallelized", abstract read at planning time from arXiv:1508.03167) is named as the parallel in-memory algorithm; it is not built.
- **Leaves to other topics:** expectation, indicators, the fixed-point expectation → topic 95; the sampling-without-replacement connection and the "uniform" proof style → topic 96; external merge sort and k-way merging → Chapter 15 (and Data Engineering §1.3 for the engineering view: `de.external-merge-sort-k` is live there); parallel shuffles (the exchange in a distributed system: hash-partitioning is not random shuffling; "shuffle" in MapReduce means regrouping by key) → Chapter 32 (named: *this chapter's "shuffle" means a random permutation and the topic says in one sentence that the word is overloaded*); statistical testing in general → one chi-squared test, taught here, nothing more; cryptographic shuffling and secure random sources → named in post-notes (the property a casino or a lottery needs is a source of unpredictable bits, which `practical-hash-functions-hashdos`' `keyed-hash` already points toward).

- **Handoff:** "A shuffle that fits in memory is one swap per item; one that does not has to settle for a buffer, because the algorithm gets to look at each item once and can keep only so much. - **Pre-notes terms:** permutation (an ordering of n items; there are n! of them), factorial, cyclic permutation (one cycle through every item; no item stays in place), uniformly random choice, pseudo-random generator (a deterministic sequence from a seed; its number of states bounds the outputs it can produce), the chi-squared test in one line (compare counts to the expected counts; a large statistic means the counts are unlikely to be uniform), pointer chasing (from Chapter 1; one line).
- **Hook:** the "obvious" shuffle (for each position, swap with a random position) looks right and is wrong, and in a way you can prove without running it: it makes 27 equally likely choices on three cards, and 27 cannot be split evenly among six orderings. Predict: out of 60,000 shuffles of three cards, how many times does each of the six orderings come up?
