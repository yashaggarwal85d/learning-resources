## The chapter

### The problem it solves

Chapter 19 certified a greedy choice with a statement about cuts. This chapter keeps the cut and changes what an edge is: **an edge is a pipe with a capacity**, and the question is how much can flow from a source s to a sink t. A freight network, a cluster's links, the tasks that can run on only some machines, the images whose pixels must be labelled foreground or background (SciPy's own documentation names this last use, "graph cuts optimization in computer vision"). These look unrelated. They are one problem, and the chapter's second topic shows the cleanest instance: **matching** (pair each task with at most one machine and each machine with at most one task) is a flow problem in disguise, with every capacity equal to 1.

The chapter's lesson is that **greedy augmentation fails and the fix is to allow undoing**. Push flow down any path with spare capacity until none is left, and you can be stuck at a flow that is not maximum: on the chapter's own network the stuck flow is 7 and the maximum is 9. The residual graph is the mechanism that repairs it: it records, for every edge, both the capacity still available and the flow that may be taken back, so a later path can reroute earlier flow. The theorem that makes the whole thing trustworthy is another statement about cuts, **max-flow equals min-cut**, which also gives the algorithm a certificate of optimality: when no augmenting path exists, the vertices still reachable from s form a cut whose capacity equals the flow. The second topic then asks how fast matching can be done once it is seen as unit-capacity flow, and answers with Hopcroft and Karp: augment along many shortest paths at once, in O(√V) phases. König's theorem (maximum matching equals minimum vertex cover in a bipartite graph) is the same max-flow min-cut theorem read on that graph.

### Order of ideas, and why

1. **§20.1 Max-flow min-cut and Ford–Fulkerson (69, full).** Flow networks and the two constraints (capacity, conservation); the value of a flow; the greedy that gets stuck, the residual graph that repairs it, the augmenting path, Ford–Fulkerson's loop; the **max-flow min-cut theorem** (the value of a maximum flow equals the capacity of a minimum cut, and the algorithm's last residual graph hands you the cut); **integrality** (integer capacities give an integer maximum flow, which is what makes matching a flow problem); the running time O(E·|f\*|) and why it is pseudo-polynomial (a network whose augmenting paths can alternate 2X times); and **non-termination with irrational capacities** (why SciPy requires integer capacities). First, because everything else in the chapter is this topic's residual graph and theorem.
2. **§20.2 Bipartite matching and Hopcroft–Karp (70, full).** Matching; the reduction to unit-capacity flow (source to every left vertex, sink from every right vertex, all capacities 1; integrality turns an integer flow into a matching); the augmenting path read back in the bipartite graph as an **alternating path** (Berge's lemma: a matching is maximum exactly when there is none); the plain algorithm in O(VE); Hopcroft–Karp's phase (build the layered graph by search from all free left vertices, find a **maximal set of vertex-disjoint shortest augmenting paths**, augment along all of them), O(√V) phases, O(E√V) in total; and **König's theorem** (maximum matching = minimum vertex cover, with the cover read off the last alternating search). Second, because it is topic 69's theorem and algorithm specialised to capacities of 1, where an extra idea (many augmentations per phase) pays.
3. **Handoff to Chapter 21.** Topic 70 ends with a specialised algorithm beating a general one by exploiting structure. Chapter 21 steps back from graph problems to the two design paradigms behind most algorithms: **divide and conquer** (topic 71, a refresher: split, solve, combine, read the cost off the recurrence of Chapter 1) and **greedy** (topics 72–73), where the exchange argument that proved Chapter 19's cut property is turned into a technique, and the failure that opened Chapter 20 (greedy augmentation stuck at 7) is the first counter-example to measure the technique against.

### Shared notation and terms

- **Flow network:** a directed graph G = (V, E) with capacities c(u, v) ≥ 0, a source s, a sink t. A **flow** f satisfies **capacity** 0 ≤ f(u, v) ≤ c(u, v) and **conservation** (flow in equals flow out at every vertex except s and t); its **value** |f| is the net flow out of s. An **s–t cut** is a partition (S, T) with s ∈ S, t ∈ T and its **capacity** c(S, T) is the sum of capacities of edges from S to T only (edges back from T to S do not count; the chapter's one picture of this is D→E). Written as CLRS writes them, so a reader of the book recognises them.
- **Residual graph G_f:** for each edge (u, v) with flow f(u, v) and capacity c(u, v), a **forward residual** c(u, v) − f(u, v) and a **backward residual** f(u, v) (the amount that may be taken back); an **augmenting path** is an s–t path of positive residual edges; its **bottleneck** is the smallest residual on it. **Matching** (a set of edges, no two sharing a vertex), **free** (unmatched) vertex, **alternating path** (alternately not in and in the matching), **augmenting path** in the matching sense (alternating, from a free left to a free right vertex), **vertex cover** (a vertex set touching every edge), **layered graph** (vertices grouped by distance from the free left vertices). **|f\*|** is the value of a maximum flow; **n = |V|, m = |E|**, written V and E inside O() as the syllabus writes them.
- **Pseudo-polynomial:** the Chapter 1 term (running time polynomial in the *value* of a number in the input, not in its length in bits). Ford–Fulkerson's O(E·|f\*|) is that kind of bound, and the chapter says so by name.
- **Spoken numbers:** narration has no digits, symbols or code names (automatic check); say "residual graph", "augmenting path", "bottleneck", "max flow equals min cut", "source", "sink", "alternating path", "vertex cover", "phase"; every number in words (QUALITY.md #29). The names Ford, Fulkerson, Edmonds, Karp, Hopcroft, König, Zwick go in the lexicon the first time they appear in a script.

### One visual language for the chapter's reels

The chapter builds from `site/src/reels/kit.tsx` and `DESIGN.md`'s briefing theme: black and white carry everything, colour is rare and means something. On top of that this chapter shares one grammar:

- **Colour:** `crema` a vertex, and an edge drawn as a pipe at its capacity; `latte` secondary labels (flow / capacity written as `f/c`, a residual number); `taupe` an edge carrying no flow and a residual edge not on the current path; `caramel` **the thing to look at**: the augmenting path being pushed, the cut line when the theorem lands, the alternating path being flipped; `cinnamon` (rare) **the alternative being compared**, always the same side of each pair: the stuck flow against the maximum flow (69), the one-path-at-a-time algorithm against Hopcroft–Karp's phases (70); `cherry` with `cherryText` **the bad outcome**: a flow stuck below the cut's capacity, a path that can no longer be found, a vertex no matching reaches (the Hall obstruction).
- **A pipe is drawn as a pipe:** a directed edge with its capacity as thickness; the flow on it fills it from the tail towards the head as a bar of `crema`, so `f/c` is always visible as a filling and a number. Pushing an augmenting path **draws** (`Draw`) along it, filling each pipe by the bottleneck at once on the beat.
- **The residual graph is drawn beside the network, not in place of it:** each pipe gets a second, thin arrow pointing backwards whose length is the flow that could be taken back; an augmenting path that crosses a backward arrow is the **undo** and is drawn as flow draining out of a pipe while the rest of the path fills. This is the chapter's one hard picture and it is reused unchanged in topic 70.
- **The cut is a closed curve:** a loop around S with the crossing edges lit and their capacities summed in a counter that counts up to the flow value, and **locks on** (`Reticle`) when the two numbers are equal; D→E is shown pointing back across the loop and greyed, so the learner sees why back edges do not count.
- **A bipartite graph is two columns** (left vertices on the left, right on the right), matched edges thick and white, free vertices ringed; an alternating path is a zig-zag that draws from a free left vertex to a free right vertex and, on the flip, every edge on it swaps between thick and thin in one beat. A Hopcroft–Karp phase draws its **layers** as vertical bands.
- **Reel shape.** Both topics are full topics (no refresher): about 13–15 minutes each, 2–4 predict questions, the first within the first four steps; open on the problem named in each topic's hook, then the mechanism, the measured number, then "Prove it".

### 69. Max-flow min-cut and Ford–Fulkerson (`dsa.max-flow-min-cut`): full, §20.1

- **Folder:** `max-flow-min-cut`. About 15 min.
- **Scope (syllabus clause → what it delivers):**

### 70. Bipartite matching and Hopcroft–Karp (`dsa.bipartite-matching-hopcroft-karp`): full, §20.2

- **Folder:** `bipartite-matching-hopcroft-karp`. About 15 min.
- **Scope (syllabus clause → what it delivers):**
  - "matching as unit flow": a **matching**, a maximum matching against a maximal one (the greedy scan that matches 3 of 4 tasks is maximal and not maximum); the reduction (add a source with an edge to every left vertex and a sink with an edge from every right vertex, give every edge capacity 1; a matching is a flow of the same value, and by topic 69's integrality a maximum flow of integers is a maximum matching), shown on the chapter's task–machine graph with the network from topic 69 redrawn; the cost of simply running Ford–Fulkerson on it, O(VE) (at most V augmentations of O(E) each; to verify against Erickson), as the baseline Hopcroft–Karp beats. The reduction is the one SciPy's `maximum_flow` documentation spells out, together with its remark that a specialised routine "will generally perform better" (SciPy's `maximum_bipartite_matching` "implements the Hopcroft–Karp algorithm").
  - "augmenting paths": the same augmenting path read back in the bipartite graph as an **alternating path** from a free left vertex to a free right vertex; flipping it (symmetric difference with the matching) grows the matching by one; **Berge's lemma** (a matching is maximum if and only if no augmenting path exists; Berge 1957, checked) as the matching form of topic 69's theorem, with the direction used in the algorithm (no augmenting path implies maximum) justified from the max-flow min-cut theorem rather than re-proved; the one-augmentation-at-a-time algorithm (try to augment from each free left vertex by a search) on the task–machine graph, including the augmentation that reroutes an earlier choice (t2 takes m1, so t1 moves to m2, so t3 moves to m3, so t4 takes m4: the undo of topic 69 as a zig-zag).
  - "O(E√V) phases": Hopcroft–Karp's phase: breadth-first search from **all free left vertices at once** builds the layers (the shortest augmenting path length ℓ), a depth-first search through the layers finds a **maximal set of vertex-disjoint augmenting paths of length ℓ**, all of them are flipped, and the next phase's shortest path is strictly longer; the argument for **O(√V) phases** at the level the original paper gives it (after √V phases the shortest augmenting path has length at least √V; the matching then differs from a maximum one by at most √V augmenting paths, so at most √V further phases), shown as the phase counter in the Hopcroft–Karp run (808, 125, 19, 1 augmenting paths in 4 phases on 1,000 + 1,000 vertices); each phase costs O(E), so **O(E√V)** (Hopcroft and Karp 1973, whose title states the earlier n^{5/2}; checked).
