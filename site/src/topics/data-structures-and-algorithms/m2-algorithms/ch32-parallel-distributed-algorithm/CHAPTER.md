## The chapter

### The problem it solves

Every algorithm in this course so far was costed on one core: a number of steps, or of block transfers. Two things break that. The first is one machine with many cores: running an algorithm on p cores can take anywhere from 1/p of the time to no less time at all, and the sequential cost does not say which. A loop with 10⁶ independent iterations speeds up almost linearly; a chain of 10⁶ dependent steps speeds up not at all, however many cores wait. The second is many machines: the data does not fit on one, moving it costs more than computing on it, and the cost to count is the number of times everyone has to exchange data before the answer is complete. This chapter gives one cost model for each and one algorithm that each model is built to reason about. **The work–span model** answers "how fast can this go on p cores, and how do I make a scheduler achieve it?" with two numbers per algorithm (the total work T₁ and the longest chain of dependencies, the span T∞) and a randomized scheduler (work stealing) that meets the bound in expectation. **The MapReduce pattern, costed in the MPC model,** answers "how do I express this as a few rounds of local computation and one global exchange?" with a shape (map, shuffle, reduce) and a bound (rounds, and the data any one machine may receive). The thread through both: **parallelism is a property of the dependency structure, not of the machine**; you find it by counting what has to wait for what.

### Order of ideas, and why

1. **§32.1 Work–span model and work stealing (105).** The shared-memory half first, because it is the one the reader can run on the laptop in front of them and because it supplies the language (work, span, speed-up bounded by parallelism) that the second half borrows. The topic builds the model on one running computation (a divide-and-conquer sum, then a parallel merge sort's span read off a recurrence as Chapter 1 taught), states the two laws (the work law and the span law), then Brent's bound, which says a greedy schedule achieves T₁/p + T∞ and is therefore within a factor two of optimal for every p. It ends on the scheduler that meets a bound of this kind with no central queue: randomized work stealing, as Blumofe and Leiserson analysed it (expected time T₁/p + O(T∞)). Parallel prefix sum and parallel merge (both *(skipped)*) are exactly the two algorithms the span argument is usually taught on; the topic names them once as where a reader goes next.
2. **§32.2 MapReduce as an algorithmic pattern (106).** The distributed half second, because it needs two things the first half supplied: the idea that a computation has a shape (a DAG of steps) separate from its cost, and the habit of asking what can run independently. It adds the new resource (communication, counted in rounds) and the one algebraic fact the pattern rests on: a combiner is legal only because the reduce function is associative and commutative, so partial results can be merged in any grouping and any order. It then maps the pattern back to joins and aggregations, the operators of Chapters 31 and 37 and of Data Engineering Chapter 7.
3. **Vertex-centric graph processing (§32.2) and parallel prefix sum and parallel merge (§32.1) are *(skipped)*** (`syllabus/review-notes/dsa-cuts.md`, "Chapter 32 — Parallel and Distributed Algorithm Primitives (1 of 5)" and "(2 of 5)"). The chapter's *Lab* (Blelloch scan, a parallel radix sort on a work-stealing pool, then connected components on CSR against a Pregel-style framework) rests on three of those four skipped items: see "Notes for the release run".
4. **Handoff to Chapter 33.** Topic 106 ends on what parallelism cannot buy: more machines shrink a job only until the longest chain of dependencies or the volume of data that must cross the network, and no number of machines turns an exponential into a polynomial. Chapter 33 is the first chapter of Milestone 3 and opens on exactly that: a precise way to say which problems no algorithm of this course will make fast.

### Shared notation and terms

- **Work T₁, span T∞, T_p, parallelism T₁/T∞:** written as the syllabus writes them and as Blumofe and Leiserson and CLRS do. T_p is the running time on p workers. The span is the length of the longest chain of operations that depend on each other (the critical path), the time on infinitely many workers. The chapter writes **p** for the number of workers (the syllabus's P in Blumofe and Leiserson's statement), consistently.
- **Worker, task, deque, steal:** a *worker* is an OS thread with its own queue of *tasks*; the queue is a *deque* (double-ended): the owner pushes and pops at one end, a *thief* removes from the other. Rust Systems Engineering Ch.9 uses the same words for Tokio's scheduler and says "local run queue"; this chapter keeps "deque" for the algorithm and names the Tokio queue once as one engineering instance.
- **Round, partition, shuffle, combiner:** a *round* is one local computation phase followed by one all-to-all exchange; a *partition* is the subset of keys one reduce task owns; the *shuffle* is the exchange that moves each record to the partition of its key; a *combiner* is the reduce function applied early, on one map task's output, before the exchange.
- **Spoken numbers:** narration has no digits, symbols or code names (automatic check). Say "T sub one", "T infinity" ("T infinity" spelled as the words), "work", "span", "parallelism", "big O of T infinity"; say "map", "shuffle", "reduce" and "combiner" as the plain words; speak every number in words (QUALITY.md #29). Names: Blumofe, Leiserson, Brent, Cilk, Rayon, Tokio, Hadoop, Dean and Ghemawat, Karloff, Suri and Vassilvitskii, Beame, Koutris and Suciu: the builder checks them against `site/narration/voice.json` aliases and adds missing ones.

### 105. Work–span model and work stealing (`dsa.work-span-model-work`): full, §32.1

- **Folder:** `work-span-model-work`. About 16 min.
- **Scope (syllabus clause → what it delivers):**
  - "work T₁, span T∞, parallelism T₁/T∞": the model: a computation as a DAG of unit-cost tasks; work is the number of tasks, the time on one worker; span is the longest path, the time on unboundedly many; parallelism is their ratio, the most speed-up any number of workers can ever give. The two laws in the form a reader will meet them: T_p ≥ T₁/p (the work law, p workers do at most p tasks per step) and T_p ≥ T∞ (the span law). The sum DAG of the running example drawn as a DAG twice (balanced tree: T₁ = 15, T∞ = 4; a left-to-right loop: T₁ = 15, T∞ = 15), so the same work has parallelism 3.75 and 1: the point that parallelism is a property of the dependency structure. Reading a span off a recurrence, using Chapter 1's tools: a divide-and-conquer algorithm whose halves run in parallel has span T∞(n) = T∞(n/2) + (combine span), where work adds the two halves and span takes the larger; parallel merge sort with a sequential merge has Θ(n log n) work and Θ(n) span, parallelism Θ(log n) (this is exactly why merge is the first thing worth parallelising, and where the topic names the skipped Parallel merge topic once).
  - "Brent's bound": a greedy schedule (no worker idles while a ready task exists) finishes in T_p ≤ T₁/p + T∞ (Blumofe and Leiserson's Theorem 1) and, in Brent's sharper form as the analysis-of-parallel-algorithms literature states it, T_p ≤ T∞ + (T₁ − T∞)/p (the form the running example's table uses; the builder checks which form each source states); together with the two laws this is within a factor two of the optimum for every p (the optimum is at least max(T₁/p, T∞), the bound at most twice that). Attribution to be read at research time: Blumofe and Leiserson attribute the greedy-scheduling theorem to adaptations of Brent (1974) and Graham (1966, 1969) (read at planning time in the paper's introduction: "adaptation of theorems by Brent [1974] and Graham [1966; 1969] on dag scheduling"; the statement of the theorem as Theorem 1, "any greedy P-processor execution schedule achieves T(Σ) ≤ T₁/P + T∞", read at planning time). The builder greedily schedules the sum DAG on p = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and records the real step counts against the bound table above. A consequence stated with its condition: linear speed-up (T_p = O(T₁/p)) when the parallelism T₁/T∞ is Ω(p), i.e. when there is enough slack, the paper's own sentence.
  - "randomized work stealing (Blumofe & Leiserson, JACM 1999)": the scheduler: each worker owns a deque of ready tasks; when it spawns a child it pushes the parent continuation and runs the child (so a worker running alone executes the sequential order, depth first); when its deque is empty it becomes a thief and removes the *top* (oldest) task from a victim chosen uniformly at random, retrying until it succeeds. Why steal the oldest: it is the root-most task and so tends to carry the most work (the paper's structure lemma about the deque, which the topic states in one sentence and does not prove). The theorem, as read at planning time (abstract): **for a fully strict computation, the expected time on P processors is T₁/P + O(T∞); space at most S₁·P; expected total communication O(P·T∞·(1 + n_d)·S_max)**, "existentially optimal to within a constant factor". The condition (fully strict, i.e. every join waits for children it spawned itself: the well-structured fork–join computations Cilk, Rayon's `join` and Tokio's tasks-with-joins have) is named, since it is the scope of the claim. What the number says in words: the extra cost over the greedy bound is only a constant times the span, and steals are rare: they grow with p and the span, not with the work; the simulation of the running example reports steals against tasks. The builder does not assert the constant and says the bound is in expectation (with a high-probability version in the paper, to verify at research time).
  - "as in Cilk, Rayon and Tokio": the three real systems, each stated only as far as a primary source says. Cilk: the language the algorithm was designed for (MIT Cilk, then Cilk++/Cilk Plus, now OpenCilk; names and history to read from the Cilk project's own pages at research time, the Wikipedia page was only used to confirm that these names exist). Rayon: `rayon::join`'s documentation (read at planning time, version 1.12.0): "the Rayon runtime uses a fixed pool of worker threads and attempts to only execute code in parallel when there are idle CPUs to handle it", "the underlying technique is called 'work stealing'", the calling thread runs closure A first while "it will advertise closure B as being available for other threads to execute", and if B was stolen the thread "will look for other work while waiting for the thief"; and its warning that closures must be CPU-bound (blocking inside `join` can deadlock). Tokio: a *different point on the same design*: its multi-thread scheduler has per-worker local run queues and steals from them, but is built for tasks that wait on I/O, not for fork–join; Rust Systems Engineering Ch.9 owns this (its "Work stealing and cooperative budgeting" topic, live, introduces `work-stealing-queues` and `cooperative-budgeting` in that domain's ledger): this topic names it in one sentence and links, it does not re-teach the queue (steals half of a busy worker's queue, a LIFO slot, a global injection queue are all that topic's content, from the Tokio documentation and source it read).
  - "→ Rust Systems Engineering Ch.9": the cross-reference is respected by stating what each side owns. **This topic owns:** the model (work, span, parallelism), Brent's bound, the algorithm (random victim, steal the top, the expected-time bound) and its scope. **Rust Ch.9 owns:** Tokio's implementation (local queue capacity, LIFO slot, injection queue, cooperative budgeting, `spawn_blocking`), `LocalSet`, I/O drivers.   - What this topic **does not** do, and where it goes: the deque's *synchronization* (the owner and a thief racing to take the last task, solved with compare-and-swap in the Chase–Lev deque) is Chapter 14's territory (`compare-and-swap`, `lock-freedom`); the topic names the problem in one sentence and does not build the deque. ### 106. MapReduce as an algorithmic pattern (`dsa.mapreduce-as-algorithmic-pattern`): full, §32.2

- **Folder:** `mapreduce-as-algorithmic-pattern`. About 15 min.
- **Scope (syllabus clause → what it delivers):**
  - "map, shuffle and reduce": the three phases as an algorithm, not as a framework: *map* applies a function to each input record independently and emits (key, value) pairs; the *shuffle* moves every pair to the reduce task that owns its key, using a partition function (the original paper's default, read at planning time: "hash(key) mod R", with R and the function chosen by the user), then groups and sorts each partition's pairs by key; *reduce* is called once per key with all its values. The paper's own word-count walk-through as the first example, on the running example's three map tasks. Why the shuffle is the whole cost: map and reduce are embarrassingly parallel (this is the work–span lesson of topic 105: each phase has span of one task), and the exchange is the one step in which every map task talks to every reduce task, M×R transfers; Data Engineering Chapter 7 owns Spark's shuffle mechanics (sort-based shuffle, spill, fetch failure) and is only named here. The fault-tolerance machinery of the paper (re-execution, backup tasks for stragglers, §3.6) is named once as the engineering that makes the pattern usable and as the place a straggler is a tail-latency event (Chapter 1's idea); it is not taught.
  - "combiners and the associativity/commutativity they require": a combiner applies the reduce function early on one map task's output before the exchange. The paper's own justification, read at planning time (§4.3): used when "there is significant repetition in the intermediate keys produced by each map task, and the user-specified Reduce function is commutative and associative", with the word-count example (word frequencies follow a Zipf distribution, so a task emits "hundreds or thousands of records of the form <the, 1>"); "the only difference between a reduce function and a combiner function is how the MapReduce library handles the output". The algebra, derived rather than recited: partial results must combine into the same answer *however the records were split among map tasks (associativity) and in whatever order they arrive (commutativity)*, because the framework chooses neither. The counter-example (the mean is not associative; the fix is a combinable representation, the pair (sum, count), and the last step divides) is the lesson that *the combiner's input and output types must be the same, or be a monoid*; the builder finds a concrete failing triple and asserts it. Spark's `reduceByKey` documents the same contract in its source (read at planning time: "Merge the values for each key using an associative and commutative reduce function. This will also perform the merging locally on each mapper before sending results to a reducer, similarly to a "combiner" in MapReduce"), and `groupByKey` deliberately does no map-side combine; Data Engineering owns the Spark API, this topic only states the contract. Counted: the shuffle's record count with and without the combiner on the running example.
  - "round complexity in the MPC model": the cost model that makes MapReduce a subject for algorithm analysis. The Massively Parallel Communication (MPC) model as Beame, Koutris and Suciu define it (read at planning time): p servers, "each server receives only O(N/p^(1−ε)) bits of data at any step", where N is the input size and ε ∈ [0, 1] is "a parameter that controls replication", computation arranged in rounds, local computation free, and the cost the number of rounds and the maximum communication per server. This is the MPC model *as that paper names and defines it*; the syllabus uses MPC for the same family, and the topic states in one sentence that the literature also has the earlier MRC model of Karloff, Suri and Vassilvitskii (SODA 2010, whose parameters the BKS paper summarises as p = Θ(N^(1−ε)) machines each exchanging Θ(N^(1−ε)) bits per step: a source the builder reads at research time, because only the survey's summary was read at planning time) and Goodrich, Sitchinava and Zhang (an arXiv abstract read: sorting, multi-searching and simulation of BSP and CRCW PRAM algorithms in a constant number of rounds when each machine has memory N^ε). What the model gives, with every claim verified at research time: sorting and prefix computations in O(1) rounds when each machine has N^ε memory (the Goodrich et al. abstract); the cartesian-product example and the replication-versus-reducer-size tradeoff (BKS's introduction, read: n² reducers with replication n, or one reducer, or g² reducers of size 2n/g with replication g); a one-round lower bound for conjunctive queries in terms of the fractional vertex cover of the query's hypergraph (space exponent at least 1 − 1/τ*), with L₂ at ε = 0 and the triangle at ε = 1/3 (read); and the multi-round consequence that transitive closure cannot be computed in O(1) rounds in that model (the abstract, read). The reader's takeaway: **rounds are the resource, and a problem's round complexity is a property of the problem and the memory per machine, not of the framework**.
  - "mapping back to joins and aggregations": a reduce-side (shuffle) equi-join as map-with-a-tag, shuffle on the join key, reduce emitting the cross product of the two sides for each key, and its cost shape (every record crosses the network once; a skewed key sends all its records to one reduce task: the `cherry` frame), against the hash join of Chapter 31 (partition the build side so each piece fits in memory: the same partitioning idea in a different cost model, one machine's memory against the network); a group-by aggregate as map-emit-key, combine, shuffle, reduce, and its partial-aggregation form (Chapter 37 and Data Engineering own the operators; this topic gives the pattern they instantiate). Chapter 31's topic 104 is the bridge back: the Grace hash join's partition-then-join is the same shape on one machine (a partition function, then independent per-partition work), and the topic says so as a connection, not a repeat. Data skew (a hot key sending most records to one reducer) is shown and named; the remedies (salting, two-phase aggregation, a skew-join split) are Data Engineering §7.2's (named only, not taught).
  - "→ Data Engineering Ch.7": the cross-reference is respected by stating what each side owns. **This topic owns:** the pattern as an algorithm, the combiner's algebra and the round-complexity view. **Data Engineering Ch.7 owns:** Spark's execution (stages, shuffle files, AQE, skew remedies, memory, retry), which is the engineering instance. A reader who has both gets "why a combiner must be associative" here and "what Spark's map-side combine does with it" there.
  - What this topic **does not** do, and where it goes: *vertex-centric graph processing* (Pregel supersteps, label-propagation connected components, PageRank, and the single-machine COST argument) is a *(skipped)* topic and is named once in the post-notes: Pregel is the same "rounds of local compute and an exchange" shape, with the graph kept in place and messages sent along edges (read from the Pregel paper at research time); the COST argument (McSherry et al., HotOS 2015) is the honest warning that a cluster's speed-up must be measured against a good single-thread baseline (primary source to read at research time before a number is stated). Data Engineering §17.3 owns graph processing.

This course has already met such problems, in passing: a join order, a packing, a schedule. The next chapter says exactly what makes a problem that kind, and how to recognise one before you spend a month on it. P, NP and polynomial reductions, next."

- **Hook:** a word count over a gigabyte of text emits one record per word, and a naive run ships every one of them across the network, though the answer has only the distinct words in it: the word "the" alone is hundreds of thousands of records, each a pair (the, 1), all headed for one reduce task. A one-line change collapses that, and a one-line change makes the answer wrong. Predict: you replace the sum with an average to get the mean count per key; can the same early-combining trick still be applied, and if you apply it anyway, does the answer come out too high, too low, or still right? (Neither: it is wrong in an unpredictable direction, because the mean of means is not the mean.)
