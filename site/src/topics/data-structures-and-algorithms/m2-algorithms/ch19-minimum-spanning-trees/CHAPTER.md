## The chapter

### The problem it solves

Chapters 17 and 18 asked what a graph looks like from one vertex: who is reachable, and by what cheapest route. This chapter asks a question about the whole graph at once: **you must connect all n vertices, every link has a price, and you want the cheapest set of links that leaves nobody cut off.** Cable runs between sites, the links of a cluster's overlay network, the edges kept by a clustering step. The answer is always a tree (any cycle has an edge you could drop for free), with n − 1 edges: a minimum spanning tree. It is **not** the shortest-path tree: on the running graph below the shortest-path tree from A weighs 12 and the minimum spanning tree weighs 11, because one question prices a route and the other prices a network.

Both algorithms of the chapter are greedy: take the cheapest thing that is safe. The chapter's real subject is **why that is correct here when greedy is wrong almost everywhere else** (Chapter 21 shows coin systems and 0/1 knapsack where it fails). The answer is one lemma, the **cut property**, plus its mirror, the **cycle property**. Topic 67 proves them and builds Kruskal on them (sort the edges, add an edge unless it closes a cycle, with union–find answering "closes a cycle?"); topic 68 reuses the identical lemma to justify a second algorithm that grows a single tree from a start vertex with a priority queue (Prim), and asks which of its two implementations to pick as the graph gets dense. Same lemma, two algorithms, two cost profiles: that is the pattern the chapter wants the learner to keep.

### Order of ideas, and why

1. **§19.1 Cut property and Kruskal's algorithm (67, full).** What a spanning tree and a minimum spanning tree are; the generic algorithm ("keep a set of edges that is part of some MST; add any *safe* edge"); the **cut property** (the lightest edge crossing any cut is in some MST) and the **cycle property** (the heaviest edge of any cycle is in no MST, with distinct weights) proved by one exchange argument; what ties do (the MST may not be unique, its weight is); then Kruskal: sort, scan, union–find. First, because the lemma is the chapter's content, and Kruskal is the algorithm that consumes it with the least extra machinery: every piece it needs (sort, union–find) the learner already owns.
2. **§19.1 Prim's algorithm (68, full).** The same lemma used the other way round: the cut is always "tree so far versus the rest", the safe edge is the lightest one leaving the tree. A priority queue of candidate edges (or of vertices keyed by their cheapest edge into the tree); the O(n²) array version against the heap version, and where the crossover sits (about m ≈ n² / log n, derived and measured, not asserted). Prim after Kruskal because its correctness is a one-line corollary of topic 67's lemma and its interest is entirely in the implementation trade-off: the structure that wins depends on m against n².
3. **Handoff to Chapter 20.** Topic 68 ends having answered "the cheapest network" with a lemma about cuts: a cut is the place a greedy choice is certified. Chapter 20 keeps the cut and changes the question: edges now carry *capacities*, the cut's value is the capacity crossing it, and the question is how much can be pushed from a source to a sink. The theorem that settles it is again a statement about cuts (max-flow equals min-cut), topic 69.

**Skipped in this chapter** (stay in the curriculum, get no reel, per `syllabus/review-notes/dsa-cuts.md`, "Chapter 19 — Minimum Spanning Trees (1 of 3)"): **Borůvka and parallel MST** (§19.1: simultaneous cheapest-edge contraction in O(log n) rounds, the randomized linear-time Karger–Klein–Tarjan algorithm, single-linkage clustering as an MST cut). They are named once, with their citations, in topic 68's post-notes "Where this goes next", and never built. The syllabus's own *Lab* ("implement Kruskal, Prim and Borůvka; compare them on sparse and dense graphs, then parallelize Borůvka and measure speed-up") is kept for Kruskal and Prim only, in topic 68's post-notes lab; the Borůvka and parallel half is left out (see "Notes for the release run").

### Shared notation and terms

- **Graph:** G = (V, E), **n = |V|**, **m = |E|**, weights w(e) real numbers (negative weights are allowed for MST; unlike shortest paths they cause no trouble, and the post-notes say why). A **spanning tree** is a subset of n − 1 edges that connects all of V and has no cycle; a **minimum spanning tree** (MST) minimizes the total weight. A disconnected graph has a **spanning forest** and Kruskal returns it unchanged.
- **Cut and crossing edge:** a cut is a partition (S, V∖S); an edge **crosses** it if its endpoints lie on different sides; a cut **respects** a set of edges A if no edge of A crosses it. An edge is **safe** for A if A ∪ {e} is still contained in some MST. Written as CLRS writes them, so a reader of the book recognises the generic algorithm.
- **Distinct weights:** the statements about uniqueness assume distinct weights; with ties the MST is a *set of trees of equal weight*, said once, in topic 67.
- **Union–find:** exactly Chapter 11's `find` and `union`; α(n) is the inverse Ackermann function, never a load factor (the Chapter 1 and 11 convention). Heap operations are Chapter 6's, named `push`, `pop-min`, `decrease-key` where a variant needs it (`decrease-key` is declared in topic 68; the heap of Chapter 6 offers sift-up, which is what a decrease-key is).
- **Spoken numbers:** narration has no digits, symbols or code names (automatic check); say "n minus one edges", "the cut property", "the cycle property", "safe edge", "minimum spanning tree"; every number in words (QUALITY.md #29). The names Kruskal, Prim, Jarník, Borůvka go in the lexicon the first time they appear in a script.
