## The chapter

### The problem it solves

Time ranges are the most common non-numeric key a data engineer meets: a meeting, a session, a lease, a job's start and end, a billing period, a log span. Two questions recur in every system that holds them. *Which of these overlap?* (merge them, count them, schedule around them, refuse the double-booking) and *what is true at each moment?* (how many are active, what is the tallest, what is the union). A scan comparing every pair answers either in O(n²), which is what a naive range join does and what a database avoids (DuckDB's blog measures a 100,000-row inequality self-join at 21.4 s for a nested loop against 0.28 s for its IEJoin, read at planning time).

This chapter's one idea is that **sorting turns a two-dimensional question into a one-dimensional walk**. Sort the interval endpoints once, walk them left to right, and at every coordinate the only things that matter are the intervals *currently open*. Topic 93 uses the idea in its plainest form on intervals on a line (merge, schedule, count rooms) and finds out which greedy rule is safe and why, with a dynamic programme for the case where greedy is not. Topic 94 generalises it into the **sweep line**: an event queue says when something changes, an active set says what is open right now, and the same skeleton solves the skyline, the area of a union of rectangles, and the reporting of every crossing among n segments in O((n + k) log n) for k crossings, the one bound in the chapter that depends on the *answer's* size as well as the input's.

### Order of ideas, and why

1. **§28.1 Interval merging and scheduling (93).** The one-dimensional case first, because it needs no geometry and every later idea is visible in it: sort by start and merge (the walk), sort by finish and take greedily (the safe greedy rule, proved by exchange), count the overlap at the busiest point (the depth, which is also the number of rooms), and add weights (where greedy fails and a DP over the sorted order takes over). The topic ends on the observation the next one is built on: every one of these programs is a sweep over sorted endpoints with a tiny active set.
2. **§28.1 Sweep-line algorithms (94).** The skeleton named and generalised (events, active set), then three problems with three different active-set structures: a heap with lazy deletion (skyline), a segment tree of cover counts (union area), and a balanced tree ordered by the sweep position (segment intersection). 93 before 94: 94's active sets are the structures 93 only needed in miniature (a count, a heap of end times).
3. **§28.2 Computational geometry is skipped entirely** (orientation tests and robust predicates, convex hulls, Voronoi and Delaunay; `syllabus/review-notes/dsa-cuts.md`, "Chapter 28 (3 of 5)"). The chapter's title promises geometry and delivers its sweep half. Topic 94 needs exactly one geometric primitive (the sign of a cross product, to decide on which side of a segment a point lies and whether two segments cross) and introduces it for exact integer coordinates only; the floating-point failure of that primitive and Shewchuk's exact and adaptive predicates (Discrete & Computational Geometry 18, 1997) belong to the skipped topic and are named once in topic 94's post-notes. The syllabus's *Lab* for the chapter names the monotone-chain hull and robust orientation tests (skipped) and then "the meeting-rooms and skyline sweeps"; only the second half is in this chapter (see "Notes for the release run").
4. **Handoff to Chapter 29.** Topic 94 ends having shown that one sorted pass and a small structure solve problems whose naive form is quadratic, with the running time stated in the worst case on the input. Chapter 29 asks what changes when the algorithm is allowed to flip coins: a running time that is a *random variable*, and the arithmetic (expectation, indicators) needed to say what it is.

### Shared notation and terms

- **Interval** `[s, e)`, **half-open**, written `s ≤ x < e`; two intervals **overlap** iff `s₁ < e₂ and s₂ < e₁` (one line, derived in topic 93 and reused unchanged in 94). Closed intervals differ only in the tie rule at a shared endpoint; the topic says so once and never mixes the conventions. This is the convention PostgreSQL's range types use by default (`[)`, "standard form" in its documentation, read at planning time), which is why the choice is not arbitrary.
- **n** the number of intervals (or rectangles, segments), **d** the depth (the largest number of intervals open at one coordinate), **k** the number of reported answers (segment crossings).
- **Event** a coordinate where something changes (a start, an end, a crossing); **active set** the intervals open just after the sweep has handled all events at its current coordinate.
- **Spoken numbers:** narration has no digits, symbols or code names (automatic check). Say "half open interval", "earliest finish time", "min heap of end times", "n plus k, times log n"; every number in words (QUALITY.md #29). Names (Bentley, Ottmann, Shamos, Hoey, Kleinberg, Tardos) are checked against the pronunciation lexicon and added when missing.

The chapter builds from `site/src/reels/kit.tsx` and `DESIGN.md`'s briefing theme: black and white carry everything, colour is rare and means something. On top of that this chapter shares one grammar:

- **The time axis is a horizontal ruler, drawn the same way in both topics;** every interval is a bar on its own row (or on its assigned room's row), its endpoints labelled with real coordinates. The sweep is a **vertical hairline** that advances event by event (`Draw`), never an instant jump.
- **Colour:** `crema` an interval or building that exists; `latte` secondary labels (a weight, a coordinate, a room number); `taupe` an interval not yet reached by the sweep or already discarded; `caramel` **the thing to look at**: the interval the algorithm just chose, or the active set at the sweep's current position (the solid shape, plus a `Reticle` on the decisive event); `cinnamon` (rare) **the alternative being compared**, always the same side of a pair: the wrong greedy rule's pick set beside the right one's; `cherry` with `cherryText` **the bad outcome**: a conflict (two chosen bars overlapping), a booking turned away that a better rule would have kept, or the n² pair count a scan pays.
- **The active set is drawn as what it is:** in 93 a small stack of rooms with the current end time on each; in 94 a heap (skyline), a column of cover counts under the ruler (union area) and the vertically ordered list of segments crossing the sweep line (intersection). Never an abstract box labelled "active set".
- **Reel shape.** Both topics are full (about 13–15 minutes, 2–4 predict questions, the first within the first four steps). Topic 93 opens on a choice between greedy rules, topic 94 on a count of pairs a scan would test.

### 93. Interval merging and scheduling (`dsa.interval-merging-scheduling`): full, §28.1

- **Folder:** `interval-merging-scheduling`. About 14 min.
- **Scope (syllabus clause → what it delivers):**
  - "sort-by-start merging and insertion": merging n intervals into the minimal set of disjoint ones: sort by start, keep the current merged interval, extend its end or start a new one; O(n log n), the sort being the whole cost; why it is correct (an invariant: everything left of the cursor is final); the half-open overlap predicate derived (`s₁ < e₂ and s₂ < e₁`) and the tie at a shared endpoint decided once; **insertion** into an already merged, sorted list (find the first interval that ends after the new one starts, absorb the run of overlapping ones, splice): O(n) with a linear scan, O(log n + r) to find the place by binary search when the list is an array and r intervals are absorbed (the shifting of elements is still O(n); the topic says which cost is which). Real instance: Apache Flink's session windows ("creates a new window for each arriving record and merges windows together if they are closer to each other" than the session gap, read in the Flink documentation at planning time; the stable docs page read was for Flink 2.3.0, to be recorded at research time) are exactly this merge; the topic shows sessionization as the merge pass, and leaves the windowing semantics (event time, watermarks) to Data Engineering.
  - "earliest-finish-time greedy for maximum non-overlapping intervals": the problem (largest set of pairwise non-overlapping intervals), three plausible greedy rules (earliest start and shortest first, each beaten on the running data; fewest conflicts, beaten on the separate eight-interval set in the table above) and the one that is safe: sort by finish, take an interval whenever it starts no earlier than the last taken one finished. The proof is an **exchange argument** (Chapter 21's idea, used here and declared): any optimal set can have its first interval swapped for the earliest-finishing one without losing size or creating an overlap, then induct. O(n log n). Stated as the unweighted case of maximum independent set in an interval graph (one sentence; the topic does not teach graph colouring).
  - "minimum rooms via a min-heap of end times": the interval partitioning problem; sort by start; keep a min-heap of the end times of the rooms in use; for each interval, if the earliest-ending room has ended by this interval's start, reuse it (pop and push the new end), else open a room (push); the heap's size at the end is the answer; O(n log n). **Why that is optimal:** the number of rooms can never be below the **depth** (the maximum overlap at any point), and the algorithm opens a new room only when all rooms in use are busy at that start, which witnesses a point of depth equal to the rooms now open. The same number is computed by an even simpler sweep over +1/−1 events (sorted endpoints, running count); the topic shows both and says they are the same walk, which is the bridge to topic 94. Real instances the topic names without building: meeting-room allocation, and the number of concurrent connections or jobs a set of start/end logs implies.
  - "weighted interval scheduling by DP": with weights, earliest-finish greedy fails (the running data: weight 28 against the optimum 29); the DP over intervals sorted by finish, `OPT(j) = max(OPT(j−1), wⱼ + OPT(p(j)))`, with `p(j)` the last interval that ends no later than `j` starts, found by binary search (Chapter 16's invariants, declared); O(n log n) overall (sort plus n binary searches plus an O(n) table), with the chosen set reconstructed by walking back. Optimal substructure and overlapping subproblems named with Chapter 22's vocabulary (declared), not re-taught; the naive recursion's exponential blow-up shown once against the memoised table. The topic names, without proving, the sharp contrast the sources state: choosing a maximum-weight set of non-overlapping intervals is polynomial, while the same problem when each job is offered at several alternative time slots is NP-complete in general (Wikipedia's "Interval scheduling" article states the NP-completeness for groups of three or more intervals; read at planning time, to be confirmed from a primary source at research time before it is said).

### 94. Sweep-line algorithms (`dsa.sweep-line-algorithms`): full, §28.1

- **Folder:** `sweep-line-algorithms`. About 15 min.
- **Scope (syllabus clause → what it delivers):**
  - "event queues and active-set structures": the skeleton: an imaginary line moves across the plane; the only coordinates where the answer can change are **events** (finitely many, usually known up front and sorted); between events the **active set** (what the line currently crosses) does not change; the algorithm is "for each event in order: update the active set, then record what changed". The two design questions for every sweep: what are the events, and what structure holds the active set with which operations. **Tie-breaking at equal coordinates** is where sweeps go wrong: with half-open intervals, ends are processed before starts at the same x; the topic shows one bug from getting it backwards (an interval counted as overlapping its neighbour). Cost: sorting the events is O(n log n), and each event costs one active-set operation. Topic 93's depth sweep is the first instance (the active set is a count).
  - "skyline": given buildings `(left, right, height)`, output the outline's key points. Events: left and right edges; active set: the heights of the buildings the line crosses, in a **max-heap with lazy deletion** (a building is removed only when it surfaces at the top and its right edge has passed); a key point is emitted when the maximum changes. O(n log n) (n pushes and n pops on the heap, plus the sort). The topic contrasts it with Chapter 2's monotonic deque (which handles a window of fixed order, not arbitrary expiry). The running data's skyline (key points (2,10) (3,15) (7,12) (12,0) (15,10) (20,8) (24,0)) is the picture.
  - "rectangle union area": the area of the union of n axis-parallel rectangles in O(n log n) by sweeping a vertical line: events are the left and right edges of rectangles; the active set is the set of y-intervals currently covered, kept in a **segment tree over the compressed y-coordinates** storing, per node, a cover count and the covered length beneath it (no lazy push-down is needed: a count is added at the O(log n) canonical nodes of an interval and the length is recomputed on the way up); between consecutive events the contribution is (covered length) × (Δx). This is Klee's measure problem in two dimensions; Bentley's O(n log n) algorithm (1977) is the source, and the d-dimensional extension (time O(n^(d−1) log n) per the survey literature, to verify at research time) is named in post-notes. The running data's union (29, against 34 for the sum of the areas) is the number.
  - "Bentley–Ottmann segment intersection in O((n + k) log n)": report every crossing among n line segments. Events: segment endpoints (known) and crossings (discovered); active set: the segments crossing the sweep line, in a balanced search tree **ordered by y at the sweep's current x**; two segments can only cross after becoming neighbours in that order, so each time a segment is inserted, removed or two segments swap places, only the new neighbour pairs are tested and any crossing found is pushed into the event queue (a priority queue keyed by x). Correctness in one invariant: the order in the tree is always the true vertical order at the sweep position, and every crossing is detected before the sweep reaches it. Cost: n + k events each costing O(log n), so O((n + k) log n); the output-sensitive form against the O(n²) all-pairs test (at k = O(n) the sweep is O(n log n); at k = Θ(n²) it is slower than the scan by a log factor). The O(n log n) part is necessary in algebraic decision-tree models (the survey's statement; **the relation to Chapter 1's element distinctness is the topic's one reduction, to verify against the source before it is said**); asymptotically faster algorithms exist (Chazelle and Edelsbrunner 1992; Balaban 1995) and are named, not built. **Degenerate inputs** (three segments through one point, vertical segments, shared endpoints) are the algorithm's real-world difficulty: the topic works in general position with exact integer coordinates, says what breaks otherwise, and names the primitive it relies on: the sign of a cross product (`cross-product-orientation`), exact on integers; floating-point failure of that sign and the exact and adaptive predicates that repair it belong to the skipped "Orientation tests and robust predicates" and are named once in post-notes. Shamos and Hoey's earlier algorithm (FOCS 1976, pp. 208–215) decides only whether *any* two segments cross, in O(n log n), and is named as the ancestor.
- **Leaves to other topics:** the 1-D merge, greedy and DP programs → topic 93; the segment tree's construction and lazy propagation → Chapter 8 (used, not rebuilt; the union-area tree is a *non-propagating* use, said once); balanced search tree internals → Chapter 4; convex hulls, Voronoi, Delaunay, robust predicates → skipped (§28.2); rotating-calipers and Fortune's sweep named in post-notes only; spatial indexes for static point and region queries → Chapter 9 (space-filling curves are the only live topic there); sweeps in databases (interval joins, the merge step of an IEJoin) → Data Engineering, named in post-notes with the DuckDB measurement read at planning time.

- **Hook:** a thousand segments could cross in nearly half a million places; a scan tests every pair. Draw eight random segments and the pair count is 28 while only a couple cross. How many pairs does a sweep need to look at? Predict: all of them, the ones that overlap in x, or only pairs that become neighbours. The answer is the last, and a bound that counts the crossings themselves.
