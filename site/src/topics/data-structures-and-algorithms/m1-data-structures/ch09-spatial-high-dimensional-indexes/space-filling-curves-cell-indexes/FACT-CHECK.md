# Fact-check — Space-filling curves and cell indexes

## Claims table

| # | Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Z-order/Morton code maps multidimensional data to one dimension by bit-interleaving, preserving locality with high probability | Confirmed | `sources/zorder.txt`: "functions which are Z-order, Lebesgue curve, Morton space-filling curve... map multidimensional data to one dimension while preserving locality of the data points (two points close together in multidimensions with high probability lie also close together in Morton order)." |
| 2 | The z-value is calculated by bit-interleaving the binary representations of a point's own coordinates | Confirmed | `sources/zorder.txt`: "The z-value of a point in multidimensions is simply calculated by bit interleaving the binary representations of its coordinate values." |
| 3 | Once sorted by interleaved bits, any one-dimensional structure can index the result | Confirmed | `sources/zorder.txt`: "Once the data are sorted by bit interleaving, any one-dimensional data structure can be used, such as simple one dimensional arrays, binary search trees, B-trees, skip lists..." |
| 4 | Z-order preserves locality "well" but not perfectly | Confirmed | `sources/zorder.txt`: "Although Z-order is preserving locality well, for efficient range searches an algorithm is necessary for calculating... the next possible Z-value which is in the multidimensional search range" |

| 6 | A Hilbert curve preserves locality "fairly well," one-directionally | Confirmed | `sources/hilbert.txt`: "Both the true Hilbert curve and its discrete approximations are useful because they give a mapping between 1D and 2D space that preserves locality fairly well... | 8 | Geohash is built on Z-order, base-32 encoded | Confirmed | `sources/geohash.txt`: "It is a hierarchical spatial data structure which subdivides space into buckets of grid shape, which is one of the many applications of what is known as a Z-order curve" / "reinvented it, adding the use of base32 representation." |

| 10 | S2 projects a cube's six faces and runs a Hilbert curve on each | Confirmed | `sources/s2.txt`: "The top level of the hierarchy is obtained by projecting the six faces of a cube onto the unit sphere..." / "...consists of six Hilbert curves linked together to form a single continuous loop over the entire sphere." |

## Currency check

- Wikipedia "Z-order curve", "Hilbert curve", "Geohash": no fixed publication date (Wikipedia articles), fetched fresh 2026-10-24; the underlying mathematics (Morton 1966, Hilbert 1891, Niemeyer's geohash 2008) is historical and settled, not time-sensitive content that could be stale.
- S2Geometry official developer guide (`s2geometry.io`): no visible date on the page, but it is the project's own current documentation, consistent with topic 34's own precedent of treating official project docs without a date as current.

## Red team

1. **Does Z-order's own "preserves locality well" claim get contradicted by the measured numbers?** No — checked directly: `examples.py` measures a boundary-crossing pair at distance 22 in Z-order versus 1 in Hilbert, which is consistent with, not contradicting, the source's own careful "well" (not "perfectly") phrasing. The source itself names the exact failure mode (range queries need BIGMIN/LITMAX machinery because interleaving alone doesn't handle every case) that the measured boundary pair demonstrates concretely.
2. **Is the Hilbert-over-Z-order locality advantage actually measured, or just repeated from the source?** Measured directly: `examples.py` computes real Hilbert and Z-order codes for three fixed pairs and reports real integer distances, not a restatement of the source's qualitative "better locality-preserving behavior" claim. Caught during this check: the first draft of the near-neighbour pairs (three pairs picked without checking their own ordering effects first) produced a case — a pair near the grid's origin — where Hilbert's own distance (3) was *worse* than Z-order's (1) and tied with row-major. Rather than discarding that pair to keep a clean "Hilbert always wins" story, it is kept and reported honestly in `OUTLINE.md`'s "Claims not made" and in the reel's own "Prove it" takeaway, because the source's claim is about databases' aggregate case, not every single pair.
3. **Does the chosen boundary-crossing pair actually cross a real power-of-two quadrant boundary, not just look close to one?** Verified: with an 8×8 grid (3 bits per axis), the quadrant boundary at x=4 (binary 100) and y=4 (binary 100) is where the third bit flips for both coordinates' interleaved representation simultaneously; the pairs (3,4)-(4,4) and (0,3)-(0,4) were chosen specifically because they straddle exactly this boundary, confirmed by inspecting each pair's own binary coordinates before computing the codes, not by searching for whichever pair produced the most dramatic-looking gap.
4. **Is the Hilbert construction used in `examples.py` the standard one, not an ad hoc approximation?** Checked: the `xy2d` implementation (quadrant selection by the two high bits, rotate/reflect the remaining bits, recurse one level down) is the standard public-domain recursive construction attributed to the Hilbert curve's own well-known algorithmic form; it is verified structurally in this topic (a full bijection onto every one of the 64 cells, asserted directly) rather than cited as a textual quote, since no source fetched for this topic reproduced the algorithm's own code as extractable text (Wikipedia's "Hilbert curve" page keeps its example C code in a form the fetch tool does not extract as plain text).
5. **Predict-step distractor check** (`ZOrderPredict`, step 3): "keeps every neighbour pair close, with no exceptions" is ruled out by the source's own explicit BIGMIN/LITMAX caveat (if interleaving worked everywhere, no extra range-search machinery would be needed); "no better than row-major" is ruled out because the pair nearest the origin is the only measured case where Z-order ties row-major, not the general case — most adjacent pairs away from a boundary measure better under Z-order than under naive row-major at larger scales; "only works for horizontal neighbours" is ruled out structurally, since the interleaving treats both axes identically.
6. **Predict-step distractor check** (`HilbertPredict`, step 6): "any curve must jump at some boundary" is ruled out by the measured result itself — Hilbert's own distance at the identical boundary pair is 1, not a jump; "gives up the one-cell-per-visit guarantee" is ruled out because the Hilbert ordering is verified as a full bijection onto all 64 cells, identically to Z-order; "only defined for one dimension at a time" is ruled out since the construction is defined and built directly over the 2D grid throughout.
7. **Does naming geohash/S2/H3's one distinguishing choice each actually hold up against each one's own official source, rather than a secondary summary?** Checked: geohash's choice (Z-order + base-32 text) is from Wikipedia's own "Geohash" article, which states both the Z-order relationship and the base-32 addition directly; S2's choice (cube projection + per-face Hilbert curve) is from S2Geometry's own official developer guide, not a blog post; H3's choice (hexagonal cells for equidistant neighbours) is from H3's own official documentation's "Aggregation" page, which states the triangle/square/hexagon neighbour-distance comparison directly, not a third-party restatement.

## Corrections applied

- The original near-neighbour pair set included a diagonal pair whose own computed numbers were asserted from a hand-guess rather than computed; replaced with three pairs chosen deliberately (one at the grid's own origin, two crossing opposite-axis quadrant boundaries) and every number in `examples.py` is now computed and asserted directly, including the honest result at the origin pair (see red-team item 2 above and `OUTLINE.md`'s "Claims not made").
