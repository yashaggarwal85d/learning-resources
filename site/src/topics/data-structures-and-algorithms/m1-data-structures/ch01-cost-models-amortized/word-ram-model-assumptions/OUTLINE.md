## Learning outcomes

1. Say which operations the word-RAM charges one unit for (C-style arithmetic, comparisons, bitwise operations and shifts, a load or store at a computed address, each on one w-bit word) and why the word size is tied to log n from below (a word must hold a pointer or index) and bounded above (unbounded words would let one unit operation do the whole input's work).
2. Name two real costs the model hides, a cache miss and a branch misprediction, with their order of magnitude from a cited source (hundreds of cycles for a trip to main memory against a few for a cache hit, on 2007 hardware; tens of cycles for a misprediction, about the pipeline's length), and say which later topics expose them (8 and 9 model the hierarchy, 10 measures it; Ch.15 and Ch.16 go branchless).
3. Explain how integer keys beat the comparison model: digits as array indices (radix sort, w/log₂ n passes), halving the key's bits (van Emde Boas, O(log w): 64 → 32 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1), many keys compared in one word (fusion trees, O(log_w n)), and name the price of each (scattered writes; Θ(U) space unless hashed; constants that make fusion trees theory only).
4. Say for a given cost claim whether it was made in the comparison model or the word-RAM, and whether a comparison-model lower bound (proved in topic 4) binds it.

**The problem (opened in step 1, closed in step 14):** the vector holds a million sorted 64-bit keys; a request needs the predecessor of a key x. Binary search takes 20 comparisons. Is 20 a law? And what does each of those 20 "steps" cost on the machine the service runs on? Closed: 20 is a law only in the comparison model; on the word-RAM a vEB tree does it in 6 halvings (at Θ(U) space, or Θ(n) with hashing), a fusion tree in O(log n / log w) (theory only); and every one of those unit steps is a load that is either a cache hit or a trip to memory, and a comparison the pipeline either predicted or paid tens of cycles for.

3. **Predict: can anything beat twenty?** (`BeatTwenty`, h 300, 1.5 s, **predict**). *Question frame:* two columns: left, under a `cinnamon` label "comparison model", the counter **20** in cinnamon 34 units; right, under a `caramel` label "word-RAM", a caramel "?" 34 units. The Deep dive has both arguments in full.

7. **A wrong guess flushes the pipeline** (`Flush`, h 340, ≈ 5 s). *Visual idea:* a pipeline of eight stage boxes filling with the instructions of the guessed path, then emptying in one `cherry` flush when the comparison resolves the other way; the model counter says 1 for the comparison. *Portrait:* a row of 8 `taupe` stage boxes (each 60 × 44) at y 120, labelled "pipeline" (latte, above left); above right the model counter "model: 1 unit" (crema 15); below (y 220) the guess "x < a[mid]? predicted: yes" in latte 15; at y 290 the penalty label. *Landscape:* same row, wider boxes. *Motion:* crema instruction chips enter the pipeline from the left on a 0.35 s beat (8 chips); at 3.2 s a caramel marker at the right end reads "resolved: no"; all chips turn `cherry` and drop out over 0.4 s; the row refills from empty while the label "lost: 15–25 clock cycles ≈ the pipeline's length" appears (`C.cherryText` 17). *Real values:* 15, 25, 1, 8 (stages; drawn only, but assert it). *Final frame:* a half-refilled pipeline, the penalty label, footnote `taupe` 13: "Zen 5, measured by Agner Fog (2026)". *Caption:* a pipelined core guesses which way the branch goes and runs ahead; a wrong guess throws that work away and refills: Agner Fog's measurements put the penalty at 10–20 cycles on the Intel cores he lists and 15–25 on Zen 5, about the length of the pipeline; the model charges 1 for the comparison; binary search's comparison depends on the data, so a predictor has nothing to learn from; this is the **branch misprediction** (introduced here); branchless search is Ch.16, branchless partitioning Ch.15; Data Engineering's "CPU caches, SIMD and vectorization" reel covers the same costs from the other side.

8. **The comparison model: one bit per question** (`OneBit`, h 360, ≈ 5 s). *Visual idea:* keys as face-down cards that only answer "<": each answer is one bit, and 9 possible places for x need 4 answers; a million need 20. *Portrait:* a row of 8 face-down `crema` cards (each 50 × 70, a "?" on each in taupe) at y 90, 9 gap marks under them in `taupe` (y 180); the query x as a `caramel` card above the row; the question "x < this?" and its answer "yes / no → 1 bit" in cinnamon 17 at y 230; a counter "answers 0" (cinnamon 24) at y 300 left and "places left 9" (crema 17) right. *Landscape:* same row, counters at right. *Motion:* on a 0.8 s beat x hovers over a card, a cinnamon "<" flashes, the gaps that are ruled out fade: 9 → 5 → 3 → 2 → 1, the answer counter 1 → 4. *Real values:* 8 cards, 9 places, 4 answers, "1,000,001 places → 20 answers" in the final line. *Final frame:* one lit gap, "answers 4", the line "9 places: 4 answers. 1,000,001 places: 20." *Caption:* the **comparison model** (introduced here): keys are opaque, the only operation on them is a comparison, and a comparison answers one yes/no question; telling n + 1 places apart needs log₂(n + 1) answers; lec11: in the BST model predecessor queries take Θ(log n); the lower bounds, Ω(log n) for search and Ω(n log n) for sorting, are proved in topic 4, not here.

9. **On the word-RAM, a key is a number** (`KeyIsNumber`, h 360, ≈ 4.5 s). *Visual idea:* the same 64-bit key row, cut three ways: used whole as an address, split into two halves, split into four digits; each cut names an escape. *Portrait:* the key row at y 50 (crema bits); three copies below it appear in turn (y 130, 210, 290), each with a `caramel` bracket and one label at the right end: "index an array" (a short caramel arrow into a cell drawn at the far right), "two halves of 32 → van Emde Boas", "four digits of 16 → radix sort"; the fusion-tree escape is a footnote in `latte`: "many keys' sketches in one word → fusion trees". *Landscape:* the three cuts side by side under the key. *Motion:* 0.8 s beats, bracket then label. *Real values:* 64, 32, 16, 4. *Final frame:* three cuts, three names. *Caption:* on the word-RAM a key is a number: it can be an address, it can be cut into bit fields, two keys can be subtracted; this is exactly what the comparison model forbids (Pătraşcu–Thorup: with C's integer instructions "we are no longer limited by the Ω(lg n) comparison based lower bound for searching"); three escapes follow, one each.

10. **Radix sort: digits as addresses** (`RadixPasses`, h 380, ≈ 7 s). *Visual idea:* a 64-bit key as four 16-bit digits, each pass scattering a million keys into 65,536 buckets by one digit and collecting them back; four passes against twenty million comparisons. *Portrait:* top (y 50): the key row with four `caramel` brackets "16 | 16 | 16 | 16", the active digit's bracket brighter; middle (y 120–230): a bucket strip of 60 drawn slots standing for 65,536 (label `taupe` "65,536 buckets, one per digit value"), `crema` dots scatter into slots and are collected back into a strip below; bottom (y 290–350): the tally, left in caramel 20 "4 passes × 1,000,000 keys = 4,000,000 moves", right in cinnamon 17 "n · log₂ n ≈ 20,000,000 comparisons". *Landscape:* key and buckets left, tally right. *Motion:* four passes on a 1.4 s beat, "pass 1 of 4 … pass 4 of 4" (latte 15) above the buckets, the active bracket lit; the tally lines land after pass 4. *Real values:* 64, 16, 4, 65,536, 1,000,000, 4,000,000, 20,000,000 (and 19.9 if the caption shows log₂). *Final frame:* sorted strip, both tallies. *Caption:* a digit of log₂ n bits is an array index; counting sort places n keys by one digit in O(n), stably, so w/log₂ n passes sort w-bit keys: O(n · w / log n), linear when w = O(log n) (lec14); for a million 64-bit keys, 16-bit digits give 4 passes; the price the unit hides: every pass scatters writes across 65,536 buckets, so each move is the load of step 6 in disguise; mechanism and engineering in Ch.15 "Counting, radix and bucket sort". The 20,000,000 is n · log₂ n, the order a comparison sort needs (topic 4).

11. **van Emde Boas: halve the bits, not the keys** (`HalveBits`, h 380, ≈ 7 s). *Visual idea:* the key row splits down the middle into cluster and position, then the half splits again, and again: a chain 64 → 32 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1 builds in caramel beside the cinnamon 20 of step 1. The halving of bits, drawn on the same row that the comparison model could only hold up against another key, is the click. *Portrait:* the key row at y 50 with its two halves bracketed "high 32: cluster 3" / "low 32: position 7" (caramel 15; the key is x = 3·2³² + 7); below (y 120–200) the structure: a `taupe` summary strip above a row of cluster boxes, cluster 3 lit in caramel; at y 230–330 the chain "64 → 32 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1" in caramel 20 units, growing one link per beat, with the counter "halvings 6" (caramel 26) at the right and, small beside it in cinnamon 17, "20" with the label "comparisons" to carry the contrast. *Landscape:* key and structure left, chain right. *Motion:* 0.7 s beats: halves bracket, cluster lights, then one link per 0.6 s. *Real values:* 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1, 6, 20, 3, 7. *Final frame:* the whole chain, "halvings 6" against "20", footnote `taupe` 13: "each level stores its min, so a query recurses once". *Caption:* a vEB tree over u = 2ʷ keys holds √u clusters of √u keys each plus a summary of which clusters are non-empty; the high half of the key picks the cluster, the low half the position in it; every level stores its minimum and maximum directly, so predecessor recurses once, into the cluster or into the summary (lec11: "we only recurse once"); each level halves the number of bits: T(u) ≤ T(√u) + O(1), which is O(log log u) = O(log w) (topic 3 solves the recurrence); six halvings for 64-bit keys, whether n is a thousand or a billion. The **van Emde Boas tree** is introduced here; the Deep dive gives insert and delete.

12. **The price: Θ(U) space, unless you hash** (`SpacePrice`, h 340, ≈ 4.5 s). *Visual idea:* the top-level cluster array drawn as a band of 2⁶⁴ slots that dwarfs the million keys, then collapsing to only the non-empty clusters when hashed. *Portrait:* a wide `taupe` band (520 units) at y 100 labelled "array of clusters: 2⁶⁴ slots" (latte 15); inside it, a `crema` sliver of 1 unit labelled "1,000,000 keys" with a leader line; below (y 230) the hashed version: a short row of caramel dots "only the non-empty clusters: Θ(n)"; the two bars' labels "Θ(U)" (taupe 22) and "Θ(n)" (caramel 22) at the right. *Landscape:* bands stacked the same way, wider. *Motion:* band draws, sliver appears with its label (1.2 s), band dissolves into the dotted row (1.5 s). *Real values:* 2⁶⁴ (written as a power, as topic 1 does), 1,000,000. *Final frame:* the two bars with Θ(U) and Θ(n). *Caption:* the plain structure needs Θ(u) words (lec11: "O(log(w)) time per query, and Θ(u) space"), impossible for u = 2⁶⁴; store only non-empty clusters in hash tables and the space is Θ(n) with the O(log w) query bound holding with high probability (lec11 §6.1; the hashing itself is Ch.3's business and is named, not taught); topic 1's time–space trade-off, with the time bought at the price of space or randomness. Pătraşcu–Thorup proved this bound optimal for polynomial universes in near-linear space (Deep dive, frontier).

13. **Fusion trees: four comparisons in one subtraction** (`Sketch`, h 400, ≈ 7 s). *Visual idea:* four 8-bit keys collapse to their two distinguishing bits, line up in one word, and one subtraction tells where the query sits among all four. Word-level parallelism as a picture: the keys shrink until they fit side by side. *Portrait:* four key rows of 8 cells (24 units each, 192 wide) at y 40, 80, 120, 160 with their values at the right in crema 15: "00010101", "00011011", "01100100", "01101110"; the two distinguishing columns (bit 6 and bit 3, counted from 0 at the right) light `caramel`; each row's sketch, 2 cells, forms at x 420: "00", "01", "10", "11"; at y 230 the node word as 12 cells "1 00 | 1 01 | 1 10 | 1 11" (the 1s in caramel), label `latte` "node word: a 1 before each sketch"; at y 280 the query 25 = "00011001" with its sketch "01" and the query word "0 01 | 0 01 | 0 01 | 0 01"; at y 330 the difference "011 100 101 110" with the leading bit of each block lit: 0 1 1 1; at y 375 the verdict in caramel 20: "3 keys ≥ q: q sits after the first → predecessor 21". *Landscape:* keys and sketches left, the three words right. *Motion:* 0.8 s beats: columns light, sketches form, node word assembles, query appears, subtraction, leading bits read. *Real values:* the binary strings above; 21, 27, 100, 110, 25; 2 (bits), 3 (keys ≥ q). *Final frame:* everything in place, the verdict. *Caption:* a fusion-tree node holds about w^(1/5) keys; at most k − 1 bit positions distinguish k keys (lec12), so each key's sketch is tiny and all of them fit in one word (o(w) bits); put a 1 before each sketch, subtract the query's sketch from every block at once, and the leading bit of each block says whether that key is ≥ the query (lec12 §7); so a node is searched in O(1) and the tree's height is Θ(log_w n) = O(log n / log w); the sketch is computed with multiplication (lec12 §6) and Fredman and Willard say the constants make it "theoretical interest only"; a query whose sketch lands in the wrong place needs one more step (Deep dive, desketchifying). The **fusion tree** and **word-level parallelism** are introduced here.

14. **Back to the problem** (`BackToProblem`, h 380, ≈ 4 s). *Visual idea:* the three counts for the opening problem side by side with their model and their price, so the learner reads "which model, which cost, what it hides" off one card. *Portrait:* three columns at x 100, 300, 500: headers in latte 15 "comparison model" / "van Emde Boas" / "fusion tree"; the numbers "20" (cinnamon 34), "6" (caramel 34), "log n / log w" (caramel 20); under them in taupe 13 (two lines each, stacked): "proved a floor in topic 4" / "Θ(U) space, or hash" / "theory only"; a strip at y 300 across all columns: "every unit step: a load, hit or miss; a compare, predicted or flushed" in latte 15 (one line in landscape, two in portrait). *Motion:* columns land left to right on 0.8 s beats, the strip last. *Real values:* 20, 6. *Final frame:* the card. *Caption:* the problem answered: binary search's 20 is a law of the comparison model only; the word-RAM allows 6 halvings (at the price of space or hashing) and, on paper, O(log n / log w); and the unit the counts were made in hides a hit-or-miss load and a predicted-or-flushed compare, which topics 8–10 price and measure; from here, say of every bound: which model, and what its unit hides.

## Deep dive (`index.mdx`) headings and what each covers

- `## After this topic you can` — the four outcomes.
- `## Into the internals`
  - `### The word-RAM, precisely` — memory as an array of S cells of w bits addressed by words; registers; the fixed set of operations each modifying O(1) cells; the operation list as lec11 gives it ("+, −, ∗, /, %, &, |, ^, >>, <<, <, >", "as you would expect as primitives in … C") and as Mehlhorn–Sanders list it (including div, mod, XOR); loads and stores at computed addresses; the **transdichotomous RAM** as the 6.851 notes name the model whose word grows with the problem (w > log S, so w ≥ log n; "Words serve as pointers"); the fixed-universe assumption U = {0, …, u − 1} with u = 2ʷ; why the word is bounded above: Mehlhorn–Sanders ("integers bounded by a polynomial in the size of the data … can be stored in a single cell", "allowing cells to store arbitrary numbers would lead to absurdly overoptimistic algorithms", repeated squaring gives a 2ⁿ-bit number in n steps) and Fredman–Willard (Paul and Simon's linear-time sort "suffers from an abuse of the unit cost assumption"; the b-bit word model in which each input "fits into one word"); the model as an abstraction of real machines (Mehlhorn–Sanders: fixed 32- or 64-bit registers against a word that scales); the neighbouring models in one paragraph: cell-probe (only reads and writes cost; for lower bounds; lec11 §2.3, Pătraşcu–Thorup) and the BST and pointer-machine models (lec11 §3), with "topic 4 proves lower bounds in such models". Says plainly that the word "transdichotomous" is used here as the course notes use it and that this topic attributes its coinage to no one.
  - `### Which operations are unit cost is a choice` — multiplication as the contested operation: the AC⁰ RAM forbids it because it needs logarithmic circuit depth (lec12), a restriction that mattered "when multiplication was very costly … no longer the case due to … pipelining" (lec12); fusion trees need it (Fredman–Willard's instruction set "addition, subtraction, multiplication, comparison, and bit-wise AND"; lec12's approximate sketch "using just multiplication and other standard operations"); OR and XOR from AND and subtraction (Fredman–Willard); Pătraşcu–Thorup's "unit-cost word RAM" wording; randomization and integer division as further choices (Fredman–Willard's randomized variant). Framing: a bound's model names its instruction set; "O(1)" for a multiply is a modelling choice the standard word-RAM makes and the AC⁰ RAM does not. Hagerup 1998 is not cited (not opened).
  - `### Numbers longer than a word` — ⌈b/w⌉ words; addition Θ(b/w), comparison Θ(b/w) in the worst case, schoolbook multiplication Θ((b/w)²), executed in `examples.py` on 4,096-bit numbers (64 words; 64 word additions; 4,096 word multiplies); Mehlhorn–Sanders on spreading a cell over logarithmically many cells "with a logarithmic overhead in time and space"; back-reference to topic 1's Θ(b) and "n is the size of the input, in bits" (pseudo-polynomial): the value-vs-length point is the same point one word up.
  - `### What the unit hides` — the memory hierarchy with Drepper's Pentium M table (register ≤ 1, L1d ~3, L2 ~14, main memory ~240 cycles; "200+ cycles"; "450 cycles and more" under TLB pressure), dated 2007 and labelled as such, and the measured staircase ("below 10 … around 28 … 480 cycles and more" as the working set outgrows L1d and L2); cache lines of 64 bytes; the binary-search arithmetic (20 probes, 17 distinct lines for the query in `examples.py`); Agner on misses ("forget about everything else and concentrate on … cache misses"); branch prediction: speculative execution, the flush, "The number of wasted clock cycles is approximately equal to the length of the pipeline", and the measured penalties by core (10–20 PPro/P2/P3; ~13 PM, 15 Core2; ≥ 17; 15–20; 16 typical, 20 max; ~18 Zen 1–3; 15–18 Zen 4; 15–25 Zen 5), with Agner's edition date; the list of what else the model leaves out, one line each: SIMD and vector lanes (the model's own "limited form of parallelism" is one w-bit word per step, Mehlhorn–Sanders), multicore parallelism, memory allocation and freeing (counted as unit or not at all), virtual memory and the TLB (Drepper ch. 4; topic 10), I/O (topic 8). Forward pointers: 8, 9, 10; Ch.15, Ch.16; Data Engineering's "CPU caches, SIMD and vectorization".
  - `### The comparison model and its escapes` — the model; one bit per comparison and log₂(n + 1) for n + 1 places; lec11's Θ(log n) in the BST model; Pătraşcu–Thorup's "no longer limited by the Ω(lg n) comparison based lower bound"; the lower bounds as topic 4's; the three escapes in a table (structure, bound, space, price, source); the combination min{log w, log_w n} ≤ O(√log n) (lec11 §3.2, as the notes state it); x-fast and y-fast trees named with their bounds (lec11 §6); the chapter note that vEB and fusion trees have no other home in the syllabus, so this topic carries them.
  - `### van Emde Boas trees in full` — the structure (clusters, summary, min and max stored at every level; high(x), low(x)); successor/predecessor, insert and delete as lec11 §4 describes them, each recursing once; T(u) ≤ T(√u) + O(1) and its solution O(log log u) (forward to topic 3); the binary-tree view (lec11 §5); space Θ(u) and the fix: hash tables for non-empty clusters give Θ(n) space, updates then O(w) unless indirection is added (lec11 §6.1, quoted); the toy in `examples.py` (dictionary clusters, w = 16 and w = 64, checked against `bisect`, recursion depth ≤ log₂ w).
  - `### Fusion trees: the idea, and what is left out` — B-tree with branching w^(1/5) and height Θ(log_w n); sketching (≤ k − 1 distinguishing bits, order preserved, total o(w) bits); the approximate sketch by multiplication (lec12 §6, named only); parallel comparison (the 1-prefix, the subtraction, the leading bits; counting the 1s by a multiplication, lec12 §7); desketchifying (lec12 §5: "the location of sketch(q) can be completely different from the location of q", and how the longest common prefix fixes it); most significant set bit in O(1) (lec12 §8, named); dynamic variants (exponential trees O(log_w n + lg lg n), hashing O(log_w n) expected; lec12); O(n) space; Fredman–Willard's own verdict on practicality; the toy in `examples.py` (four 8-bit keys, exact sketch, parallel compare). States that the full construction is out of scope.
  - `### Radix sort on the word-RAM` — direct-access sort Θ(u) ("Θ(n) if u = Θ(n)", 6.006); tuple sort and stability (6.006: "Want sort to be stable"); c = log_n u digits base n, O(cn), linear for u ≤ nᶜ with constant c (6.006); lec14's statement "linear time if w = O(lg n) using radix sort"; the pass table for w = 64 (r = 8: 8 passes; 16: 4; 20: 4; 32: 2) from `examples.py`; what a pass costs on a real machine (scattered writes across 2ʳ buckets); mechanism and engineering in Ch.15.

## Examples to execute (`examples.py`) and what each must assert

1. **Binary search over the vector.** `n = 1_000_000`; a sorted list of n keys (seeded 64-bit keys, or `range`); a predecessor search by halving that counts comparisons; for a fixed query and for a sample of 2,000 random queries, the count is ≤ 20 and equals 20 for the fixed query; `math.log2(10**6)` rounds to 19.9; `math.ceil(math.log2(n + 1)) == 20`; the halving sequence of range lengths starts 1,000,000, 500,000, 250,000, 125,000, 62,500, 31,250, 15,625; `n + 1 == 1_000_001`; `n * math.log2(n)` is between 19,900,000 and 20,000,000 (shown as "≈ 20,000,000"); `math.log2(10**9)` rounds to 29.9 and up to 30.

Addresses 0, 1, 2 appear.
