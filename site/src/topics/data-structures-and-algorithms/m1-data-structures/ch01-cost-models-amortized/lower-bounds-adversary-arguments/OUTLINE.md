## Learning outcomes

1. Prove the Ω(n log n) comparison-sorting bound from the decision tree: n! leaves force height ⌈log₂ n!⌉ in the worst case, and average leaf depth at least ⌊log₂ n!⌋ over random orders.
2. Use an adversary to prove a tight bound: n − 1 comparisons for the maximum, ⌈3n/2⌉ − 2 for the minimum and maximum together, and say what makes an adversary valid (every answer consistent with some input; no assumption about the algorithm).
3. Turn the number of possible answers K into the information-theoretic bound ⌈log₂ K⌉ (search, sorting, merging), and recognise when an adversary beats it (maximum, merging, element distinctness).
4. Say exactly which algorithms a lower bound rules out (comparison algorithms for that problem, in the worst or average case) and which it doesn't (radix sort and hashing, better constants, easy inputs, a different problem), and tell a problem's lower bound from one algorithm's cost.

**The problem (opened in step 1, closed in step 14):** three claims about the vector of a million 64-bit keys, each made by a colleague who "only uses comparisons": (A) a sort that orders *any* million keys with at most 15,000,000 comparisons; (B) a routine that finds the minimum and the maximum with at most 1,200,000; (C) a check that says whether all million keys are distinct with at most 5,000,000. None is refuted by testing: each might pass every test you write. By step 14 all three are proved impossible for every comparison algorithm (A and C: some input needs at least 18,488,885; B: some input needs at least 1,499,998), and two of them become possible the moment the keys are used as numbers (radix sort for A, a hash set for C, with what each gives up).

5. **The average order pays it too** (`AverageDepth`, h 380, duration 4.4).
   - *Visual idea:* a lopsided tree has a few cheap inputs and many expensive ones; pulling its deepest pair of leaves up under its shallowest leaf can only lower the average, until the tree is balanced. The learner watches the average fall to its floor, which is still about log₂ n!: luck doesn't help on average.
   - *Portrait:* three small six-leaf trees side by side (x centres 100, 300, 500; each about 160 wide), top-down, leaves as `crema` dots with their depth in `latte` 13 under each dot: tree 1, a caterpillar, depths 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 5 (sum 20); tree 2, depths 2, 2, 2, 3, 4, 4 (17); tree 3, depths 2, 2, 3, 3, 3, 3 (16; the shape of step 2's tree). Under each tree its average in `caramel` 15: "3.33", "2.83", "2.67". A `taupe` dashed floor line across the bottom labelled "log₂ 6 = 2.585" (13 units). Bottom line in `latte` 13: "a million keys: average ≥ 18,488,884". *Landscape:* identical.
   - *Motion:* tree 1 draws (0.2–0.8 s); its two depth-5 leaves lift along `caramel` arcs to hang under the depth-1 leaf, turning into tree 2 (1.2–2.0 s); the same move turns tree 2 into tree 3 (2.4–3.2 s); averages update with each tree; floor line and the million line at 3.6 s (end 4.0 s).
   - *Real values:* depth sums 20, 17, 16; averages 20/6 = 3.33, 17/6 = 2.83, 16/6 = 2.67; log₂ 6 = 2.585; 16 = the minimum depth sum of any binary tree with 6 leaves (dynamic programme in `examples.py`); ⌊log₂ 1,000,000!⌋ = 18,488,884.

   - *Caption's point:* the bound holds on average too: over all n! orders equally likely, any comparison sort's average leaf depth is at least ⌊log₂ n!⌋ (Northeastern's Theorem 5.2). The proof is the move you just watched: take the two deepest sibling leaves and hang them under the shallowest leaf; the average drops whenever the depths differ by 2 or more, so the cheapest tree is balanced, and a balanced tree with n! leaves has every leaf at depth ⌊log₂ n!⌋ or ⌈log₂ n!⌉. This is an average over inputs (topic 1's average case); the same notes extend it to a randomized sort's expected count, an average over its coins (Deep dive).

10. **The scoreboard: 14 units, 10 comparisons** (`Scoreboard`, h 400, duration 5.2). The chapter's visual idea for this topic.
    - *Visual idea:* the adversary as a referee keeping a four-column scoreboard. Every key starts **untested**; its first win moves it to **only won**, its first loss to **only lost**, and once it has both it is out (**both**). The units counter climbs to 14 while the comparisons counter stops at 10: the bound is the gap between the two counters.

(The syllabus's 3n/2 − 2 is this for even n.) The answers stay consistent: a key gets its value at its first comparison, above everything so far if it wins, below everything if it loses, and every later answer just reads the values (Deep dive).

12. **Distinctness: every neighbour must be compared** (`Neighbours`, h 380, duration 5.0).

So every "distinct" answer comes after all n − 1 neighbour comparisons, which spell out the whole sorted order: two different orders can't share a leaf, the tree has at least n! Sort-then-scan is optimal up to lower-order terms.   - `### A lower bound is a claim about every algorithm`: Erickson 12.1's definitions (an algorithm's worst case max over inputs; a problem's complexity min over algorithms of that), so an upper bound needs one algorithm and a lower bound needs all; why a model must be fixed first ("there is no formal definition of the phrase ‘all algorithms’"); the decision-tree model, its k-ary form and ⌈log_k N⌉; the comparison model as topic 2 defined it (only comparisons inform; moving keys is free) and Northeastern's Definition 5.1; why counting comparisons gives a lower bound on time but never an upper bound; "Comparison trees describe almost all well-known sorting algorithms … but not radix sort or bucket sort"; the hashing objection and Erickson's answer (a hash value is a query with more than a constant number of outcomes; "choosing the right model of computation is absolutely crucial"). The general principle that turns hard input distributions into randomized lower bounds belongs to a later randomized-algorithms topic (named, not taught).
  - `### Counting answers: when it is tight and when it is not`: the information-theoretic bound ⌈log_k K⌉ stated once; tight for search (n + 1 outcomes; binary search's ⌊log₂ n⌋ + 1 = ⌈log₂(n + 1)⌉ three-way probes; why three-way probes still meet a yes/no count: an "=" answer ends the search, so the probe tree branches two ways and 2ᵏ − 1 ≥ n, Amenta's Example 3; with yes/no comparisons only, "x < A[i]?" and "x = A[i]?", it takes ⌈log₂ n⌉ + 1, 21 at a million, because "equal" can no longer share a probe with "less" and "greater"; exhaustive for n ≤ 10), for sorting up to lower-order terms, for merging 4 + 4; loose for the maximum (⌈log₂ n⌉ against n − 1; "this bound is tight" only for unrestricted questions, Erickson), for min and max (⌈log₂ n(n − 1)⌉ = 40 at a million against 1,499,998), for merging (log₂ C(2n, n) ≈ 2n − ½ log₂ n − ½ log₂ π, NMSU, against 2n − 1, Amenta; exhaustive for n = 2 … 5: 3, 5, 7, 9 against counts 3, 5, 7, 8), for the median (Erickson 13.8 combines counting with two adversaries for 3n/2 − O(log n) and cites 2n − o(n); Waterloo's 2013 notes prove a weaker 1¾n by an adversary; the best known lower bound is about 2n, Bent and John 1985, and the best known algorithm uses 2.95n, both as the SEA 2025 selection paper cites them), and for element distinctness (1 against ⌈log₂ n!⌉). Second-largest: n + ⌈log₂ n⌉ − 2 by a tournament, and that this is also the lower bound (Kislitsyn 1964, as CMU 15-451 cites it for n a power of 2; the general V₂(n) = n − 2 + ⌈log₂ n⌉ from Erickson 13's exercise 5 and the SEA 2025 paper), so counting (⌈log₂ n⌉: the answer is one of n keys) is far off again.
  - `### Adversary arguments: the rules`: the three rules (answer consistently with some input at every moment; assume nothing about the algorithm; at the end, exhibit an input the algorithm gets wrong or a long run); the n-card monte (any algorithm that skips a bit is fooled: the trivial Ω(n) for reading the input, Erickson 13.2); the max's adversary (xᵢ = i, mark losers, raise an unmarked key: Erickson 12.6; CMU's equivalent "components" proof); the min/max referee in full: the four states (Waterloo's V, W, L, O; UMass's A, B, C, D; Erickson's + and − marks), the answer table (only-won beats only-lost; an untested key against an only-won key loses; and so on) and why each answer removes at most one unit except untested-against-untested; how the answers stay consistent (keep a value for every key; a key gets its value at its first comparison, above every value so far if the table says it wins, below every value so far if it loses; every comparison between two already-tested keys is answered by the current values. An only-won key then always beats an only-lost one, because whichever got its value later was placed above or below everything, including the other; earlier answers never change because values never change after a key's first comparison), checked against random algorithms in `examples.py` (the fact-check's simulation: 40,000 random runs for n = 5 … 10, every answer consistent, every run ≥ ⌈3n/2⌉ − 2, at most ⌊n/2⌋ two-unit comparisons); odd n and ⌈3n/2⌉ − 2. An adversary outside comparisons: connectivity of an n-vertex graph from adjacency-matrix probes needs all n(n − 1)/2 probes (CMU 15-451, Theorem 6.9; Erickson 13.5), with its strategy (answer "no edge" unless that would disconnect the graph).
  - `### Element distinctness in three models`: the comparison-model proof in full (the neighbour lemma; why a "distinct" leaf determines the order; why ties don't help the algorithm; exhaustive checks for n = 3, 4, 5); its one assumption, that comparisons are between two keys (the comparison model of topic 2 and Northeastern's Definition 5.1): if the algorithm could also compare a key with a constant c lying between two neighbours, moving one onto the other could change that answer, and the bound for such algorithms comes from the linear decision tree and algebraic models below instead; with yes/no comparisons that have no "=" answer the same lower bound holds but the exact cost is higher (4, 7, 10 for 3, 4, 5 keys against 3, 5, 7 with three-way comparisons: exhaustive, fact-check); algebraic computation trees (Ben-Or 1983: the model, arithmetic +, −, ×, ÷, √ and real constants plus sign tests, every operation and test counted as one step; Theorem 1, Ω(n log n) for n-element distinctness, from counting the n! connected components of the set of inputs with all keys distinct, one per order, "a maximal connected component of W for each permutation"; Theorem 11, the same in expectation for keys uniform in [0, 1]); linear decision trees (Dobkin and Lipton 1979, cited only as Ben-Or cites it: "This result extends the lower bounds of Dobkin and Lipton [5] for the linear decision tree model"); what this means: sort-then-scan is optimal up to lower-order terms in all three (Θ(n log n)); outside them, a hash set gives expected O(n), the expectation over a randomly chosen hash function (it computes a hash value, which none of the three models can: Ben-Or's has no floor or mod; and any fixed hash function has inputs that all collide: "for any fixed hash function, there is a subset of at least |U|/m items that all hash to the same location", Erickson's Lecture 5) (and with integer keys, any faster integer sort of topic 2 followed by a scan beats n log n deterministically). Lower bounds also travel by reduction (Ben-Or's discriminant example: any algorithm for it can test distinctness in one more step); reductions in general are a later topic.
  - `### What a lower bound does not rule out`: the list from step 13 with numbers (radix sort's 4 passes; hash set's expected O(n) and lost worst case; adaptive sorting on nearly sorted input, Ch.15; partial sorting in linear time, Northeastern's question; top-k and selection, Ch.16; better constants, merge sort's 2.5 %; the trivial Ω(n)); a problem's lower bound against one algorithm's cost (insertion sort's n(n − 1)/2 worst case, UMass); models this topic does not cover: the I/O model's sorting and permuting bounds (topic 8 states, Ch.31 proves) and streaming lower bounds (Ch.30).

## Examples to execute (`examples.py`) and what each asserts

Pure Python 3 standard library, run with `python3 -I`, deterministic (seeded where random), under about 30 s in total (the exhaustive searches are memoised over the set of inputs still consistent, with the ⌈log₂ |S|⌉ prune for the order problems). Every number the reel shows is produced or asserted here.

- **Claims of step 1:** `CLAIM_SORT = 15_000_000`, `CLAIM_MINMAX = 1_200_000`, `CLAIM_DISTINCT = 5_000_000`, `N = 1_000_000`, `W = 64`; asserts each claim is below its bound (15,000,000 < 18,488,885; 1,200,000 < 1,499,998; 5,000,000 < 18,488,885).
- **The 3-key decision tree (step 2):** run insertion sort on all 6 permutations of (a, b, c) with a comparator that records (pair, answer); assert 6 distinct transcripts (6 leaves), depth list [2, 3, 3, 2, 3, 3] in the order of step 2's leaves, max 3, sum 16; level capacities [1, 2, 4].
- **The adversary halving (step 3):** Northeastern's run: start from the 6 orders, compare a : b, keep the larger side (3), compare b : c, keep the larger side (2); assert sizes [6, 3, 2] and that a third comparison is needed (2 > 1); for every one of the possible first two comparison choices of any algorithm (exhaustive over pairs and branches), the larger-half adversary leaves ≥ 2 orders: no height-2 tree sorts 3 keys.
- **⌈log₂ n!⌉ (steps 4, 11, 12, 13, 14):** exact integer computation `(math.factorial(n) - 1).bit_length()`: 3 → 3, 4 → 5, 5 → 7, 8 → 16, 10 → 22, 12 → 29, 10⁶ → 18,488,885; ⌊log₂ 10⁶!⌋ = `math.factorial(10**6).bit_length() - 1` = 18,488,884; log₂ 10! = 21.79 (2 decimals); 5! = 120, 8! = 40,320; powers 1 … 128 and 64 < 120 ≤ 128; CMU bracket at 10⁶: round(n log₂ n − 1.443n) = 18,488,569 < 18,488,885 < round(n log₂ n) = 19,931,569; UMass bracket (n/2)(log₂ n − 1) ≤ log₂ n! ≤ n log₂ n for n = 2 … 1,000.
- **Exact minimum comparisons, exhaustive (steps 4, 6):** game search over consistent permutations, binary comparisons: sorting 3, 4, 5 keys needs exactly 3, 5, 7 = ⌈log₂ n!⌉.
- **Merge sort's worst-case comparisons (step 4, Deep dive):** C(n) = C(⌈n/2⌉) + C(⌊n/2⌋) + n − 1, C(1) = 0, equals n⌈log₂ n⌉ − 2^⌈log₂ n⌉ + 1 for n ≤ 10⁵ (and by formula 18,951,425 at 10⁶); ratio 18,951,425 / 18,488,885 = 1.025 (3 decimals).
