<section class="prereq basics" data-section="basics">

### <span class="n">00</span> Basics: the terms this reel uses

- **Percentile (p50, p99, p99.9)**: the value that a given share of the measurements stay at or under; p99 is the cost 99 of every 100 stay under. *Where you meet it:* "p99 is 120 ms" means 1 request in 100 is slower.
- **Tail latency**: the slow end of the latency distribution, which a service is judged on instead of the average. *Where you meet it:* a page that loads in 2 s for 1 user in 100.
- **Tick**: this reel's unit of time, the time of one element write. *Where you meet it:* a request arrives every 4 ticks.
- **Worst-case versus amortized bound**: a worst-case bound holds for every operation; an amortized one for a whole sequence. *Where you meet it:* append is O(1) amortized, yet one append copies half a million keys.
- **Deamortization**: turning an amortized bound into a worst-case one by doing the expensive work in small pieces over later operations. *Where you meet it:* a vector copy spread over later appends.
- **Incremental copying**: when the old buffer fills, allocate the new one, then let each operation move a fixed number of old keys. *Where you meet it:* a doubling vector that moves 2 old keys per append.
- **Frontier**: how many old keys have moved so far; a read compares its index with it to pick the buffer. *Where you meet it:* reading index 5 when only 2 keys have moved.
- **Global rebuilding**: let a structure go stale for Θ(n) updates, then rebuild all of it; Overmars's version rebuilds in the background. *Where you meet it:* a sorted vector rebuilt once half its slots are tombstones (marks left by deletes).
- **Partial rebuilding**: rebuild only the part that went bad. *Where you meet it:* one subtree of a scapegoat tree.
- **Scapegoat tree**: a plain binary search tree with no balance data in its nodes; only the root keeps the size and the largest size. *Where you meet it:* a too deep insert triggers a rebuild of one ancestor.
- **alpha-weight-balanced, h(n)**: a node is balanced if neither child holds more than alpha of its subtree; h(n) = ⌊log base 1/alpha of n⌋ is the depth limit. *Where you meet it:* alpha = 2/3 and n = 9 give h = 5.

### <span class="n">01</span> Aggregate and accounting methods

You know that an amortized bound is a worst-case bound on the total of a whole sequence divided by its length, never an average over random inputs. You have seen the doubling vector, the charge of 3 per append (1 to write, 2 saved for later copies), and the cost strip with its spikes.

<p class="check"><b>Check yourself:</b> how many of a million appends to the doubling vector copy anything? (Answer: 20, plus the first append, which only allocates.)</p>

### <span class="n">02</span> The potential method

You know the gauge Φ = 2n − cap that fills on cheap appends and empties on a resize, and the rule that shrinking at a quarter, not at a half, avoids thrashing.

<p class="check"><b>Check yourself:</b> what is Φ = 2n − cap just before append 9? (Answer: 8, exactly the keys append 9 copies.)</p>

### <span class="n">03</span> Binary search trees: search and insert

You know that a binary search tree keeps smaller keys to the left and larger to the right, that a search follows one path from the root, and that an insert ends as a new leaf.

<p class="check"><b>Check yourself:</b> insert 5, 3, 8, 4 into an empty tree. What is the depth of 4? (Answer: 2, under 3.)</p>

<section class="prereq" data-section="percentiles">

### <span class="n">04</span> Percentiles

You can read a percentile off a sorted list: p50 is the middle value, p90 the value 9 tenths of the way up, p99 the value only 1 in 100 exceeds. This reel counts a percentile by nearest rank: with N values, the p-th percentile is the one at rank ⌈p × N / 100⌉ in sorted order.

<p class="check"><b>Check yourself:</b> ten requests take 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1 and 50 ms. What are p50, p90 and p99? (Answer: 1, 1 and 50 ms.)</p>

</section>

<section class="prereq" data-section="logarithms">

### <span class="n">05</span> Logarithms

You can read a logarithm in any base as the number of times a size is divided by that base to reach 1. The scapegoat tree's depth limit uses base 3/2 for alpha = 2/3.

<p class="check"><b>Check yourself:</b> about what is log base 1.5 of 9? (Answer: about 5.4, since 1.5⁵ is about 7.6 and 1.5⁶ about 11.4.)</p>

</section>
