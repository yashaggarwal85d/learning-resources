## The chapter

### The problem it solves

A senior engineer says "append is O(1)", "this sort is n log n", "that index is log n", and is right on paper and wrong in production: the append that copies the whole array stalls a request, the "n log n" sort slows down sharply once the data leaves the cache, the "log n" lookup in a million sorted keys can be close to twenty cache misses. (No slowdown factor is planned here: topic 10 measures one.) Each claim was made in a cost model without naming it. This chapter makes the model explicit. It gives the learner the language of bounds (1), the machine the counts assume and what it hides (2), the tools that turn a recursive algorithm into a bound (3) and prove no algorithm can beat one (4), the analysis of a *sequence* of operations instead of one (5–7), and the models in which only block transfers count (8–9), and then measures the real machine those models stand for (10). The learner leaves able to say of any cost claim: in which model, which case, per operation or amortized, and what it will cost on real hardware. Every later chapter argues speed with these tools: amortized bounds for hash resizing (Ch.3), splay trees (Ch.4), Fibonacci heaps (Ch.6) and union–find (Ch.11); the I/O and cache-oblivious models for B-trees (Ch.5) and Ch.31; the lower bound for sorting (Ch.15).

### Order of ideas, and why

1. **§1.1 The language and the machine (1–4).** Notation and cases (1, the refresher) → the word-RAM, the machine those step counts assume, and what it hides (2) → recurrences, the step count of a recursive algorithm (3) → lower bounds, what *every* algorithm in a model must pay (4). The model (2) comes before lower bounds (4) because a lower bound is a statement about a model: topic 4's last clause ("what a lower bound does and does not rule out") needs topic 2's integer-key structures that escape the comparison model. Recurrences (3) come before lower bounds so that 4 can contrast an algorithm's upper bound (solved in 3) with the problem's lower bound.
2. **§1.2 Costs over a sequence (5–7).** Aggregate and accounting (5) → the potential method (6), which generalises accounting and is needed the moment the table can also shrink → deamortization (7), which turns the amortized bound back into a worst-case one, because a service is judged on its tail. One running example (the dynamic array) carries all three, so each method is seen proving the same bound a new way.
3. **§1.3 Change the unit of cost (8–10).** Topic 2 names what the word-RAM hides; §1.3 pays that off. Block transfers between two levels with known M and B (8) → algorithms that never learn M and B and are still optimal at every level (9) → measuring the real B and M, the latency staircase, and the chapter lab, which closes the loop on 5 and 6 by measuring the growth and shrink policies they proved (10). The lab sits in the last topic because it needs every model of the chapter.
4. **Handoff to Chapter 2.** Topic 10 has proved and measured the doubling array; Chapter 2 opens with "Dynamic arrays and growth policy", the container itself.

A topic may scale a number up or down if it says how it relates to these. | Growth rule | An empty vector has capacity 0. The first append allocates capacity 1. | Cost unit for §1.2 | **one element write**: an append without a resize costs 1; an append that resizes a vector holding n elements costs n + 1 (n copies plus the write). Allocation and freeing are not counted in 5–6; 7 and 10 say what that hides. | 5, 6, 7 |
| The small trace | 16 appends with g = 2 from empty: per-append costs 1, 2, 3, 1, 5, 1, 1, 1, 9, then seven 1s; **total 31**, against the bound 3n = 48. (A 16-increment binary counter from 0 also flips 31 bits; topic 5 may use the coincidence or not.) | 5, 6 |

Strict push/pop alternation is *not* the bad sequence: under shrink-at-½ (strictly less than half) it never shrinks, so topic 6 must not call it that. Under shrink-at-½ every second operation of the repeat resizes and copies about C elements; under shrink-at-¼ nothing resizes after the first grow. The total is Θ(n²) for n operations only when C grows with n (CLRS's form: about n/2 pushes to fill, then the thrash); with C fixed it is Θ(C) per operation. | 6, 10 |
| The large vector | **n = 1,000,000** appends for counts at scale (with g = 2, the copies total 2²⁰ − 1 = 1,048,575 over 20 resizes, the last copying 524,288; plus the first append, which only allocates) | 5, 7, 10 |
| Out of memory | The vector grown to **N = 2²⁷ keys (1 GiB)**; disk blocks of 4 KiB, so **B = 512 keys**; memory of 64 MiB, so **M = 2²³ keys**; then N/B = 262,144 and M/B = 16,384. | 8, 9 |
| At the cache level | B = 8 keys for a 64-byte cache line (the line size of the measured machine is measured in 10, not assumed: to verify on it) | 8, 9, 10 |

### Shared notation and terms

- **Logarithms.** log₂ on screen wherever the base changes a number; plain log n inside O(·), Θ(·), Ω(·), where the base doesn't matter; ln for natural logs. log_{M/B} is written with its base. (Topic 1 uses the same convention.)
- **Bounds and cases** exactly as topic 1 defines them: O, Ω, Θ, o, ω; best, worst, average (over inputs), expected (over the algorithm's coins), with high probability. **Amortized** is another kind of promise, a worst-case bound on a whole sequence divided by its length (topic 1 calls it "a third kind of promise" beside average-case and expected bounds, says "Amortized is neither" and points here; topics 5–7 keep that wording and don't renumber it); topics 5–7 never call it an average.
- **Word-RAM** (topic 2): word size **w** bits, with w ≥ log₂ n; universe **U = 2ʷ**.
- **Recurrences** (topic 3): T(n) = a·T(n/b) + f(n), with **a ≥ 1, b > 1**, and the watershed function **n^(log_b a)**. Akra–Bazzi: T(n) = f(n) + Σ aᵢ·T(bᵢn + hᵢ(n)), exponent **p** with Σ aᵢ·bᵢᵖ = 1. The driving function is **f** in both theorems, never g (Leighton and Akra–Bazzi write g; topic 3 says so once), because **g** is the growth factor in 5–7 and 10.
- **The letter b:** the bit length of a number in topics 1–2 (topic 1's letter, as in Θ(b)); the Master theorem's split factor in topic 3; bᵢ the Akra–Bazzi split fractions. **B** (capital) is only the block size. A reel that needs two of these says which.
- **Amortized analysis** (topics 5–7), CLRS's letters: **cᵢ** the actual cost of operation i, **ĉᵢ** its amortized cost, **Φ(Dᵢ)** the potential after it, ĉᵢ = cᵢ + Φ(Dᵢ) − Φ(Dᵢ₋₁), with Φ(D₀) = 0 and Φ ≥ 0. A dynamic array holds **n** elements in capacity **cap**; its **load factor α = n / cap** (topic 6). The inverse Ackermann function, named in topic 6 as union–find's bound, is always written **α(n)** with its argument and in words, so it is never read as a load factor. **g** is the growth factor.
- **Memory-hierarchy models** (topics 8–10): **N** items in the problem, **M** items that fit in the faster level, **B** items per block, all counted in **items, not bytes** (as Aggarwal and Vitter and the Data Engineering I/O topic do). Vitter writes lower-case n = N/B and m = M/B; this chapter does not, because n is already the number of elements: write N/B and M/B in full. Frigo et al. write the cache size Z and the line length L; topic 9 says so once and uses M and B. Aggarwal and Vitter's P (blocks moved in parallel) is set to 1 and named once by topic 8. **scan(N) = Θ(N/B)** and **sort(N) = Θ((N/B)·log_{M/B}(N/B))** are written exactly so in 8, 9 and every later chapter (Ch.15 writes the pass count log_{M/B}(N/M), which is consistent).
- **Words:** "block" is the model's unit of transfer; "cache line" is the block at the CPU-cache level (topic 8 introduces it); "page" is the virtual-memory page (topic 10, for the TLB). Each topic says which one it means.
- **Latency percentiles:** p50, p99, p99.9, introduced by topic 7, used by 10.
- **Sizes:** KiB, MiB, GiB for memory and files; ns for measured latency; thousands separators (16,384); no scientific notation on screen except where topic 1 already set the pattern (2⁶⁴, 10⁶ written as powers).
- **What each topic counts** (each reel names its unit on the first step that counts anything):

  | Topics | Model | One unit of cost |
  |---|---|---|
  | 1 | any | a step, whatever the claim counts |
  | 2–4 | word-RAM; comparison model | one word operation; one comparison |
  | 5–7 | word-RAM, over a sequence | one element write |
  | 8 | external memory (I/O) | one block transfer of B items |
  | 9 | ideal cache | one block (line) transfer, with B and M unknown to the algorithm |
  | 10 | the real machine | nanoseconds per load, measured |

- **Spoken numbers:** narration has no digits, symbols or code names (automatic check). Say "big O of n log n", "theta of n", "phi", "two to the sixty-four", "M over B", and add new terms (such as Akra–Bazzi, Aggarwal, Vitter, Frigo, Prokop, Ramachandran, van Emde Boas, Sattolo, Overmars, Pohl, Ben-Or, Brodal, Fagerberg, Ackermann) to the pronunciation lexicon the first time they appear (QUALITY.md #29).

### 2. The word-RAM model and its assumptions (`dsa.word-ram-model-assumptions`): full, §1.1

- **Folder:** `word-ram-model-assumptions`. About 12 min.
- **Scope (syllabus clause → what it delivers):**
  - "unit-cost operations on w ≥ log n-bit words": what the machine is (memory of w-bit words addressed by integers; unit-cost +, −, ×, integer division, comparisons, bitwise operations and shifts, load and store at a computed address); why **w ≥ log₂ n** (an index or a pointer to any of the n inputs must fit in one word: the trans-dichotomous assumption, Fredman and Willard; which of their papers coins the term, to verify); why w can't be unbounded either (a model with arbitrarily large unit-cost words could pack a whole input into one word and "compute" too much in one step; CLRS's c·log n word-size convention, to verify against CLRS 4th ed. §2.2). Which operations count as unit cost is itself a modelling choice: the standard word-RAM includes multiplication, the AC⁰ RAM leaves it out (Deep dive; to verify against Hagerup 1998, and that the original fusion trees use multiplication). What is *not* unit cost: on numbers of b ≫ w bits, addition and comparison cost Θ(b/w) word operations and schoolbook multiplication Θ((b/w)²) (picks up topic 1's Θ(b) note and its trial-division example). w = 64 on the running example.
  - "what that hides (cache misses, branch mispredicts)": the same `a[i]` load is one unit in the model and anywhere from a cache hit to a trip to main memory on a real core; a mispredicted branch throws away the work a pipelined core did ahead of it. **Introduces both on the spot** in one or two sentences each, with orders of magnitude only from a cited source (the exact cycle counts vary by microarchitecture: to verify, and show as "tens" or "hundreds" of cycles if the source supports no single number). Points forward: topics 8–9 model the hierarchy, 10 measures it; branchless search is Ch.16, branchless partitioning Ch.15. Also names what else the model leaves out in one Deep dive list (SIMD, parallelism, allocation).
  - "and why integer-key structures (radix sort, van Emde Boas trees, fusion trees) beat comparison-model bounds": the **comparison model** (keys are only compared, so an algorithm learns at most one bit per yes/no comparison) against the word-RAM (keys are numbers: they can index arrays and be cut into bit fields). Three escapes, each one frame:
    - **Radix sort:** splitting each key into digits of r = log₂ n bits gives O(n·w / log n) time, linear when w = O(log n) (CLRS Lemma 8.4 form, to verify). Mechanism and engineering left to Ch.15 "Counting, radix and bucket sort".
    - **van Emde Boas trees:** predecessor search in O(log w) = O(log log U) by recursing on the high and low halves of the key's bits: 64 → 32 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1, six halvings for 64-bit keys against log₂ n comparisons; the price is Θ(U) space in the plain form (hashing brings it down: named, not taught). vEB trees have no other home in the syllabus, so this topic carries the mechanism (cluster and summary) at the level of the halving argument, and the Deep dive gives the structure in full.
    - **Fusion trees:** O(log n / log w) by compressing ("sketching") the keys of a wide node into one word and comparing a query against all of them at once with word-level parallelism. Taught as the idea only (sketch + parallel compare); the full construction is out of scope and the Deep dive says so. No claim that either structure is fast in practice unless a source says it.
  - The comparison-model **lower bounds** these structures escape (Ω(n log n) sorting, Ω(log n) search) are stated as "proved in topic 4", not proved here.
- **Outcomes:**
  - Say which operations the word-RAM charges one unit for, and why the word size is tied to log n.
  - Name two real costs the model hides and say which later models expose them.
  - Explain how integer keys beat the comparison model: digits as array indices (radix sort), halving the key's bits (vEB), many comparisons in one word (fusion trees).
  - Say for a given cost claim whether it was made in the comparison model or the word-RAM.
- **Introduces:** `word-ram`, `word-size-assumption`, `comparison-model`, `cache-miss`, `branch-misprediction`, `word-level-parallelism`, `integer-sorting`, `van-emde-boas-tree`, `fusion-tree`. **Uses:** `big-o-theta-omega`, `pseudo-polynomial`, `time-space-tradeoff`, `logarithms`, `standard-sorts`, `binary-numbers`, `binary-search`. **Declared:** `logarithms`, `standard-sorts`, `binary-numbers`, `binary-search`.
- **Bridge:** "Every count in the last reel assumed each step costs the same. Here is the machine that makes that true, one word at a time, and the costs it leaves out."
- **Handoff:** "Counting a loop on this machine is easy. A recursive algorithm hands you its count as a recurrence; next, how to solve one."
- **Prerequisites panel:** topic 1 (Before this); binary numbers, bits and bitwise operations (declared, with a self-check: what is 13 >> 2, and 13 & 6?); binary search (declared: halve a sorted range). On-site: Data Engineering "CPU caches, SIMD and vectorization" as another track for the hidden costs.

### 4. Lower bounds and adversary arguments (`dsa.lower-bounds-adversary-arguments`): full, §1.1

- **Folder:** `lower-bounds-adversary-arguments`. About 13 min.

  - "decision-tree Ω(n log n) for comparison sorting": the decision tree of any comparison sort (3 keys: 6 leaves, so some input needs at least 3 comparisons); n! leaves force height ≥ log₂ n! = Θ(n log n); the same counting bounds the *average* over input orders (average leaf depth ≥ log₂ of the number of leaves). Ch.15's elementary sorts topic quotes "the Ω(n log n) bound from Ch.1": this is it.
  - "element distinctness": Ω(n log n) comparisons to decide whether the vector's keys are all distinct, in the comparison model (an adversary argument: an algorithm that answers "distinct" without having compared some two keys adjacent in sorted order can be fooled by making those two equal, so on an all-distinct input it must have learned the whole sorted order; each "distinct" leaf then fits only one of the n! orders, and the sorting bound applies), and in algebraic computation trees (Ben-Or 1983; earlier for linear decision trees, Dobkin and Lipton 1979; stated, not proved; to verify both exact statements). Exact form and attribution (Pohl 1972) to verify. n = 8: 10 comparisons against 2n − 3 = 13 for two separate scans.

### 9. Cache-oblivious model (`dsa.cache-oblivious-model`): full, §1.3

- **Folder:** `cache-oblivious-model`. About 12 min.
- **Scope (syllabus clause → what it delivers):**
  - "Frigo, Leiserson, Prokop & Ramachandran (FOCS 1999)": the model: the I/O model's two levels, but the algorithm is written without M and B, and analysed for all of them; the paper's letters (Z, L) translated once into M, B.
  - "algorithms optimal for every unknown B and M": a scan is cache-oblivious and costs N/B + O(1) for every B; an algorithm optimal for every B and M between two levels is optimal between every pair of levels of a multilevel hierarchy at once, given each level behaves like an ideal cache (the reason the model matters; the exact conditions, such as inclusion, as Frigo et al. state them, to verify); the counter-example: binary search on the sorted vector touches about log₂(N/B) blocks, not log_B N, and the fixes (van Emde Boas layout, cache-oblivious B-trees) are named and left to Ch.31 "The van Emde Boas layout". The reel says once that the vEB *layout* is a memory order for a static tree that shares only the name and the √-split recursion with topic 2's vEB *tree*, so the learner doesn't confuse them. The analysis pattern: recurse until a subproblem fits in a block or in the cache, then count (uses topic 3's recursion tree).
  - "under the tall-cache assumption": M = Ω(B²); what it buys (cache-oblivious sorting and transposition meet their bounds; lazy funnelsort needs only M = Ω(B^(1+ε))) and that some tall-cache assumption can't be dropped for optimal comparison-based cache-oblivious sorting (Brodal and Fagerberg 2003; to verify the exact statement, which is about M = Ω(B^(1+ε)), not B² specifically).
  - "ideal-cache and LRU simulation arguments": the ideal cache is fully associative with optimal offline replacement; it is justified because LRU with twice the cache does at most a constant factor more transfers (Sleator and Tarjan's competitive bound; exact form to verify) and the bounds are "regular" (doubling M changes them by a constant factor); fully associative caches simulated by real ones (to verify what Frigo et al. claim here).
  - "the algorithms themselves are in Ch.31": vEB layout, recursive matrix transpose and multiplication, funnelsort are named in one frame with "Goes deeper later"; this topic teaches the model, not the algorithms.
- **Outcomes:**
  - State the ideal-cache model and what "cache-oblivious" promises.
  - Explain why optimal for every M and B means optimal at every level at once.
  - Say what the tall-cache assumption and the LRU simulation argument each justify.
  - Show why binary search on a sorted array is not cache-oblivious optimal, and name the layout that is.
- **Introduces:** `cache-oblivious-model`, `ideal-cache-model`, `tall-cache-assumption`, `lru-simulation`, `multilevel-optimality`. **Uses:** `io-model`, `memory-hierarchy`, `cache-lines`, `cache-miss`, `scan-cost`, `io-search-bound`, `io-sort-bound`, `recursion-tree`, `recurrence-relations`, `big-o-theta-omega`, `binary-search`, `logarithms`. **Declared:** `binary-search`, `logarithms`.
- **Bridge:** "An I/O-model algorithm is tuned to one M and one B. A cache-oblivious algorithm never sees them, and can still be optimal at every level at once."
- **Handoff:** "Both models take B and M as given. Next: measure them on your own machine, and see where the staircase of latencies comes from."
- **Prerequisites panel:** topics 2, 3 and 8 (Before this; 2 for the cache miss); binary search (declared); Ch.31 "The van Emde Boas layout", "Cache-oblivious matrix algorithms" and "Funnelsort" as "Goes deeper later".
- **Visual idea:** one recursion drawn once, with a slider that changes the block size B over it: the block grid redraws at each B while the recursion boxes stay put, and the level where a box first fits in one block lights `caramel`, which is where the count stops, for every B. Then binary search on the sorted vector touching a `cherry` block on almost every probe until the range fits in one block.

### 10. Measuring the memory hierarchy (`dsa.measuring-memory-hierarchy`): full, §1.3, carries the chapter Lab

- **Folder:** `measuring-memory-hierarchy`. About 15 min. Last topic of the chapter.
- **Scope (syllabus clause → what it delivers):**
  - "pointer-chasing … microbenchmarks": a vector of indices holding one random cycle (Sattolo's algorithm), so every load depends on the last and nothing can be overlapped or prefetched; ns per load as the working set grows. Why each element of the method matters (dependence, a single cycle, enough iterations, warm-up, fixed frequency).
  - "and strided-access microbenchmarks": touching every k-th byte of a working set larger than the cache; the cost per access rises until the stride reaches the line size, then stays flat: that is the cache-line size (Saavedra and Smith's method). Caveat to show, not hide: on current cores the stride and adjacent-line prefetchers can flatten or shift this knee (an adjacent-line prefetcher can make lines look 128 bytes), so the benchmark either visits the strided addresses in a random order or reports the knee with that caveat; which prefetchers the recorded CPU has is cited from its vendor's documentation (to verify). With page-sized strides over many pages, the TLB shows up.
  - "that expose cache-line size, TLB reach and prefetcher behaviour": cache line from the stride sweep; **TLB** introduced on the spot (the cache of virtual-to-physical page translations) and TLB reach = entries × page size, with what huge pages change (to verify on the measured machine); prefetchers: the same pointer chase in sequential order against random order, and a stride the prefetcher learns against one it can't.
  - "reading the latency staircase": plateaus for each cache level and DRAM, knees at the capacities, the TLB step, and why the knees are soft (associativity, shared caches, other processes); mapping the staircase back to M and B of topics 8–9 at each level.
  - "→ Rust Systems Engineering Ch.29": the Prerequisites panel links Rust Ch.29 "Benchmarking discipline and statistics" and "Cache lines and false sharing" as another track; the Deep dive points there for benchmarking statistics, profilers and NUMA instead of teaching them.
  - The **chapter Lab** (see "The chapter lab: who hosts what"), all four clauses, in the Deep dive "Build, break, measure".
  - **On-screen numbers:** a recorded run of the benchmarks (machine, CPU model, compiler and flags, date stated) is saved as a data file in the topic folder; `examples.py` asserts every value the reel shows from that file and computes the predictions from 5 and 6. The reel says the shape transfers to other machines, the numbers don't. Any vendor figure (cache sizes, line size, TLB entries) for the recorded CPU is cited from the vendor's documentation.
- **Outcomes:**
  - Build a pointer-chasing benchmark that really measures latency, and read cache sizes and DRAM latency off its staircase.
  - Find the cache-line size and the TLB reach with strided access.
  - Tell prefetching apart from a cache hit in a measurement.
  - Reconcile a measured growth or shrink policy with its amortized proof, and explain where time and the count of copies disagree.
- **Introduces:** `pointer-chasing-benchmark`, `strided-access-benchmark`, `latency-staircase`, `tlb`, `tlb-reach`, `hardware-prefetching`. **Uses:** `memory-hierarchy`, `cache-lines`, `cache-miss`, `io-model`, `scan-cost`, `multilevel-optimality`, `amortized-cost`, `dynamic-array-doubling`, `growth-factor`, `load-factor`, `table-expand-contract`, `resize-hysteresis`, `potential-method`, `latency-percentiles`, `reading-c`, `virtual-memory`. **Declared:** `reading-c`, `virtual-memory`.
