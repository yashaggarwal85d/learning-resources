Allocation and freeing are not counted.
- **Shrink rules, made exact for this topic** (CHAPTER.md's words, plus the two edge cases the chapter leaves open, stated once in the Deep dive and coded in `examples.py`): **shrink-at-½**: after a pop, if n < cap/2 (strictly), set cap to ⌊cap/2⌋ and copy the n keys; **shrink-at-¼**: the same with n < cap/4. Capacities are always 0 or a power of two, so halving is exact except 1 → 0 (popping the last key of a capacity-1 vector frees it). A pop on an empty vector is not an operation. Only one halving per pop.
- **The adversarial push/pop sequence** (defined here for the chapter lab, CHAPTER.md's table): fill the vector from empty until n = cap = C, C a power of two (C = 16 in the reel), push once, then repeat (pop, pop, push, push). Each repeat is called a **round**.

## Learning outcomes

As CHAPTER.md lists them, made concrete:

1. **Turn an accounting argument into a potential function and back:** read Φ as the bank of the last reel summed over the whole state (counter: Φ = number of 1 bits; stack: Φ = number of keys; doubling vector: Φ = 2n − cap, the bank 2n − cap + 1 without its spare coin); compute ĉᵢ = cᵢ + Φ(Dᵢ) − Φ(Dᵢ₋₁); explain why Φ(D₀) = 0 and Φ ≥ 0 make Σĉᵢ an upper bound on Σcᵢ after every operation (the sum telescopes); and go back: a potential is credit placed on the whole structure.
2. **Prove amortized O(1) push and pop for a table that grows and shrinks:** with load factor α = n/cap, the two-piece potential Φ = 2n − cap when α ≥ ½ and Φ = cap/2 − n when α < ½ (that is, Φ = max(2n − cap, cap/2 − n)); the six cases; every push costs at most 3 amortized and every pop at most 2 under shrink-at-¼, so any m operations from empty cost at most 3·pushes + 2·pops ≤ 3m.
3. **Show why shrink-at-½ costs Θ(n²) on an adversarial sequence, and choose thresholds that can't thrash:** on the adversarial sequence every round of four operations costs 2C + 3 writes (35 at C = 16), so the cost per operation grows with C; Θ(n²) for n operations when C is about n/2 (fill first), Θ(C) per operation when C is fixed; strict push/pop alternation does not thrash under the strict "less than half" rule and does under "at most half"; **resize hysteresis**: after any resize the vector must land Ω(cap) operations away from both thresholds, which halving at any fixed fraction a < ½ gives and a = ½ does not.
4. **Recognise the potential behind splay trees, Fibonacci heaps and union–find:** splay trees, the sum over nodes of log₂ of the subtree size (Sleator and Tarjan); Fibonacci heaps, trees in the root list plus twice the marked nodes; union–find, the O(α(n)) amortized bound (α(n) the inverse Ackermann function), given its first precise analysis by Tarjan in 1975 (a proof Tarjan 1985 describes as using credits and debits), now also proved with potential functions (Lambert et al. 2023, e.g. the sum of √size for O(log log n)).

## The problem (opened in step 1, closed in step 16)

"The vector now gives memory back: a pop that leaves it less than half full halves its capacity. Full at 16 keys, push once, then repeat pop, pop, push, push. Is a push or pop still O(1) amortized?" It is not: every round costs 35 writes, and the cost per operation grows with the vector (8.75 at C = 16, 524,288.75 at C = 1,048,576). Closed in step 16: under shrink-at-¼ the same 49 operations cost 80 writes instead of 328 (31 copies instead of 279), the potential proves at most 3 per push and 2 per pop for every sequence (bound 131 here), and Σĉ = 82 = 80 + the final Φ of 2. The single push that copied 16 keys is still one operation (the next topic's problem).

  - `### Choosing Φ, and what a bad one looks like`: Erickson's rule of thumb (goes up a little on cheap operations, down a lot on expensive ones; "no general technique ... except to play around"); Φ as "how far the structure is from just after its last expensive operation" (CHAPTER.md's intuition, no opened source: read it as distance in *state* from where an expensive operation leaves the structure, not as the number of operations since one: a push then a pop after a grow puts Φ back at 2); different Φ give different bounds (Erickson); the four bad shapes, each executed: **doesn't drop on the expensive step** (Φ = n: ĉ = 2ᵏ + 2 for the append that copies 2ᵏ; Φ = empty slots: ĉ₉ = 16; Φ = cap: ĉ₉ = 17), **drops too little** (2n − cap for g = 1.5), **goes negative** (2n − cap with pops: Φ = −2 after two pops from 17 of 32, Σĉ = 48 < Σc = 50; −16 at 8 keys; then even the shrinking pop gets ĉ = 22), **doesn't start at zero** without a seed.   - `### A table that grows and shrinks`: the two-piece potential Φ = max(2n − cap, cap/2 − n) (equal to 2n − cap when α ≥ ½ and cap/2 − n when α < ½); why the slopes are 2 and 1 (the cap/2 pushes from half to full must save cap copies; the cap/4 pops from half to a quarter must save about cap/4); the six cases with their algebra (push α ≥ ½: +2 → 3; grow from n = cap: Φ n → 2, ĉ = n + 1 + 2 − n = 3; push α < ½ (including the one that reaches exactly ½): −1 → 0; pop staying ≥ ½: −2 → −1; pop ending below ½: +1 → 2; shrink at n = cap/4 → cap/4 − 1: cost cap/4, Φ cap/4 → 1, ĉ = 1); the edge cases at capacities 0, 1 and 2 (first push from cap 0: ĉ = 2; push into cap 1 with no keys, Φ ½ → 1: ĉ = 3/2; pop that shrinks 2 → 1: ĉ = 3/2; pop that frees cap 1 → 0: ĉ = 0); conclusion Σc ≤ Σĉ ≤ 3·pushes + 2·pops ≤ 3m for every sequence from empty; checked exhaustively over every state reachable from empty up to capacity 4,096 (6,158 states) and on 2,000 seeded random sequences of up to 3,000 operations. Erickson's exercise 9 (grow when more than ¾ full, shrink when less than ¼ full, both by rebuilding) as the same idea with other thresholds; its hint says to do it without potentials (More questions). CLRS treats dynamic tables in its amortized-analysis chapter (framing only: the book was not opened, and no formula or section number is taken from it).
  - `### Thrashing and hysteresis`: the adversarial sequence defined exactly (for the chapter lab): fill to n = cap = C, push once, then rounds of (pop, pop, push, push). Under shrink-at-½, every round costs 2C + 3 writes, 2C − 1 of them copies (C − 1 on the shrink, C on the grow); under shrink-at-¼ every round costs 4 and copies nothing; the fill and the push cost 3C writes and 2C − 1 copies under both. The honest scaling (CHAPTER.md's caveat): **with C fixed** the thrash is Θ(C) per operation, any number of operations; **with C proportional to the number of operations** (C pushes to fill, one push, C/4 rounds: m = 2C + 1 operations) the totals are exactly C²/2 + 15C/4 under ½ (528,128 at C = 1,024; 549,759,746,048 at C = 1,048,576), about m²/8, which is Θ(n²), against exactly 4C under ¼ (4,096; 4,194,304). Strict alternation: under "less than half" it costs 1 per operation after the first grow (the pop leaves exactly half); under "at most half" every operation resizes (17 writes each at C = 16). Why no potential can rescue ½: the actual total of the rounds is Θ(C) per operation, so any valid Φ must give some operation a Θ(C) amortized cost; with the two-piece Φ it is the shrinking pop, ĉ = cap − 2 for a shrink from capacity cap (30 at cap 32). **Hysteresis in general:** with doubling when full and halving when a pop leaves n < a·cap, the vector lands at about 2a·cap′ after a shrink and just over cap/2 after a grow; for any fixed 0 < a < ½ both landings are Ω(cap) operations from both thresholds. A potential that proves it: Φ_a = max(2n − cap, k·(cap/2 − n)) with k = max(1, (3a − ½)/(½ − a)); push ≤ 3 and pop ≤ 1 + k amortized (k = 1 for a ≤ ¼; 3 for a = ⅓; 5 for a = ⅜; 13 for a = 7/16; executed by exhaustive search to capacity 4,096); k grows without bound as a → ½, and a = ½ fails. The trade-off: a smaller a wastes more memory (a vector may sit at load factor just above a) for a smaller constant.
  - `### The tool behind splay trees, Fibonacci heaps and union–find`: **splay trees** (Ch.4 "Splay trees", Goes deeper later): Sleator and Tarjan assign each item a fixed positive weight w(i), the size s(x) of a node is the total weight in its subtree, its rank r(x) = log s(x) (binary logarithms), the potential is the sum of all ranks; the access lemma: splaying at x in a tree with root t costs at most 3(r(t) − r(x)) + 1 = O(log(s(t)/s(x))) amortized, proved per rotation step and telescoped; with unit weights this is Tarjan 1985's version (sum over nodes of log of the number of descendants, amortized access O(log n)); other weight choices give the balance and static-optimality theorems. **Fibonacci heaps** (Ch.6, Goes deeper later): Φ(H) = trees(H) + 2·marks(H), the potential Wayne's Kleinberg–Tardos slides use to prove the theorem they credit to Fredman and Tarjan (dated 1986 on the slide; the 1987 J. ACM paper was not opened, so the Φ is not quoted as theirs); insert ΔΦ = +1; meld ΔΦ = 0; decrease-key with c cuts ΔΦ ≤ 4 − c, O(1) amortized; extract-min O(log n) amortized because ΔΦ ≤ rank(H′) + 1 − trees(H). **Union–find** (Ch.11, Goes deeper later): Galler and Fischer (1964) described the structure (Tarjan 1985: Galler and Fischer proposed union by size, McIlroy and Morris path compression); Tarjan (1975) gave the first precise analysis of union by size with path compression (Tarjan 1985's description; the Waterloo notes state the same theorem for union by rank), O(m·α(m, n)) for m operations on n elements (Tarjan 1985 adds: assuming m = Ω(n)), with α the inverse Ackermann function (the Waterloo notes' statement; at most 4 for most practical cases); Tarjan 1985 calls his proof "a complicated amortized analysis that uses debits as well as credits" (the banker's view, not a potential); Lambert, Huang, Nie and Peng (2023) prove the O(log log n), O(log* n), O(log** n) and O(α(n)) amortized bounds with potential functions, e.g. each node's potential the square root of its subtree size for O(log log n), Size/(3 + log Size)² for O(log* n), and, for O(α(n)) (their Section 6), a potential built on a rank r(p) = ⌊log Sizemax(p)⌋ and summed over Ackermann levels k ≤ α(n), which uses the classical recursive Ackermann definitions; their "no explicit definitions of the Ackermann function" describes the size-based route through log** n (Section 5, via the iterated-log characterization), and beyond log* n their potentials involve a node and its parent, not Size alone; they say only the log log n and log* n proofs are significantly simpler. They analyse the union-by-size version. The inverse Ackermann function is written α(n) or α(m, n), always with its arguments, never the load factor. **Move-to-front** (Tarjan 1985): a potential can also compare two algorithms (inversions between MTF's list and another algorithm's), the start of competitive analysis (the cache-oblivious model topic uses Sleator and Tarjan's paging result).
