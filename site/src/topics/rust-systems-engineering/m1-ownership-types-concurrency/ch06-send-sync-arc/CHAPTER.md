## A.1 The problem it solves

Chapter 4 built `Arc` and `Mutex` and used them freely across threads, but it never once asked *how the compiler knows* a given type is safe to move to another thread, or safe to touch through a shared reference from several threads at once. That knowledge isn't inferred from behaviour; it is tracked as two ordinary (if special-cased) traits, `Send` and `Sync`, computed structurally from a type's fields and propagated automatically — which is exactly why `Rc`'s single, non-atomic refcount makes it neither, while `Cell`'s interior mutability makes it `Send` but never `Sync`. The chapter's second half returns to `Arc<Mutex<T>>` with a sharper question than Chapter 4 asked: `Arc` and `Mutex` are not one tool but two, doing two unrelated jobs (sharing ownership; synchronizing access), and conflating them is how a critical section quietly starts protecting an I/O call instead of a few memory accesses. The chapter closes on the only four places ownership is actually allowed to cross a thread boundary at all, because a program that doesn't choose them deliberately ends up choosing them by accident.

By the end of the chapter the learner can: state what `Send` and `Sync` each mean in one sentence, name a type that is one but not the other from real `std` types (not invented ones), and explain why `unsafe impl Send` is a real proof obligation rather than a formality; distinguish `Arc`'s one job (shared ownership) from `Mutex`/`RwLock`'s separate job (synchronized access), and know when `parking_lot` or `tokio::sync::Mutex` actually changes that picture; recognize a critical section that has started serializing external latency instead of protecting memory, and name lock ordering as deadlock's fix and lock convoys/priority inversion as the failure modes that remain; and list the four and only four places ownership actually changes threads in real code.

## A.2 The order of ideas, and why

1. **Send and Sync as auto-traits** (topic 18) — the foundation the rest of the chapter assumes: what the two traits each mean, how they're derived automatically from a type's fields (an auto-trait, not something implemented by hand in the ordinary case), the four concrete asymmetric examples the syllabus itself names (`Cell`/`RefCell` Send-not-Sync, `MutexGuard` not Send, `Rc` neither), `PhantomData`'s role in opting a generic type out, `unsafe impl Send` as a real, checkable proof obligation, and why a future is `Send` only if everything it holds across an `.await` is.
2. **Arc shares, Mutex synchronizes** (topic 19) — needs (1): now that "safe to share" and "safe to touch concurrently" are two separately-tracked properties, this topic separates the two *tools* that provide them: `Arc` only ever shares ownership (it adds no synchronization of its own), while `Mutex`/`RwLock` (futex-based in `std`, with poisoning semantics on a panicking thread) or `parking_lot` (a different set of trade-offs, no poisoning) are what actually synchronize access to the shared value. `tokio::sync::Mutex` exists for exactly one reason — holding a lock across an `.await` — and a `std` guard held across an `.await` is a real, distinct bug this topic names precisely.
3. **Critical sections and external latency** (topic 20) — needs (2): once a lock's job is understood precisely, this topic names the mistake of putting something other than memory access behind it — I/O inside a critical section turns a lock meant to protect a few writes into something serializing an external call's entire latency across every thread waiting on it. Lock ordering is the fix for deadlock; lock convoys and priority inversion are named as the two failure modes ordering doesn't fix.
4. **Explicit ownership-crossing points** (topic 21) — needs (1) and (2): closes the chapter by naming the complete, closed set of places ownership is actually allowed to move across a thread boundary — `thread::spawn`, `thread::scope`, `tokio::spawn(async move { .. })`, and a channel send — and why `std::sync::mpsc` being crossbeam-based internally is a fact worth knowing, not a trivia footnote. Hands off to Chapter 7 (atomics and the memory-ordering model), where "safe to share" stops being enough and the chapter asks what's actually guaranteed about *when* one thread's write becomes visible to another's read.

## A.3 The running example: a shared job counter touched by a worker pool

None of Chapters 1–5's running examples need a worker pool, so this chapter introduces its own, self-contained per topic (no cross-topic imports): a small **job counter** a fixed-size pool of worker threads increments, because it is the simplest possible shape that still needs every idea this chapter covers — ownership crossing into each worker, a value shared across all of them, and a lock (or its absence) actually synchronizing access to it.

- **Topic 18** builds the counter's cell three ways — `Cell<u64>` (Send, not Sync), `RefCell<u64>` (Send, not Sync, for the contrast with topic 13's single-threaded framing), and the counter wrapped so a `MutexGuard` is visibly held across a scope — and uses the compiler's own `Send`/`Sync` auto-trait inference (via a real failing `thread::spawn` call, asserting the exact `error[E0277]`) rather than asserting it from documentation alone.
- **Topic 19** takes the identical counter and builds the same shared-access shape three ways: a bare `Arc<u64>` (compiles, but nothing can mutate it — demonstrating `Arc` alone shares and nothing more), `Arc<Mutex<u64>>` (the synchronized version), and a `parking_lot::Mutex` version, contrasting their real, measured lock/unlock cost and poisoning behaviour (a panic while holding a `std::sync::Mutex` really does poison it; `parking_lot` really doesn't).
- **Topic 20** reuses topic 19's `Arc<Mutex<u64>>` counter but adds a deliberately slow "external call" inside the critical section (a real `std::thread::sleep`, not a comment claiming it would be slow) to measure the real cost of serializing that latency across several threads, then fixes it by moving the slow call outside the lock, and separately builds a real two-lock deadlock via lock ordering violated on purpose, fixed by a consistent lock order.
- **Topic 21** reuses the same counter one last time, built via each of the four legal ownership-crossing points in turn (`thread::spawn`, `thread::scope`, a `tokio::spawn(async move { .. })`, and an `mpsc` channel send), to show the same value crossing a thread boundary through every legal path and contrast it with an illegal one that the borrow checker actually rejects.

## A.4 Terms and notation (continuing Chapters 1–5's vocabulary)

Everything in Chapters 1–5 §A.4 still applies. This chapter adds:

- **`Send`**: a marker trait meaning a value of this type may have its ownership moved to another thread.
- **`Sync`**: a marker trait meaning `&T` is itself `Send` — i.e., a shared reference to this type may safely be touched from more than one thread at once.
- **Auto-trait**: a trait (like `Send`/`Sync`) the compiler derives automatically from a type's fields, rather than one implemented by hand in the ordinary case; a type is `Send`/`Sync` exactly when all of its fields are, unless an explicit (unsafe) impl says otherwise.
- **`PhantomData`**: a zero-sized marker field used, among other roles, to opt a generic type out of an auto-trait it would otherwise get, by carrying a type parameter's auto-trait properties without actually storing a value of it.
- **`unsafe impl Send`**: a hand-written, unchecked claim that a type is safe to move across threads; a real proof obligation on the programmer, since the compiler cannot verify it.
- **`MutexGuard`**: the RAII guard `Mutex::lock()` returns; it is `!Send` by design, so a lock acquired on one thread can't be handed to, and released from, a different one.
- **`Arc` (revisited)**: gives shared ownership only; it adds no synchronization of its own, and a plain `Arc<T>` without an interior-mutability or locking type inside still can't be mutated through.
- **`Mutex`/`RwLock`**: the types that actually synchronize access; `std`'s are futex-based with poisoning (a panic while holding the lock poisons it for every later locker) and `parking_lot`'s are a different, non-poisoning implementation.
- **Critical section**: the span of code holding a lock; correctly sized, it protects only the shared memory access, never an I/O call or other externally-latent operation.
- **Lock ordering**: always acquiring a fixed, agreed order of locks across every thread, the standard fix for deadlock.
- **Lock convoy**: many threads serializing behind one lock far more often or for far longer than the work inside it would suggest, degrading throughput even without deadlock.
- **Priority inversion**: a low-priority thread holding a lock a high-priority thread needs, inverting their effective priorities for as long as the lock is held.
- **Ownership-crossing point**: one of the four places ownership is actually allowed to move to another thread: `thread::spawn`, `thread::scope`, `tokio::spawn(async move { .. })`, and a channel send.

Narration words (continuing the convention: no digits, no code-style names spoken): "safe to hand to another thread" for `Send`, "safe to touch from more than one thread through a shared reference" for `Sync`, "a trait the compiler works out for you" for an auto-trait, "a promise the compiler can't check for you" for `unsafe impl Send`, "the guard a lock hands back, tied to the thread that took it" for `MutexGuard`, "sharing ownership, nothing more" for a bare `Arc`, "the thing that actually keeps two threads from touching the same memory at once" for `Mutex`/`RwLock`, "a lock turned into a queue for something slow" for a critical section serializing external latency, "always taking locks in the same order" for lock ordering, "the only doors ownership is allowed to walk through to another thread" for the ownership-crossing points.
