This topic follows "Single ownership and move semantics" and "RAII, drop order, and leak safety": you should know that every value has one owner, that a move deinitializes its source, and that a value's fields are dropped in declaration order after its own `Drop::drop`. The first two steps set up the chapter's buffer pipeline (`Buf`, `Pool`); the guard `PooledBuf` is built in steps 7 and 12. `Rc` and `ManuallyDrop::take` get a one-line gloss on the spot, and so does the `'p` lifetime on the guard, which says the guard cannot outlive its borrow of the pool; their Rust details are in Chapters 2 and 4. `Box` is from the first reel, and `E0499` is the Book's two-`&mut` error.

<section class="prereq basics" data-section="basics">

### <span class="n">00</span> Basics: the terms this reel uses

- **Partial move**: moving one field out of a struct, so the field has a new owner and the rest of the struct is still owned. The struct as a whole is then unusable (E0382). *Where you meet it:* `let payload = frame.payload;`.
- **E0509**: the error for moving a field out of a type that implements `Drop`. `drop` may read every field, so none may be missing. *Where you meet it:* destructuring the `PooledBuf` guard.
- **E0507**: the error for moving out of something behind a reference (`&`, `&mut`, a `Vec` index, an `Rc`). A borrower may not leave a hole. *Where you meet it:* moving `self.buf` out inside `drop`.
- **`take`, `replace`, `swap`**: idioms that move a value out through a `&mut` by putting a valid value back in the same step. `Option::take` leaves `None`; `mem::replace` leaves what you pass. *Where you meet it:* `self.buf.take()` in a guard's `drop`.
- **Option as a legal hole**: a field stored as `Option<T>` can say "already handed out". *Where you meet it:* the guard's `buf: Option<Buf>`.
- **Drop flag (per field)**: a run-time boolean recording whether a conditionally moved field still needs dropping. *Where you meet it:* a field moved on only one branch of an `if`.
- **Pattern as transfer point**: each binding in a `let`, `match` arm or `if let` decides what happens to its part: a plain name copies or moves, `ref` borrows, and `_` touches nothing. *Where you meet it:* `Some(_)` leaves the value usable, `Some(_b)` moves it.
- **Default binding mode**: when you match a reference with a non-reference pattern, Rust switches the mode from `move` to `ref` (or `ref mut`), so bindings borrow. *Where you meet it:* `match &frame { ... }` moves nothing.
- **Rust 2024 reservations**: in a pattern that is already borrowing implicitly, `mut` on a binding, explicit `ref`/`ref mut`, and `&`/`&mut` patterns are errors. *Where you meet it:* `cargo fix --edition` rewriting a pattern.

</section>

### <span class="n">01</span> Single ownership and move semantics

One owner per value; a move copies the handle and deinitializes the source; `Copy` against `Clone`; reading E0382. This topic asks what happens when only *part* of a value moves.

<p class="check"><b>Check yourself:</b> after <code>let t = s;</code> for a <code>String</code>, can you use <code>s</code>? (Answer: no. The place <code>s</code> is moved-from, deinitialized until it is assigned again.)</p>

### <span class="n">02</span> RAII, drop order, and leak safety

`Drop::drop` runs first, then the fields in declaration order; locals drop in reverse; drop flags track a conditional move at run time; leaking is safe. A type with `Drop` keeps its fields until it dies, which is where this topic starts.

<p class="check"><b>Check yourself:</b> what does <code>Drop::drop</code> receive, and why does that matter for the struct's fields? (Answer: <code>&amp;mut self</code>. The destructor may read every field, so every field must still be there.)</p>

### <span class="n">03</span> Patterns: <code>match</code>, <code>if let</code>, <code>let</code> destructuring

Destructuring a struct, a tuple or an enum with <code>let</code>, <code>match</code> and <code>if let</code>; binding a name, <code>_</code>, <code>..</code>. The reel teaches what each binding does with ownership; the syntax is the Book's.

### <span class="n">04</span> <code>Option</code>, <code>Result</code> and <code>?</code>

<code>Option&lt;T&gt;</code> is <code>Some(T)</code> or <code>None</code>; <code>?</code> returns an <code>Err</code> from the enclosing function at once. The guard keeps its buffer in an <code>Option</code>, and the error-path step uses <code>?</code>.

<p class="check"><b>Check yourself:</b> what does <code>?</code> do on an <code>Err</code>, and what happens to the function's locals? (Answer: it returns the error early; the locals are dropped on the way out.)</p>
